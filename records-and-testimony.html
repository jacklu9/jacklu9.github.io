What the camera cannot testify to

A record can show a change, a witness can explain it, and a caption can quietly turn one into the other.

Imagine a twelve-second clip from a basketball game. One player sits down just as the referee gestures toward the bench. A caption calls this an ejection. The player could have been sent off, substituted, or seated before a stoppage the clip does not show. Something happened on camera: a motion toward one part of the court and a player who sat down. The caption supplies a causal account. A replay at half speed can make the gesture clearer, but cannot supply the missing call.

Three versions to set side by side

The original clip. It gives the viewer an order of events. Before treating their order as the reason one followed another, someone has to identify the officiating signal or check what was said.

The same clip cropped tight on the player. It could make an expression more legible while hiding the other players’ responses. Better evidence about one detail can be worse evidence about the event.

The same clip with the arena audio restored. The audio could contain the announcement that resolves the question. Or it could contain only the crowd. Those additions could change the force of an interpretation without changing its evidential support.

Reporting what footage shows

Lutz et al. (2026) developed a visual grammar when studying how anti-immigrant rhetoric circulated on TikTok and X. Their team qualitatively examined about 1,500 pieces of media and used computational analysis on about 50,000. The coding recorded subjects, actions, and backgrounds as well as what the material depicted or claimed. Finding related imagery helped the analysts inspect reuse and editing. It did not make visually related posts equivalent: a new crop, commentary, or purpose can make a recurring image part of a different argument.

A laboratory recording has a related difficulty. In Schmidt et al.’s (2018) study of wearable sensors, breathing helped distinguish the protocol’s stress task, baseline, and amusement. Participants spoke during the stress task. The sensor may have been tracking a feature of the situation that helped identify which part of the protocol the participant was in. Nothing about the signal alone says how to distinguish speaking from the emotion the researchers were interested in. A matching trace can be excellent evidence of one thing and less direct evidence of another. That is also why a model’s ability to match an output to a label is different from an explanation of what drove the match.

A good summary of the basketball clip can say what is visible, attribute the explanation to the caption, and identify an independent check, such as the game report. Those are three separate contributions. What the first viewer hears in the soundtrack may remain important: people use it to decide what kind of scene they are watching. It just must not carry the weight of the report.
