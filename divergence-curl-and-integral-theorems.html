Electromagnetic Theory · Chapter 1

Divergence, curl, and the integral theorems

Two local operators, two orientations, and a field for which both laws can be checked by hand.

Take a slice through a field and mark a point. One question is whether the field carries more out of a small box around that point than it carries in. Another question is whether it favors motion around the edge of a small loop. It can do either, both, or neither. The answer depends on how the neighboring vectors differ, not on the length of the arrow at the marked point.

This lesson uses outward normals on closed surfaces and the right-hand rule on open surfaces: viewed from the tip of the chosen normal, the positive boundary direction runs anticlockwise. The fields below are smooth wherever the calculations are made. For the geometry of the surface element, see vectors, gradients, and flux.

Divergence: compare opposing faces

Center a small box at P=(x,y,z)P=(x,y,z) with side lengths Δx,Δy,Δz\Delta x,\Delta y,\Delta z. The face on its positive xx side counts FxF_x as outward. On the negative side, the normal points the other way and the contribution has the opposite sign. Using the face centers for the leading term, the net flux through this pair is

[Fx(x+Δx/2,y,z)−Fx(x−Δx/2,y,z)]ΔyΔz.\left[F_x(x+\Delta x/2,y,z)-F_x(x-\Delta x/2,y,z)\right]\Delta y\Delta z.

Divide by the box volume and shrink the box. The quotient for the pair tends to ∂xFx\partial_xF_x. The other two pairs give ∂yFy\partial_yF_y and ∂zFz\partial_zF_z. Their sum is the local outward flux per volume:

∇⋅F=∂xFx+∂yFy+∂zFz.\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=\partial_x F_x+\partial_y F_y+\partial_z F_z.

Equal inflow and outflow give zero net flux. A uniform nonzero field is therefore compatible with zero divergence. In electrostatics, flux of electric displacement D\mathbf D through a closed surface has units of charge; its divergence has units of charge per volume. The algebraic sign of the divergence is local and does not by itself tell which way any individual arrow points.

Curl: compare opposing edges

Use an xyxy rectangle oriented with normal +z^+\hat{\mathbf z}, so the bottom edge is traversed toward +x+x and the right edge toward +y+y. At leading order, the bottom and top contributions are Fx(bottom)ΔxF_x(\text{bottom})\Delta x and −Fx(top)Δx-F_x(\text{top})\Delta x. Their sum, divided by area, tends to −∂yFx-\partial_yF_x. The right and left edges similarly give +∂xFy+\partial_xF_y. The signed circulation per area for this orientation is therefore

(∇×F)z=∂xFy−∂yFx.(\nabla\times\mathbf F)_z=\partial_xF_y-\partial_yF_x.

The other components come from loops normal to +x+x and +y+y. The vector curl records how the circulation density changes with the chosen viewing direction. A field pointing around a large circle can have zero curl in parts of the region; the definition tests a shrinking loop, not a single distant curve.

Why the boundary sums work

Subdivide a volume into small cells. An internal face belongs to two adjacent cells, so it enters their outward fluxes once with each sign. The shared contributions cancel and only the external surface remains. Adding the local divergence contributions yields Gauss’s divergence theorem:

∫V(∇⋅F) dV=∮∂VF⋅dS.\int_V (\nabla\cdot\mathbf F)\,dV=\oint_{\partial V}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf S.

Subdivide an oriented surface into small patches. The common edge of neighboring patches is traversed in opposite directions and cancels from the sum of patch circulations. With the orientation inherited by the outer loop, this gives Stokes’s theorem:

∫S(∇×F)⋅dS=∮∂SF⋅dℓ.\int_S(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot d\mathbf S=\oint_{\partial S}\mathbf F\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell.

The cancellation argument explains the roles of the boundaries. The surface on the right of Gauss’s theorem is closed. The curve on the right of Stokes’s theorem is closed, but the spanning surface can be open. The field must be sufficiently smooth on the entire region used in the theorem. If a field is undefined at a point inside the proposed region, a calculation that omits that point requires a different region and its additional boundary.

Try a source and a swirler

In the plane, the field (x,y)(x,y) points radially out from the origin. Through a circle centered there, it has a positive normal component at every point, but no tangent component. The field (−y,x)(-y,x) instead points along the anticlockwise tangent. Its normal component is zero. Both fields have the same magnitude at the same distance from the origin.

For a circle of radius RR, that common magnitude on the boundary is RR. The circle has length 2πR2\pi R. Predict which integral, outward flux across the planar curve or circulation along it, gives R(2πR)=2πR2R(2\pi R)=2\pi R^2 in each case. Then double the radius and see whether the nonzero value doubles or quadruples.

Here the outward flux across a curve is the two-dimensional divergence theorem: length replaces surface area, and area replaces volume. The circulation is also a three-dimensional Stokes calculation if the planar field is extended with zero third component and the disk is given upward normal. The two views are compatible because each uses the orientation appropriate to its own integral.

One field, two checks

Take F=(2x−y, x+3y, −z)\mathbf F=(2x-y,\,x+3y,\,-z). Its divergence is 2+3−1=42+3-1=4, while its curl is (0,0,1−(−1))=(0,0,2)(0,0,1-(-1))=(0,0,2). The unit cube 0≤x,y,z≤10\leq x,y,z\leq1 has volume 11, so Gauss’s theorem predicts net outward flux 44. Check it by pairing faces:

  • xx faces: the outward components at x=1x=1 and x=0x=0 sum to (2−y)+y=2(2-y)+y=2 at each paired location.
  • yy faces: the corresponding sum is (x+3)−x=3(x+3)-x=3.
  • zz faces: the sum is −1+0=−1-1+0=-1. Each pair is integrated over a unit square, so the total is 2+3−1=42+3-1=4.

For Stokes’s theorem, use the square z=0z=0, 0≤x,y≤10\leq x,y\leq1, with upward normal. The curl flux is 22 times the unit area. Direct integration starting from the bottom edge gives four contributions in order:

∫012x dx+∫01(1+3y) dy+∫10(2x−1) dx+∫103y dy=1+52+0−32=2.\int_0^1 2x\,dx+\int_0^1(1+3y)\,dy+\int_1^0(2x-1)\,dx+\int_1^0 3y\,dy=1+\tfrac52+0-\tfrac32=2.

The same field gives different numerical totals because the two operations ask different questions. Reversing the loop alone changes the circulation to −2-2. Stokes’s theorem then requires the normal to flip too. It does not change the outward flux through the cube.

In Maxwell’s equations, an enclosing surface naturally tests the sources of electric displacement, while an oriented loop tests the circulation of electric or magnetic fields. Keeping those two pictures separate makes it much easier to decide where a sign or an orientation enters a derivation.

Check your understanding

For G=(x−y,x+y,2z)\mathbf G=(x-y,x+y,2z), find the net outward flux through a cube of side 22 and the circulation around a radius-22 circle in the xyxy plane, anticlockwise as seen from above.

∇⋅G=1+1+2=4\nabla\cdot\mathbf G=1+1+2=4 and ∇×G=(0,0,2)\nabla\times\mathbf G=(0,0,2). The cube’s volume is 88, so its flux is 3232. The upward disk has area 4π4\pi, so the oriented circulation is 8π8\pi.

A uniform field H=(3,0,0)\mathbf H=(3,0,0) points through two opposite faces of a cube. What are its divergence and curl, and where does its direct flux calculation go to zero?

Both derivatives vanish. The +x+x face has positive outward flux and the −x-x face has the equal negative contribution. The four other faces contribute zero. Cancelling the two nonzero terms gives zero net flux, not zero field.
