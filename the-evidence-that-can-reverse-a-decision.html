The evidence that can reverse a decision

A polished answer can be a good document and a bad decision. What would a review have to put in front of the reader to tell the difference?

A group of management consultants had a spreadsheet, notes from interviews, and a brand recommendation to make. Read on its own, the spreadsheet made one option look attractive. The interviews supplied information that changed how its figures should be interpreted. In this experiment, about 85 percent of consultants without GPT-4 made the correct recommendation; the two groups with GPT-4 access scored about 71 and 60 percent. Access nevertheless improved the graded quality of the written responses to this case. In product-development tasks in the same study, the model helped people finish faster and obtain better quality ratings (Dell’Acqua et al., 2026).

The contrast moves the question away from whether models make good or bad consultants. A suitable next question is what the reviewer has to notice to change a specific recommendation. An assistant that makes each paragraph clearer cannot close the gap if the decision turns on a passage the reviewer never examines.

Put the exception where it can interrupt

Consider an illustrative workplace decision. A site inspection reports that every component passed except a seal that was never tested under load. A polished summary could lift the pass figures correctly and mention the seal in its final paragraph. The quality of the summary and the soundness of the decision to reopen are different things. The key word is unless: an institution that says it will reopen unless a critical seal has not been tested needs that exception surfaced before the order is signed. A reviewer might ask not for a longer explanation but for the result, the exception, and the source that could settle whether it applies.

A review test could supply two near-identical case packets: a work order, a table of completed checks, and an inspection note. In one packet the exception applies; in the other, a dated test clears it. Compare a recommendation-first interface with one that puts the relevant check beside the recommendation. Hold reading time and the available records constant. Record decisions in both directions. An interface that causes every reviewer to refuse to reopen would catch the unsafe case but also reject the cleared one. A design improves judgment only if people become better at distinguishing them.

The approach differs from merely adding more justification after the answer. Some explanations make a decision easier to inspect; others only make it more persuasive. How an explanation changes a verdict follows experiments that distinguish those effects.

The order of the review can also leave a useful trace. A reviewer can first record which criterion settles the decision, then mark the evidence that would change it, then record the final choice. That sequence makes it possible to distinguish a reader who never noticed the seal from a reader who saw it and judged the rule inapplicable. Both may sign the same order. The first suggests a problem of retrieval or display; the second calls for inspection of the rule and the reviewer’s reasoning. Even when an interface cannot resolve the disagreement, it can preserve enough of the comparison for a second reviewer to understand what remains open.

Test the next decision, too

In a high-school mathematics experiment, students with either of two kinds of GPT-4 help performed better on practice while they had access. The next exam removed that help. Students who had practiced with the general chat interface scored about 17 percent lower than the comparison group relative to its mean. The more structured tutor used information and hints supplied by teachers; the study did not detect the same decline for that group, or a clear advantage over the comparison group on the unaided exam (Bastani et al., 2025).

The consultant experiment tested a choice made during assistance; the school experiment moved the test to a later occasion without it. Together they make a practical distinction for institutional review tools. A team may only need reliable access to a particular source during a particular review, or it may want reviewers to recognize the same kind of exception the next time there is no system to flag it. Those aims need not compete, but they deserve different outcome measures. A useful handoff might say which evidence would reverse a recommendation and what to verify next; retaining that distinction is more valuable than remembering the recommendation after its circumstances change.
