The singer, the sensor, and the next sound

A singer at a piano swayed and heard a soft noise change. When she tried switching registers, an operation central to her practice, the noise gave her no clear indication that she had done so. The sound came from VoxBox, an instrument Reed and McPherson (2023) built to let singers hear changes in the electrical activity measured at the neck. Instead of altering the sung note, the device changed the frequency balance of a separate noise. The singer could hear a movement that was incidental to the phrase, while a difference she wanted to hear remained elusive. It is a useful scene for thinking about what happens when a bodily signal becomes part of a sound.

The device had registered something real. The question was what it had registered well enough for this singer, at this moment, to use. There are three different answers to that question. The sound might make a pattern noticeable to someone hearing it for the first time. It might help a performer adjust an action on purpose. Or it might be part of the material of a piece, something the performer can work around or let surprise them. Those aims ask for different things from a sensor and from the person wearing it.

What a singer can hear

In the four-week VoxBox study, a second singer noticed the noise rising and falling as she drew breath for a long phrase. Once she began to sing, the noise receded from her attention. Reed and McPherson’s design makes this difference easier to understand. The addition is neither a second copy of the voice nor a rating of how well the singer has used it. It occupies the same time as the work. During an inhalation there is room to hear the variation in the noise; during the phrase the performer is already listening closely to the note. A display that is useful during preparation can be hard to attend to during performance, even when its electrical input and synthesis process have not changed.

Grace Leslie’s Vessels presents another relationship between performer and sound. Leslie (2021, 2023) describes using signals including brain activity, skin conductance, and heart activity to trigger or shape sampled material. Her program notes describe slower bodily impulses rendered as finger snaps, and faster ones as the sustained sound of a finger on the rim of a glass. Large gestures also brought muscle artifacts into the electronics. Leslie changed the way she played, developing the piece around small movements, slow breathing, and long tones. The mapping set conditions for a performance practice; a movement that upset the electronics had consequences for the shape of the piece.

That change is not equivalent to discovering that a particular sound means the performer felt calm. It is closer to discovering that a bow on a string has a range in which it behaves as the player wants. A musician may want to remain in that range for part of a piece and leave it deliberately elsewhere. A useful account of the work can describe which of those changes a performer can reproduce, where the performer leaves variation open, and how practice changes those decisions.

Who chooses the musical change?

In Tanaka et al.’s (2023) account of Lifting, one arm shapes the pitch of an oscillator and the other its volume. In a workshop, Tanaka replaced his previous armband with a new device on one arm, switched the devices, and then tried the new unit on both arms. He reported that he could perform confidently on the replacement and that the musical character carried over. For a dance piece, Écoute/Expansion, the group tried a different approach: rather than keeping every sensor dimension from the original setup, they reassigned two muscle channels and their sum to several synthesis and spatial controls. Composer Anne Sèdes found the connection between exertion, timbre, and space more direct with the revised arrangement. These are assessments by collaborators who knew the works, and they tell two different stories about what an electronic instrument is expected to preserve. Sometimes it is a way of playing. Sometimes it is something the audience is meant to hear.

There is another possibility: let the signal choose material, but leave the interpretation to the performers. Eaton et al. (2015) did this with a vocalist, pianist, and one audience member in The Space Between Us. At the end of successive windows, software used estimates derived from the singer’s and audience member’s EEG to help sequence passages of a score the musicians then played. The score shifted whose recording counted between movements. Moving the singer’s hand to a different position would not have allowed them to ask for a specific passage in the way the performer of Lifting can deliberately alter pitch. The listener with the EEG cap took part without having to issue a musical command. To ask whether each person had a say requires being clear about what kind of say the piece offered.

When a sound stands as evidence

A different experiment puts the listener at the center. Loui et al. (2014) converted EEG excerpts into sound and tested whether listeners could discriminate recordings before and after hearing labeled examples. Mean classification accuracy rose from 53.1% to 63.4% on balanced excerpts. It was a narrowly specified act of listening: learn the audible distinction offered by this mapping, then look for it in different ten-second excerpts. The result gives a way to check whether listeners actually hear something useful rather than assuming that any audibly changing output makes its source obvious.

Contrast that with the WESAD experiment. Schmidt et al. (2018) recorded 15 participants across baseline, amusement, and a socially stressful task; a model using chest signals classified the study conditions with about 80% accuracy on held-out participants. Breathing was its strongest single chest signal. The researchers noted a feature of the experiment that matters for interpreting that result: participants spoke in the stress task. Breathing patterns could help separate the tasks because one involved speaking. This does not make the signal spurious. It makes the label precise. The model was scored against the experimental conditions, not against a new person’s report about the feeling behind a particular breath.

The pianist listening for neck activity, the audience member contributing to a live score, and the participant wearing a sensor during a speech task may all produce recordings that can be made audible. Their roles are not interchangeable. A test of an instrument might ask a player to make one audible change without triggering another. A test of a sonification might give a listener new examples and ask which pattern they hear. A claim about what someone felt calls for a comparison that can separate their experience from the activity they were doing. The same signal can serve any of those purposes. The mistake is asking it to do one job and presenting the result as though it had succeeded at another.
