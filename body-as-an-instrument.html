When the body is part of the instrument

A sensor can turn effort into a note, leave a choice to the score, or teach a singer to hear an unfamiliar part of a familiar gesture.

A dancer moving along a pole has to work against its surface. A dancer on the floor can make a visually similar arc with a different kind of exertion. In an electronic performance, the two movements might take the sound in different directions. Designing that instrument is less a matter of revealing the dancers’ intentions than deciding which features of their movement become available to the music.

The same gesture can have different properties

When Caramiaux et al. (2015) asked people to perform one gesture with different kinds of emphasis, many participants understood “more power” as extra pressure against a surface, or as tension or intensity in the air. Greater effort showed up in the electrical activity recorded at the forearm for five of six gestures in the authors’ follow-up comparisons. It was harder to distinguish in a movement that already involved holding a fist. Participants also tended to perform the more powerful versions more quickly. The movement did not have an independent control for each of the words used to describe it.

An electronic instrument designer can make use of that coupling or work to undo it. A system that uses one feature to raise the pitch and another to change the volume could give a player an elegant swell at the top of a phrase. If the player wants the reverse, the same coupling may resist it. A practical rehearsal asks for three actions: a louder note at the same pitch, a rise in pitch at the same volume, and a rise in both. The instrument that makes the third easy might give a player very little access to the first two.

Change the sensor, keep the piece?

When Tanaka et al. (2023) tried new muscle-sensing equipment for Lifting, the piece already organized the sound around two arms, one controlling oscillator pitch and the other overall volume. The performer tried the replacement device on each arm in turn and then on both. He judged that the music retained its character. Rebuilding the sensing for the dance work Écoute/Expansion looked less like a component swap. Its revised version used two muscle channels and their sum to control several aspects of sound and its position. Composer Anne Sèdes found the link between exertion and the shifting sound more direct in the revision. The musicians were trying to preserve something recognizable about each work, but what counted as recognizable was a different thing in the two cases.

The singer using Reed and McPherson’s VoxBox (2023) had another reason to pay attention to a signal. Instead of altering the sung line, the instrument fed a changing noise alongside it. One singer heard the noise respond while drawing breath and paid less attention to it after the phrase began. This is a problem of arrangement as much as sensing. Practice can make room for a detail that would crowd another sound out of a concert. A successful musical interface need not feed all its information into the part an audience hears.

A movement can give someone else time

Choreography also governs what an observer can infer before a gesture is finished. In a study by Dragan et al. (2015), eighteen people worked with a robot that reached for a cup while they selected the relevant ingredients. Movements designed to reveal the intended cup sooner shortened the study’s coordination interval by roughly one-third compared with movements designed to follow an expected path. The interval ended when a participant identified the cup or began to collect the ingredients. Total completion time did not reliably differ between those two movement conditions. The early part of the gesture gave a collaborator time to act even when it did not provide a faster way to finish the whole task.

That is a useful distinction for the dancer as well as the engineer. An audience might see effort accumulate before hearing the sound change; a collaborator might hear the change and only afterward understand what the dancer did. The arrangement can make either sequence the point of an encounter. What it cannot do is make every expressive response as informative as a deliberate cue. An instrument test might therefore ask whether a second performer can come in on the intended beat after a gesture, as well as whether the first performer can make the right sound alone.

The score can have an independent part

Eaton et al. (2015) made a version of this independence audible in The Space Between Us. A vocalist and an audience member wore EEG caps. Signals contributed estimates that helped the software determine which passages from an existing score the musicians would play. The listener contributed to the performance without selecting a passage in the manner of a player deciding to begin a phrase. The work gives the participant a place in the score but leaves a degree of unpredictability that a musician trying to reproduce a specific note would normally resist.

“Control” names all of these relationships only at a very broad level. A player may be learning what one gesture will do, the composer may be trying to retain the shape of a piece with another sensor, and the listener may want to contribute without making a deliberate musical command. Those choices can be tested by asking who can make, inspect, or undo the next change. That question also applies to what it means for a person to express something through a tool. A device can sense more of the body without giving the wearer more of the decisions.
