A current reaches a gap
Picture two metal plates on a laboratory bench, facing one another with a small insulating gap
between them. Connect the plates to a voltage supply through wires and a resistor, then close the
switch. For a while, a meter in the wire registers a current.
Follow the wire with your eye. It reaches one plate. The other wire leaves the other plate. Between
them, the conducting path is broken.
If current is moving charge, what happens where the metal ends?
In this idealised device, no free charge travels across the insulating gap. Yet a small test charge
placed there can experience a force. A magnetic measurement around the charging wire also has
to ﬁt into the same physical account. It would be strange if our description worked perfectly in
the wire, then ceased to make sense a few millimetres away.

Figure 1.1. The device that will return throughout the chapter, shown in cross-section. The metal plates

Wait until the charging current has died away. Must the force in the gap vanish too? Now change
the experiment: drive the device repeatedly with a sinusoidal voltage. A reading rises, falls, and
changes sign. A graph can show that oscillation, but which part of the picture represents a direction
in space, and which part represents the passage of time?
Finally, replace the gap by two layers of material. The plates are still there, and the applied voltage
is still a single number. But the electric ﬁeld in one layer may diﬀer from the ﬁeld in the other.
How can one physical ﬁeld change at an interface, and which parts of it must join smoothly? If
more charge arrives at that interface than leaves it, where does the diﬀerence go?
These questions belong to the same small device. Answering them will take us from a reading at
one point to a description of an entire region. We will need to compare neighbouring arrows, look
through surfaces, and walk around loops. The mathematical language will grow out of those tasks.
Keep the gap in mind while we build that language. We will return to the charging wire when
the magnetic law seems to give two diﬀerent answers, and to the two layers when we can ﬁnally
calculate how their ﬁelds settle. By then, the apparent break in the circuit will have become a
place where several pieces of the theory meet.

Following the ﬁeld into space
A meter in one wire gives us one reading. To understand the gap, we need a richer description:
what would a probe measure at each position around the apparatus? Two points can have diﬀerent
ﬁeld strengths and directions, and either reading can change with time. The object we are trying
to understand is this whole map of local values.
A useful ﬁrst picture is an invisible array of small measuring probes ﬁlling a region. Each probe
reports the ﬁeld at its own position. We draw an arrow at that position to represent the result.
Its direction is the ﬁeld direction; its length, using a stated scale, represents ﬁeld magnitude. The
arrows form a map. They are not miniature particles, and the ﬁeld does not consist only of the
few arrows that we happen to draw.
In the notation E(r, t), E is the electric ﬁeld, r speciﬁes a position, and t speciﬁes a time. The
parentheses mean “evaluate the ﬁeld at this place and time”, not multiplication. In Cartesian
coordinates, r = (x, y, z), so the same information can be written E(x, y, z, t). A single ﬁeld value
is a vector; the entire ﬁeld is the rule assigning a vector to every position and time.

Figure 1.2. A ﬁeld lives throughout a volume. The dot marks a location P, and the coloured arrow is the
ﬁeld value there. The grey position vector tells us where to measure; it is a diﬀerent vector from the ﬁeld

There are two independent ways to explore this map. A spatial comparison holds time ﬁxed and
compares nearby probes.
A time comparison holds one probe ﬁxed and observes its changing
reading. A wave involves an organised pattern of both: the value at a point changes in time, while
the same phase of the pattern appears at successive positions. The detailed wave solutions come
later; ﬁrst we need a language for these comparisons.
A map creates questions of its own. An arrow tells us what happens at one point, but cannot
by itself tell us whether neighbouring arrows point outwards, pass straight through a region, or
favour a direction around a loop. Even the word “change” needs care: walking to a new position
and waiting at the same position are diﬀerent experiments. We will give each comparison a precise
meaning before asking what physical law it obeys.

How to read the pictures
Perspective view: all three coordinate directions represent physical space. An object drawn
as a parallelogram may represent a ﬂat plane viewed obliquely.
Plan view or cross-section: a two-dimensional picture shows a speciﬁed slice of a three-
dimensional ﬁeld. A circular line on paper may be one member of a family of circles around
a wire, not the entire magnetic ﬁeld.
Graph: an axis labelled ﬁeld value or displacement plots a quantity; it is not automatically
another direction of physical space. A sinusoidal graph of Ey against x is not the trajectory
of light.
An arrow’s placement, direction, and length have diﬀerent jobs. Dashed lines indicate hidden
edges, construction lines, or a second snapshot as stated in the caption. Neither the density
nor the length of schematic arrows should be used as numerical data unless a scale is given.
A small dictionary for reading the mathematics.
A bold letter, such as F , denotes a
vector; an ordinary letter, such as f, usually denotes a scalar. The general symbol F may stand
for any vector ﬁeld, whereas in a force law it denotes force: the local deﬁnition decides. A hat, as
in ˆn, denotes a unit vector, whose magnitude is one and whose purpose is to specify direction. A
subscript often names a component: Fx is the component along x, and Fn the component along a
chosen normal.
A surface normal is a direction perpendicular to a surface at a point; a tangent lies within the
local surface plane. The normal is not a second ﬁeld: it describes the geometry through which we
examine a ﬁeld. A volume V is a three-dimensional region, its boundary ∂V is a closed surface, a
surface S is a two-dimensional set, and its boundary ∂S is a curve when that surface has an edge.
The symbol ∂in ∂V means “boundary of”; the same printed symbol in ∂f/∂x denotes a partial
derivative. Context distinguishes the two.
The operator ∇, read “del” or “nabla”, packages spatial derivatives. A dot between vectors means
a dot product; a cross means a cross product. A dot above a time-dependent quantity, such as ˙q,
means its time derivative. A tilde, such as eE, will later denote a complex amplitude, or phasor.
Each operation will be built from its geometric meaning before it is used to derive a law.
With the map in view, the ﬁrst task is modest: learn to read one arrow, then compare it with its
neighbours. The later ﬁeld laws will use exactly those comparisons to explain the device we left
on the bench.
