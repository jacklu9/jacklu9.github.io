1 Fundamentals

A current reaches a gap

Picture two metal plates on a laboratory bench, facing one another with a small insulating gap between them. Connect the plates to a voltage supply through wires and a resistor, then close the switch. For a while, a meter in the wire registers a current.

Follow the wire with your eye. It reaches one plate. The other wire leaves the other plate. Between them, the conducting path is broken.

If current is moving charge, what happens where the metal ends?

In this idealised device, no free charge travels across the insulating gap. Yet a small test charge placed there can experience a force. A magnetic measurement around the charging wire also has to fit into the same physical account. It would be strange if our description worked perfectly in the wire, then ceased to make sense a few millimetres away.

Wait until the charging current has died away. Must the force in the gap vanish too? Now change the experiment: drive the device repeatedly with a sinusoidal voltage. A reading rises, falls, and changes sign. A graph can show that oscillation, but which part of the picture represents a direction in space, and which part represents the passage of time?

Finally, replace the gap by two layers of material. The plates are still there, and the applied voltage is still a single number. But the electric field in one layer may differ from the field in the other. How can one physical field change at an interface, and which parts of it must join smoothly? If more charge arrives at that interface than leaves it, where does the difference go?

These questions belong to the same small device. Answering them will take us from a reading at one point to a description of an entire region. We will need to compare neighbouring arrows, look through surfaces, and walk around loops. The mathematical language will grow out of those tasks.

Keep the gap in mind while we build that language. We will return to the charging wire when the magnetic law seems to give two different answers, and to the two layers when we can finally calculate how their fields settle. By then, the apparent break in the circuit will have become a place where several pieces of the theory meet.

Following the field into space

A meter in one wire gives us one reading. To understand the gap, we need a richer description: what would a probe measure at each position around the apparatus? Two points can have different field strengths and directions, and either reading can change with time. The object we are trying to understand is this whole map of local values.

A useful first picture is an invisible array of small measuring probes filling a region. Each probe reports the field at its own position. We draw an arrow at that position to represent the result. Its direction is the field direction; its length, using a stated scale, represents field magnitude. The arrows form a map. They are not miniature particles, and the field does not consist only of the few arrows that we happen to draw.

In the notation E(r, t), E is the electric field, r specifies a position, and t specifies a time. The parentheses mean “evaluate the field at this place and time”, not multiplication. In Cartesian coordinates, r = (x, y, z), so the same information can be written E(x, y, z, t). A single field value is a vector; the entire field is the rule assigning a vector to every position and time.

There are two independent ways to explore this map. A spatial comparison holds time fixed and compares nearby probes. A time comparison holds one probe fixed and observes its changing reading. A wave involves an organised pattern of both: the value at a point changes in time, while the same phase of the pattern appears at successive positions. The detailed wave solutions come later; first we need a language for these comparisons.

A map creates questions of its own. An arrow tells us what happens at one point, but cannot by itself tell us whether neighbouring arrows point outwards, pass straight through a region, or favour a direction around a loop. Even the word “change” needs care: walking to a new position and waiting at the same position are different experiments. We will give each comparison a precise meaning before asking what physical law it obeys.

How to read the pictures

Perspective view: all three coordinate directions represent physical space. An object drawn as a parallelogram may represent a flat plane viewed obliquely. Plan view or cross-section: a two-dimensional picture shows a specified slice of a three-dimensional field. A circular line on paper may be one member of a family of circles around a wire, not the entire magnetic field. Graph: an axis labelled field value or displacement plots a quantity; it is not automatically another direction of physical space. A sinusoidal graph of Ey against x is not the trajectory of light. An arrow’s placement, direction, and length have different jobs. Dashed lines indicate hidden edges, construction lines, or a second snapshot as stated in the caption. Neither the density nor the length of schematic arrows should be used as numerical data unless a scale is given.

A small dictionary for reading the mathematics. A bold letter, such as F , denotes a vector; an ordinary letter, such as f, usually denotes a scalar. The general symbol F may stand for any vector field, whereas in a force law it denotes force: the local definition decides. A hat, as , denotes a unit vector, whose magnitude is one and whose purpose is to specify direction. A subscript often names a component: Fx is the component along x, and Fn the component along a chosen normal.

A surface normal is a direction perpendicular to a surface at a point; a tangent lies within the local surface plane. The normal is not a second field: it describes the geometry through which we examine a field. A volume V is a three-dimensional region, its boundary ∂V is a closed surface, a surface S is a two-dimensional set, and its boundary ∂S is a curve when that surface has an edge. The symbol ∂ in ∂V means “boundary of”; the same printed symbol in ∂f/∂x denotes a partial derivative. Context distinguishes the two.

The operator ∇, read “del” or “nabla”, packages spatial derivatives. A dot between vectors means a dot product; a cross means a cross product. A dot above a time-dependent quantity, such as ˙q, means its time derivative. A tilde, such as , will later denote a complex amplitude, or phasor.

Each operation will be built from its geometric meaning before it is used to derive a law.

With the map in view, the first task is modest: learn to read one arrow, then compare it with its neighbours. The later field laws will use exactly those comparisons to explain the device we left on the bench.
