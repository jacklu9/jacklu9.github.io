Fill the same states in the three silicon pieces

Choose a particular conduction-band state in each piece. In the unchanged-host approximation it has the same energy and the same spin and wavevector labels in all three. Yet it should be occupied more often in the phosphorus piece than in the boron piece. Choosing a valence state reverses the useful question: how often is its electron missing? The boron piece needs the larger vacant fraction. One consistent filling rule must produce both differences.

The metal sphere gave an especially simple rule at zero temperature: fill the lowest states up to a boundary. At finite temperature, the boundary becomes a gradual transition. Lower-energy states are more often occupied, but some higher states are occupied too. We still use the symbol EF

for the energy parameter controlling the filling. It will no longer mean the highest energy of any occupied electron.

The relevant comparison is between a state’s energy difference E − EF and the thermal energy kBT. Here T is absolute temperature in kelvin and kB is Boltzmann’s constant, which converts temperature to energy:

At 300 K, kBT ≃ 0.02585 eV; our numerical exercises use the rounded value 0.026 eV = 26 meV. In the plot, the vertical value is f, the probability that a complete electron state is occupied. The warmer curve changes more gradually as energy crosses the same EF .

The state energy E and the filling parameter EF perform different jobs. Increasing E at fixed EF makes this state harder to occupy. Increasing EF at fixed E makes the same state easier to occupy. We can therefore increase the conduction population of the phosphorus piece by raising its filling level without redrawing its host band.

What determines that filling level? The curve still contains an unknown EF . Its physical meaning is the equilibrium electronic chemical potential: in introductory terms, the change in free energy associated with adding an electron to a large system under specified thermal conditions. Free energy incorporates the energetic and statistical effects relevant at a fixed temperature. It is this electron-addition balance, rather than the average kinetic energy of existing electrons, that sets their preference for occupying the available states.

In each silicon piece, we must choose the value of EF that gives carrier and impurity populations consistent with the charge balance. The phosphorus piece requires more mobile electrons to balance

its ionised donors, so its equilibrium EF generally moves toward Ec. The boron piece requires more holes to balance its ionised acceptors, so its EF moves toward Ev. The distribution equation supplies populations for a proposed level; neutrality selects a self-consistent level for the sample.

Use the same rule to follow the holes In the vacancy picture, a valence state was either occupied or empty. If its electron occupancy is f(E), its vacancy probability is 1 − f(E). Thus raising EF increases electron occupancy at both band edges: it creates more conduction electrons while leaving fewer valence vacancies. Lowering EF gives the opposite changes needed for the boron piece. Both populations follow one distribution.

The argument E here remains the electron energy of the valence state. The positive hole excitation energy εh = Ev − E runs downward on that diagram. When we later integrate using εh, the argument of the filling function must change consistently with it.

Now compare a semiconductor with a metal

We can now return to the opening picture of a filled valence band and an unfilled conduction band. The coloured blocks in the following sketch show allowed energy ranges, and the dashed line marks EF . Colour alone does not say that every state in a block is occupied. Reading the block together with the filling function tells us which electrons and vacancies can respond to a disturbance.

In the metal sketch, two allowed bands overlap and electronic states occur near the filling level. A single partially filled band is another metallic possibility; overlap is not required in every metal. Nearby available states let the occupation change when a weak field perturbs the electrons. In the ideal full-band limit, current contributions from the complete set of states cancel and there are no vacant states within that band for the ordinary weak-field response. An empty band contributes no carriers. This explains why merely drawing an allowed band did not establish a conducting population.

The semiconductor and insulator sketches instead have a gap between the host bands. Under ordinary comparable conditions, an insulator typically has a larger gap and a much smaller mobile population. A semiconductor’s population can change strongly with temperature and doping, as our silicon comparison has shown. There is no universal numerical gap cutoff in these schematic pictures that turns one label into the other. Filling, available states and the physical conditions must be considered together.

The three silicon pieces belong on the semiconductor side of this comparison. They can have nearly the same allowed-state curves but different filling levels. We are ready to multiply the two pieces of information and calculate the populations that were invisible in the band curve alone.

The infinite limits belong to the effective parabolic-band model. Real bands have a finite range and cease to be parabolic far from their edges.
