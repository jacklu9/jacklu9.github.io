Fill the same states in the three silicon pieces
Choose a particular conduction-band state in each piece. In the unchanged-host approximation
it has the same energy and the same spin and wavevector labels in all three. Yet it should be
occupied more often in the phosphorus piece than in the boron piece. Choosing a valence state
reverses the useful question: how often is its electron missing? The boron piece needs the larger
vacant fraction. One consistent filling rule must produce both differences.
The metal sphere gave an especially simple rule at zero temperature: fill the lowest states up to a
boundary. At finite temperature, the boundary becomes a gradual transition. Lower-energy states
are more often occupied, but some higher states are occupied too. We still use the symbol EF

The state energy E and the filling parameter EF perform different jobs. Increasing E at fixed

occupy. We can therefore increase the conduction population of the phosphorus piece by raising
its filling level without redrawing its host band.
What determines that filling level?
The curve still contains an unknown EF . Its physical
meaning is the equilibrium electronic chemical potential: in introductory terms, the change in
free energy associated with adding an electron to a large system under specified thermal conditions.
Free energy incorporates the energetic and statistical effects relevant at a fixed temperature. It
is this electron-addition balance, rather than the average kinetic energy of existing electrons, that
sets their preference for occupying the available states.

Use the same rule to follow the holes
In the vacancy picture, a valence state was either
occupied or empty. If its electron occupancy is f(E), its vacancy probability is 1 −f(E). Thus
raising EF increases electron occupancy at both band edges: it creates more conduction electrons
while leaving fewer valence vacancies. Lowering EF gives the opposite changes needed for the
boron piece. Both populations follow one distribution.
The argument E here remains the electron energy of the valence state. The positive hole excitation
energy εh = Ev −E runs downward on that diagram. When we later integrate using εh, the
argument of the filling function must change consistently with it.

In the metal sketch, two allowed bands overlap and electronic states occur near the filling level. A
single partially filled band is another metallic possibility; overlap is not required in every metal.
Nearby available states let the occupation change when a weak field perturbs the electrons. In the
ideal full-band limit, current contributions from the complete set of states cancel and there are no
vacant states within that band for the ordinary weak-field response. An empty band contributes
no carriers. This explains why merely drawing an allowed band did not establish a conducting
population.
The semiconductor and insulator sketches instead have a gap between the host bands. Under
ordinary comparable conditions, an insulator typically has a larger gap and a much smaller mobile
population. A semiconductor’s population can change strongly with temperature and doping, as
our silicon comparison has shown. There is no universal numerical gap cutoff in these schematic
pictures that turns one label into the other. Filling, available states and the physical conditions
must be considered together.
The three silicon pieces belong on the semiconductor side of this comparison. They can have nearly
the same allowed-state curves but different filling levels. We are ready to multiply the two pieces
of information and calculate the populations that were invisible in the band curve alone.
