Does the explanation change the verdict?

Advice can make it easier to agree or easier to check. These are different effects.

A participant in a study sees a maze and a suggested exit. In one version, the proposed route is drawn over the maze. In the other, it is presented as a list of turns. Both representations tell a participant how the assistant proposes to get out. But the drawing lets the participant see a turn in relation to the wall it is supposed to pass. With the list, the participant needs to construct that relation before they can inspect it. On the medium and hard mazes in Vasconcelos et al. (2023), the drawn route led to fewer accepted wrong exits than the written directions.

There is a difference between making a proposal easy to follow and making the evidence for it available for inspection. A route can do both. Drawing it does not, by itself, make the destination correct. It makes the work of checking it more local. The problem has much in common with a diagram that puts a relation within reach: a useful representation can reduce search in a way that still leaves the reader something to decide.

Agreement has two directions

Participants in Bansal et al. (2021) classified sentiment in book or beer reviews or answered logical-reasoning questions. On the items chosen for the experiment, the model and unassisted participants had comparable accuracy. People given the model's answer and confidence did better than either alone. Adding the tested explanations did not produce a significant accuracy gain over answer and confidence: participants became more willing to accept the model when it was right and when it was wrong. The explanations changed the interaction even though the final score showed no improvement. They helped one kind of correction and worsened another kind of error at the same time.

If participants start with their own answer, a review can record four outcomes. The tool catches an error the person would otherwise have made; the person rejects an error the tool made; a correct answer changes to an incorrect one; or the initial decision survives. The same agreement rate can describe any mixture of these, just as two identical booking records can hide different opportunities to choose. A useful explanation should make good revisions easier. In a group discussion, a revision may depend on whether one person can bring a fact that others have not seen. Agreement does not tell an evaluator whether the explanation did so.

Where the check can happen

Buçinca et al. (2021) changed when advice was available. In a study with 199 participants, people chose which ingredient to remove and which replacement would reduce carbohydrates while preserving flavor. Among examples with the simulated system's wrong choice of ingredient to remove, participants accepted that part of the recommendation on an estimated 64% of trials under immediate-explanation designs, versus 48% under pooled delayed or on-demand designs. The difference was clearer for identifying the ingredient than for agreeing with the whole two-part recommendation. An initial independent judgment can make an alternative salient. In learning settings, a related test is whether the learner still has a next move after receiving help. It can also add work for someone who has no plausible alternative to consider. Which step benefits is an empirical question.

Asking a question first is not a universal remedy. In Zheng et al. (2026), half the participants answered two preference questions before receiving recommendations for everyday decisions. The questions were used to tailor prepared recommendations. Participants retained the entire recommendation on 52% of trials with a violation after questions, compared with 39% after a direct reply. The design changed both the questions and their use in the response, so the percentages do not isolate a pure effect of asking. It did show that a recommendation could reflect a discussed installation preference while missing a deadline, and that discussion did not make every remaining constraint equally visible.

Before adding an explanation, an editor or designer can ask which check should become easier. For a proposed route, make the physical turns inspectable. For a purchase, put delivery alongside the date it is needed. For a reviewer, show where the relevant qualification enters the evidence. If the only observable effect is assent, measure what the reader judged about the underlying claim as well as what they decided to do.
