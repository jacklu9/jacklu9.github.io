Revision of basic electromagnetics
We can now distinguish an outward imbalance from a circulation, and a pointwise measurement
from a sum over a boundary. Geometry has supplied the questions. Physics must tell us which
answers charges, currents, and materials permit. We return to the apparatus, beginning with what
a probe actually measures.
1.2.1
Fields, sources, and material relations
A ﬁeld is something we can test at a position.

material interfaces.
A material responds to the applied ﬁelds.
In a dielectric, positive and negative charges
can shift slightly in opposite directions without transporting free charge all the way through the
sample. A separated positive and negative charge pair is an electric dipole. For charges −q and
+q, with q > 0, deﬁne the separation vector d from the negative charge to the positive charge.
The electric dipole moment is p = qd, in C·m: its magnitude records both the amount of charge
and how far the charges are separated. The polarisation P is the summed electric dipole moment
per unit volume, with units C/m2. It describes the response after averaging over many atoms or
molecules.

We distinguish free charge, treated as a speciﬁed or transported source, from bound charge asso-
ciated with this material response. “Free” does not mean that a charge must be moving: a charge
deposited on an insulating surface can be a free surface charge. Nor does “bound” mean that its
electric ﬁeld is unimportant. Both contribute to the physical E. The distinction lets us describe
the material response collectively, rather than tracking every microscopic charge.
The electric displacement D groups the polarisation with the vacuum contribution:
D = ε0E + P .
(1.14)
Here ε0 is the vacuum permittivity. The units of both terms are C/m2, the units of D. In the next
subsection, this deﬁnition will turn a law involving all charges into a law whose explicit source is
free charge alone.

A·m2 and points along the loop’s axis. This gives a useful macroscopic representation of microscopic
magnetic dipoles. Their summed moment per unit volume is magnetisation M, with units A/m.

Constitutive relations specify the material model.
For a linear, isotropic, instantaneous
material response, and an ohmic conduction model, the relations simplify to
D = εE,
B = µH,
Jcond = σE.
(1.16)
“Linear” means proportional response; “isotropic” means the same response in every direction.
“Instantaneous” means that no time delay or memory is included in these time-domain relations.
The permittivity ε and permeability µ account for the electric and magnetic material responses;
conductivity σ measures the conduction-current response. Their units are F/m, H/m, and S/m.
It is convenient to write ε = ε0εr and µ = µ0µr, where the relative parameters are dimensionless.
Homogeneity is a separate assumption: a homogeneous region has material parameters indepen-
dent of position. Only then may a parameter such as ε be moved through a spatial derivative.

1.2.2
Gauss’s law for the electric ﬁeld
Enclose charge with an imaginary surface.
Imagine drawing a closed surface around a
region of space. It can pass through vacuum or through matter; it need not be a conductor, a
physical membrane, or an actual boundary. It is a Gaussian surface: a geometrical surface chosen
to apply Gauss’s law. At each patch, choose the outward normal. The electric-displacement ﬂux
measures the signed normal component of D crossing all those patches.
Gauss’s electric law says that this net outward ﬂux equals the free charge enclosed:

count of material particles crossing the Gaussian surface: a static ﬁeld can have nonzero ﬂux even
though no charge is moving.
Positive free charge gives positive net outward D ﬂux; negative free charge gives negative net
outward ﬂux. Charges outside the chosen volume may aﬀect the ﬁeld on its boundary, but are
not counted in QV . If the enclosed free charge stays ﬁxed, the total ﬂux stays ﬁxed even when its
distribution over the surface changes. In particular, a volume containing no free charge has zero
net D ﬂux even in a ﬁeld produced by outside sources. Zero net ﬂux does not mean a zero ﬁeld
at every patch: inward and outward contributions can cancel.

When does Gauss’s law actually determine a ﬁeld?
The law holds for any closed sur-
face, but one number, the total ﬂux, usually cannot determine the unknown ﬁeld at every surface
point. Symmetry sometimes supplies what the integral alone cannot. For an isolated spherical
charge distribution in a uniform isotropic surrounding medium, with no external ﬁelds or asym-
metric boundaries, rotation of the whole conﬁguration changes nothing. No direction tangent to
a sphere is distinguished, so the ﬁeld is radial; all points at the same radius have the same radial
component. Then, and only then in this reasoning, the surface integral becomes a constant ﬁeld
component times 4πr2. Merely choosing a spherical Gaussian surface does not make an arbitrary
ﬁeld spherically symmetric.
