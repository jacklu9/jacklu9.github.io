1.2 Revision of basic electromagnetics

We can now distinguish an outward imbalance from a circulation, and a pointwise measurement from a sum over a boundary. Geometry has supplied the questions. Physics must tell us which answers charges, currents, and materials permit. We return to the apparatus, beginning with what a probe actually measures.

1.2.1 Fields, sources, and material relations

A field is something we can test at a position. Place a very small test charge at a chosen point in the gap. The charge must be small enough that its presence does not appreciably rearrange the charges producing the field. If this test charge is at rest, its electric force is proportional to its charge. Here q is a signed charge in coulombs (C), and force is measured in newtons (N). Consequently E has units N/C, equivalently V/m. The arrow of E points in the direction of the force on a positive test charge. A negative charge feels a force in the opposite direction. The field belongs to the position and time; its definition does not mean that a test charge must actually be present there.

A charge at rest, or one moving parallel to B, has zero magnetic force even when B is nonzero. For positive q, the force direction follows v × B; negative q reverses it. A single zero-force measurement therefore does not establish that the magnetic field is zero.

The dot product counts the component that crosses the surface. A current parallel to a surface does not cross it. Positive current follows the motion of positive charge; negatively charged carriers moving one way produce conventional current in the opposite direction.

For one population of carriers with charge density ρc and mean velocity vc, Jc = ρcvc. Contributions from different populations must be added. This distinction matters in a nearly neutral wire: the net charge density can be almost zero while moving electrons and nearly stationary positive ions produce a nonzero current. One cannot multiply the net charge density by a single velocity unless all relevant charge populations share that velocity.

A surface current Js is a further, different idealisation: current flows within a thin sheet. Its units are A/m. A strip of width w, measured in the sheet perpendicular to a uniform Js, carries current I = |Js|w. Surface charge and surface current will appear when the field equations are applied to material interfaces.

A material responds to the applied fields. In a dielectric, positive and negative charges can shift slightly in opposite directions without transporting free charge all the way through the sample. A separated positive and negative charge pair is an electric dipole. For charges −q and +q, with q > 0, define the separation vector d from the negative charge to the positive charge. The electric dipole moment is p = qd, in C·m: its magnitude records both the amount of charge and how far the charges are separated. The polarisation P is the summed electric dipole moment per unit volume, with units C/m2. It describes the response after averaging over many atoms or molecules.

We distinguish free charge, treated as a specified or transported source, from bound charge associated with this material response. “Free” does not mean that a charge must be moving: a charge deposited on an insulating surface can be a free surface charge. Nor does “bound” mean that its electric field is unimportant. Both contribute to the physical E. The distinction lets us describe the material response collectively, rather than tracking every microscopic charge.

“Linear” means proportional response; “isotropic” means the same response in every direction. “Instantaneous” means that no time delay or memory is included in these time-domain relations. The permittivity ε and permeability µ account for the electric and magnetic material responses; conductivity σ measures the conduction-current response. Their units are F/m, H/m, and S/m. It is convenient to write ε = ε0εr and µ = µ0µr, where the relative parameters are dimensionless.

Homogeneity is a separate assumption: a homogeneous region has material parameters independent of position. Only then may a parameter such as ε be moved through a spatial derivative.

Anisotropic materials require tensor relations; a response with memory is normally described by frequency-dependent parameters in the phasor treatment. Those more general models do not change the meanings of D and H.

1.2.2 Gauss’s law for the electric field

Enclose charge with an imaginary surface. Imagine drawing a closed surface around a region of space. It can pass through vacuum or through matter; it need not be a conductor, a physical membrane, or an actual boundary. It is a Gaussian surface: a geometrical surface chosen to apply Gauss’s law. At each patch, choose the outward normal. The electric-displacement flux measures the signed normal component of D crossing all those patches.

Gauss’s electric law says that this net outward flux equals the free charge enclosed:

Here V is the interior volume and ∂V its closed boundary. The circle on the surface integral indicates closure. Dimensionally, (C/m2)(m2) = C, matching the enclosed charge. Flux is not a count of material particles crossing the Gaussian surface: a static field can have nonzero flux even though no charge is moving.

Positive free charge gives positive net outward D flux; negative free charge gives negative net outward flux. Charges outside the chosen volume may affect the field on its boundary, but are not counted in QV . If the enclosed free charge stays fixed, the total flux stays fixed even when its distribution over the surface changes. In particular, a volume containing no free charge has zero net D flux even in a field produced by outside sources. Zero net flux does not mean a zero field at every patch: inward and outward contributions can cancel.

When does Gauss’s law actually determine a field? The law holds for any closed surface, but one number, the total flux, usually cannot determine the unknown field at every surface point. Symmetry sometimes supplies what the integral alone cannot. For an isolated spherical charge distribution in a uniform isotropic surrounding medium, with no external fields or asymmetric boundaries, rotation of the whole configuration changes nothing. No direction tangent to a sphere is distinguished, so the field is radial; all points at the same radius have the same radial component. Then, and only then in this reasoning, the surface integral becomes a constant field component times 4πr2. Merely choosing a spherical Gaussian surface does not make an arbitrary field spherically symmetric.
