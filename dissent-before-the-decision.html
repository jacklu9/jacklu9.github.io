Dissent Before the Decision

A person can agree with the group and still have the evidence that would change its mind.

Four people review candidates for a job. Three cite the same favorable reference. The fourth also favors that candidate, but saw a schedule showing the person cannot cover the hours the job requires. A summary that counts supporters records unanimity. A summary that counts citations gives the favorable reference three mentions. Neither brings the schedule into the decision. The team seems to agree partly because its members have not yet compared what they know.

Hearing a position is not the same as receiving the evidence

Stasser and Titus (1985) gave discussion groups information about candidates and varied which members received it. When the relevant information was shared, 15 of 18 groups chose the candidate favored by the full information. When key favorable facts were spread across members, only 7 of the 38 groups that reached a decision chose that candidate. The facts supporting the strongest candidate were present in the room in both conditions. In one condition more people arrived already able to recognize their importance.

Disagreement can interrupt the easy repetition of what everyone already knows. Schulz-Hardt et al. (2006) arranged different starting preferences in 135 three-person groups deciding among four options. Groups that began divided found the best choice more often than groups that began in agreement, even when nobody initially supported the best choice. Having someone start out in favor of it helped further. The result does not make the dissenter a proxy for correctness. It shows a case where disagreement changed the path to a choice rather than simply adding one more vote.

A common response is to ask every member to state a view before the discussion moves on. Useful as that may be, it would not surface the schedule in the hiring example if the fourth person just says which candidate they like. A group needs a way to introduce a record that could unsettle an agreement even for the person who supplies it. Preserving who saw the schedule, when it was written, and what it covers lets someone else decide whether it bears on the choice. That is the same distinction between a record and the account someone gives of it.

The contribution can be acknowledged and still go unused

Hoever et al. (2012) had 77 teams of three prepare one theater plan. The teams worked with the same calendar, locations, and ticket and cost information, but their members did not always have the same role priorities. Teams that received distinct priorities produced stronger plans on the study’s combined novelty-and-usefulness measure when they were prompted to consider teammates’ perspectives. In video coding, a team could state every perspective without using any of them to develop its plan. The researchers found evidence connecting the gain to observable use of the different perspectives, not merely to mentioning them. One way to read a meeting record is to ask where a person’s contribution actually changed the combined work.

A tool that helps people write an agreeable statement can be valuable for quite a different task. In a head-to-head comparison, participants preferred the AI-mediated statements produced by Tessler et al. (2024) in 56% of choices, compared with 44% for statements written by a human mediator. The system also supported private critique in its broader design. A statement can give people a fairer account of how they differ. The study measures what statements people favored; it does not tell a hiring committee whether the proposed candidate can cover a shift.

There is also a reason to be careful when a persuasive explanation enters the mix. In the decision tasks studied by Bansal et al. (2021), explanations made reviewers more likely to follow model recommendations when they were right and when they were wrong. The group could become more confident without becoming better at catching errors. To test an assistant in a group, a researcher could separate adding a decisive item to the record from agreeing to the wording of the conclusion. One group could agree on a candidate yet submit a record that makes the schedule easy to challenge. Another could produce an equally confident statement with the schedule nowhere to be found. The first has left the decision open to evidence. The second has only written it down. That difference matters after a decision is entered as much as it does before the group votes.
