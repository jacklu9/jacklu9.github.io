The local bond picture and the extended crystal states describe the same solid at different levels; neither is a drawing of electron trajectories.

An ideal crystal has no extended band states in the forbidden energy interval. “Continuous band” means that allowed levels are closely spaced within a band; it does not mean that every energy is allowed. Impurities can introduce additional localised states in the interval, a possibility we will use when we alter the carrier population.

Return to the opening drawing. At Γ, several curves occur at the same horizontal position. An address alone is therefore insufficient to identify a state. We also need a band label, and eventually a spin label. The curve tells us its energy once those choices are made. We can now investigate what the horizontal address actually selects.

1.1.1 Wavevector

The horizontal address selects a wave pattern

When we replaced the lower endpoint at X by the one at Γ, we changed something besides its energy. The horizontal axis is labelled k, not x. It distinguishes electron wave patterns; it does not mark where the electron sits inside the sample. To see why a new coordinate is useful, start with two waves that repeat over different distances.

The marked crests have the same phase. Their separation is the wavelength. In a plane wave, each crest extends sideways into an equal-phase plane: the “fronts” in the later diagram.

A sharply localised electron wave packet is a superposition of wavevectors rather than one infinitely extended plane wave. This is why the wavevector of a state and the position of an electron are different kinds of information.

Why the two sides of the picture name different directions

The opening figure does not merely label the axis k. It writes ⟨100⟩ on one side and ⟨111⟩ on the other. A wavelength gives a size, but a three-dimensional wave also has a direction. These bracketed symbols tell us which directions the graph explores. Their integers refer to the crystal’s own axes, so we first need a coordinate frame tied to its repeating structure. A crystal has an atomic arrangement that repeats in space. A lattice is the mathematical grid of equivalent repeat positions. A basis is the atom or group of atoms attached to every lattice point. Lattice plus basis gives the actual crystal structure. A unit cell is a region whose translations reproduce the structure; a primitive cell contains one lattice point, whereas a conventional cell may be larger because it displays the symmetry more clearly.

Si has the diamond structure and GaAs has the zinc-blende structure. Both can be described using a face-centred cubic (FCC) lattice with a two-atom basis: two Si atoms for diamond, or one Ga and one As for zinc blende. FCC means there are lattice points at the corners and face centres of the conventional cube. The conventional cube edge is the lattice constant a, a length. The cube used below is a coordinate frame for discussing directions; the arrows are not bonds and the cube is not a drawing of all atoms in Si.

Why angle brackets instead of square brackets? Square brackets select a direction; angle brackets name its symmetry-related family. In a cubic crystal,

For centrosymmetric cubic Si, the family ⟨111⟩ contains the eight combinations [±1, ±1, ±1]. A cube has equivalent edge directions and equivalent body-diagonal directions, but an edge direction is not equivalent to a body diagonal. In a band plot, ⟨100⟩ therefore says that a segment follows an axis-type direction; ⟨111⟩ says it follows a body-diagonal-type direction. The angle brackets are not inequality signs. Exact symmetry equivalence depends on the crystal structure: in polar GaAs, opposite oriented [111] directions can have different atomic terminations. The band-plot labels here identify the axis-type and body-diagonal-type cuts; they do not assert identical properties for every oriented crystal surface.

Round brackets have another meaning: (hkl) labels crystal planes, while {hkl} labels a symmetry-related family of planes. Plane indices and direction indices should not be interchanged. In a cubic crystal the normal to (100) is parallel to [100], but the plane itself is a two-dimensional set, whereas the direction is one-dimensional.1

Read the symbols aloud. [111]: “the direction with equal positive components along all three cubic axes.” ⟨111⟩: “the family of symmetry-equivalent body-diagonal directions.” Neither tells you how far to travel.

Now look again at the two labels under the opening curve. The left segment explores an axis-type direction; the right segment explores a body-diagonal-type direction. Passing through the centre switches the chosen direction of the wavevector cut. It does not change the material from one substance to another. The labels tell us which way to look; they do not yet say how far along that direction the points X and L lie.

What makes Gamma, X and L special addresses?

The question about distance brings us to the named points. Γ is the reference address k = 0. The other labels identify particular nonzero wavevectors in the repeating crystal’s natural wavevector region. To see why such a region exists, combine the two ideas we already have: electron waves and a repeating atomic arrangement. Real space contains positions and atomic repeat distances. Reciprocal space, or k-space, contains wavevectors and has inverse-length units. Each point in k-space labels a spatial phase pattern, not a location inside the sample.

A crystal’s repeating potential allows stationary electron states to be written in Bloch form,

R is any lattice translation. The function ubk repeats with the lattice, while the exponential supplies the phase change between cells. The integer b identifies the band: at the same k, several allowed wave patterns and energies can exist. We use b here to avoid confusing a band index with the electron concentration n used later. The symbol ubk is a periodic function here, not a direction index u. A Bloch state need not be a simple sinusoid inside a unit cell; at k = 0 the periodic factor can still vary strongly within the cell.

The repeated phasor rows show why k and k + G have the same lattice-translation eigenvalue. They do not claim that multiplying one unchanged uk by two different plane waves gives the same wavefunction between lattice points.

The allowed energies form functions Eb(k). The collection of these functions is the band struc-ture. The word dispersion refers to the dependence of energy on wavevector. ¯hk is called crystal momentum; in a lattice it need not equal the mechanical momentum m0v.

Why can a finite region represent all wavevector labels? In one dimension with repeat distance a, the phase change between neighbouring cells is eika. Replacing k by k + 2π/a leaves this translation phase unchanged:

A reciprocal-lattice vector G generalises this property: eiG·R = 1 for every lattice translation. Bloch labels differing by G are equivalent after the periodic factor is relabelled. One can select one representative from each equivalence class.

The first Brillouin zone is the region of reciprocal space closer to the reciprocal-lattice origin than to any other reciprocal-lattice point. Its boundaries mark a natural edge of this nonredundant labelling region. In the one-dimensional example it is −π/a ≤ k ≤ π/a. The three-dimensional zone shape depends on the crystal lattice; it is not generally a cube and is not the physical boundary of the sample.

In a periodic-boundary counting model, the grid step is 2π/Lsample, whereas reciprocal repetition is 2π/a. What are Γ, X and L? These are conventional names for particular symmetry points in reciprocal space. The Greek capital letter Γ (Gamma) is the zone centre, k = 0. For the FCC lattice relevant to Si and GaAs, X is a zone-boundary point along an axis-type direction and L is a boundary point along a body-diagonal-type direction. A reciprocal-lattice vector must give +1 for every lattice translation. This one counterexample shows that kX is not such a vector: X is not merely Γ written with a repeated label. The points X and L lie on the zone boundary; they are not reciprocal-lattice nodes. A direction specifies a ray; a symmetry point also specifies where on that ray to stop. Therefore ⟨100⟩ and X are related but are not interchangeable labels. Γ is not an atom, a band, or an energy. Eb(Γ) means the energy on Band b at the zone centre.

Unfold the path and return to the original axis

A full band Eb(kx, ky, kz) depends on three wavevector components. A two-dimensional printed graph usually shows selected one-dimensional cuts through that function. The labelled portion of the example follows X → Γ → L: first back towards the origin along an axis-type ray, then outward along a body diagonal.

Follow the two colours through all three views. The printed path first approaches Γ along an axis-type ray, then leaves it along a body diagonal. Its two sides are not negative and positive values of one Cartesian component, and their printed lengths need not represent equal reciprocal-space distances.

For clarity, a general path could be described by a plotting coordinate s, with a wavevector k(s) at each point. A band trace is then Eb[k(s)]. Many course graphs label the horizontal axis simply k even when it has this path meaning. The labels X, Γ, L and the direction brackets tell you how to interpret it.

One vertical cut, several possible states Choose one position on the horizontal path. A vertical line there intersects several bands because several states can have that same wavevector but different band indices and energies. A band curve is a catalogue of possible states. It is not a track on which a particle rolls downhill in physical space, nor does drawing a curve imply that every state on it is occupied.

The small curve extensions beyond X and L remain schematic. They do not turn the labelled route into one signed Cartesian axis.

Why several curves survive at one address At Γ, the black branch is at 1.92 eV, the blue and orange branches meet at zero, and the teal branch lies 0.33 eV below it. The wavevector is the same for all four. Their band labels select different electronic patterns within the crystal, with different allowed energies. Two distinct states can also share an energy, as the blue and orange branches do here; they are then called degenerate. This energy equality is separate from the later phrase “nondegenerate carrier statistics.”

A compact description is Eb(k): choose wavevector k and band b, then read the energy. Our initial made exactly those two choices. The branch numbers belong to this figure’s legend; they are not universal names for conduction and valence bands. The more general letters c and v identify the two band edges, while the labels X, Γ and L identify wavevectors.
