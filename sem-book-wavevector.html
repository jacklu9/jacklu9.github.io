The local bond picture and the extended crystal states describe the same solid at different levels;

An ideal crystal has no extended band states in the forbidden energy interval. “Continuous band”
means that allowed levels are closely spaced within a band; it does not mean that every energy is
allowed. Impurities can introduce additional localised states in the interval, a possibility we will
use when we alter the carrier population.

Why the two sides of the picture name different directions
The opening figure does not merely label the axis k. It writes ⟨100⟩on one side and ⟨111⟩on
the other. A wavelength gives a size, but a three-dimensional wave also has a direction. These
bracketed symbols tell us which directions the graph explores. Their integers refer to the crystal’s
own axes, so we first need a coordinate frame tied to its repeating structure. A crystal has an
atomic arrangement that repeats in space. A lattice is the mathematical grid of equivalent repeat
positions. A basis is the atom or group of atoms attached to every lattice point. Lattice plus
basis gives the actual crystal structure. A unit cell is a region whose translations reproduce the
structure; a primitive cell contains one lattice point, whereas a conventional cell may be larger
because it displays the symmetry more clearly.
Si has the diamond structure and GaAs has the zinc-blende structure. Both can be described using
a face-centred cubic (FCC) lattice with a two-atom basis: two Si atoms for diamond, or one Ga
and one As for zinc blende. FCC means there are lattice points at the corners and face centres of
the conventional cube. The conventional cube edge is the lattice constant a, a length. The cube
used below is a coordinate frame for discussing directions; the arrows are not bonds and the cube
is not a drawing of all atoms in Si.

Why angle brackets instead of square brackets?
Square brackets select a direction; angle
brackets name its symmetry-related family. In a cubic crystal,
⟨100⟩= {[100], [¯100], [010], [0¯10], [001], [00¯1]}.
For centrosymmetric cubic Si, the family ⟨111⟩contains the eight combinations [±1, ±1, ±1]. A
cube has equivalent edge directions and equivalent body-diagonal directions, but an edge direction
is not equivalent to a body diagonal. In a band plot, ⟨100⟩therefore says that a segment follows an
axis-type direction; ⟨111⟩says it follows a body-diagonal-type direction. The angle brackets are not
inequality signs. Exact symmetry equivalence depends on the crystal structure: in polar GaAs,
opposite oriented [111] directions can have different atomic terminations. The band-plot labels
here identify the axis-type and body-diagonal-type cuts; they do not assert identical properties for
every oriented crystal surface.
Round brackets have another meaning: (hkl) labels crystal planes, while {hkl} labels a symmetry-
related family of planes. Plane indices and direction indices should not be interchanged. In a
cubic crystal the normal to (100) is parallel to [100], but the plane itself is a two-dimensional set,
whereas the direction is one-dimensional.1
Read the symbols aloud.
[111]: “the direction with equal positive components along
all three cubic axes.” ⟨111⟩: “the family of symmetry-equivalent body-diagonal directions.”
Neither tells you how far to travel.
Now look again at the two labels under the opening curve. The left segment explores an axis-type
direction; the right segment explores a body-diagonal-type direction. Passing through the centre
switches the chosen direction of the wavevector cut. It does not change the material from one
substance to another. The labels tell us which way to look; they do not yet say how far along that
direction the points X and L lie.

What makes Gamma, X and L special addresses?
The question about distance brings us to the named points. Γ is the reference address k = 0. The
other labels identify particular nonzero wavevectors in the repeating crystal’s natural wavevector
region. To see why such a region exists, combine the two ideas we already have: electron waves
and a repeating atomic arrangement. Real space contains positions and atomic repeat distances.
Reciprocal space, or k-space, contains wavevectors and has inverse-length units. Each point in
k-space labels a spatial phase pattern, not a location inside the sample.
A crystal’s repeating potential allows stationary electron states to be written in Bloch form,
ψbk(r) = ubk(r)eik·r,
ubk(r + R) = ubk(r).
R is any lattice translation.
The function ubk repeats with the lattice, while the exponential
supplies the phase change between cells. The integer b identifies the band: at the same k, several
allowed wave patterns and energies can exist. We use b here to avoid confusing a band index with
the electron concentration n used later. The symbol ubk is a periodic function here, not a direction
index u. A Bloch state need not be a simple sinusoid inside a unit cell; at k = 0 the periodic factor
can still vary strongly within the cell.

Why can a finite region represent all wavevector labels?
In one dimension with repeat
distance a, the phase change between neighbouring cells is eika. Replacing k by k + 2π/a leaves
this translation phase unchanged:
ei(k+2π/a)a = eikaei2π = eika.
A reciprocal-lattice vector G generalises this property: eiG·R = 1 for every lattice translation.
Bloch labels differing by G are equivalent after the periodic factor is relabelled. One can select
one representative from each equivalence class.
The first Brillouin zone is the region of reciprocal space closer to the reciprocal-lattice origin
than to any other reciprocal-lattice point. Its boundaries mark a natural edge of this nonredundant
labelling region. In the one-dimensional example it is −π/a ≤k ≤π/a. The three-dimensional
zone shape depends on the crystal lattice; it is not generally a cube and is not the physical boundary
of the sample.

k even when it has this path meaning. The labels X, Γ, L and the direction brackets tell you how

One vertical cut, several possible states
Choose one position on the horizontal path. A
vertical line there intersects several bands because several states can have that same wavevector
but different band indices and energies. A band curve is a catalogue of possible states. It is not
a track on which a particle rolls downhill in physical space, nor does drawing a curve imply that
every state on it is occupied.
The small curve extensions beyond X and L remain schematic. They do not turn the labelled
route into one signed Cartesian axis.
Why several curves survive at one address
At Γ, the black branch is at 1.92 eV, the blue
and orange branches meet at zero, and the teal branch lies 0.33 eV below it. The wavevector is
the same for all four. Their band labels select different electronic patterns within the crystal, with
different allowed energies. Two distinct states can also share an energy, as the blue and orange
branches do here; they are then called degenerate. This energy equality is separate from the
later phrase “nondegenerate carrier statistics.”
