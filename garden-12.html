What a work allows someone to see

In 1913, Auguste Rodin had scaffolding built in his garden at Meudon. A cast of The Burghers of Calais had been purchased for Britain, and Rodin wanted to see what would happen if the six figures were displayed about five metres above the ground. In Jacques-Ernest Bulloz’s photograph of the experiment, the bodies rise against the sky. The spaces between them become part of a common outline. Rodin was also interested in displaying the figures directly on the paving stones, where passersby could encounter them close to their own height. The installation in Calais in 1895 realized neither extreme (Musée Rodin, n.d.-b, n.d.-c). In each proposal the same group remains in place relative to itself. What changes is what a person approaching it can see.

The question is larger than the height of a monument. Someone can leave an exhibit having learned an interpretation or having found a new way to look. An exhibition can do either, or both. The differences show up in where people stand, what they return to after reading, the detail they can point out to someone else, and what they notice in a work they have never seen.

A label sends the eye somewhere

A before-and-after study at Vienna’s Belvedere recorded people’s movements and gaze around a collection that was rehung (Reitstätter et al., 2020). Across the thirteen works present in both arrangements, average looking time rose from roughly 16 to 20 seconds per work, while label reading rose from about 2 to 7 seconds. For Klimt’s The Kiss, however, the amount of time spent looking at the painting barely changed, roughly 57 seconds before and 58 after; time with its label went from roughly 2 to 14 seconds. The two measures expose a distinction that a single figure for time near the painting would conceal.

Castellotti et al. (2023) changed the labels while leaving the paintings in place. Thirty people with little formal art training visited the same eight paintings twice at the Collezione Roberto Casamonti. Everyone saw basic labels on the first visit. On the second, twenty received descriptions of the works and how they were made, while ten encountered the same basic labels. The visitors given new descriptions looked at the paintings longer; those given the same labels looked for less time on the return visit. Participants with the descriptions also rated the art as easier to understand. The study detected no corresponding increase in appreciation. The label could send someone back to the surface without settling what they should make of it.

When the researchers repeated this comparison with digital reproductions, the descriptive labels again improved rated understanding, but the description group did not reliably spend longer looking at the image a second time (Castellotti et al., 2024). The comparison used different groups of viewers rather than transferring the same people between gallery and laboratory. It raises a useful distinction for an online gallery: a text can help a visitor make sense of an image without changing how long they keep looking.

A companion can point without deciding the answer

In Dirk vom Lehn’s museum video research, one visitor at the Victoria and Albert Museum drew a companion’s attention to a sculpture’s feet. As the second person approached, the first pointed out veins and toenails and traced the detail in the air (vom Lehn, 2006). Words, gesture, and position worked together. By walking over, the companion gained access to what the other visitor had noticed. They could then look for themselves.

Visitors can also arrange such encounters before anyone arrives. At Nottingham Contemporary, Fosh et al. (2014) studied eight pairs, one member of each making a gallery route for a friend or partner. One designer selected Tchaikovsky’s Romeo and Juliet for a sculpture showing a body giving birth to a leg, then demonstrated the stance she wanted her partner to try. Both found the posture helped them imagine the work. He would have preferred more playful music; she had found the sculpture eerie. The route gave the disagreement a place to start. In another pair, a participant stopped acting out the instructions. The two took off one headphone each and discussed the work instead. From the perspective of a route-completion log, that is a departure. From the perspective of the visit, it is the moment the exchange opened up.

The next question is whether an acquired way of looking carries over. A lottery for school visits at Crystal Bridges let Bowen et al. (2014) compare students who received an earlier visit with those whose visit was deferred. About two weeks after the early visit, 3,811 students across 70 groups wrote briefly about Bo Bartlett’s The Box, a painting outside the museum’s collection. They explained what they thought was happening and what in the painting made them think so. Blind scoring put the visits’ estimated gain at roughly one-tenth of a standard deviation on the composite. The test offered a different work and asked for evidence, so it could register more than recall of what an educator had said in the gallery.

A cut is not always a change in the event

A film offers a different arrangement. The viewer does not walk around the object; the camera position changes. Smith and Henderson (2008) recorded eye movements while seven people watched film excerpts and signaled when they saw a new shot. Within a comparable set of 117 straight cuts, viewers missed about a third of cuts that continued an action across a change of shot, about a quarter of other cuts within the same scene, and about a tenth between scenes. Most misses did not coincide with a blink or a rapid eye movement. Tracking the ongoing action and detecting the edit are not the same task.

Magliano and Zacks (2011) instead asked viewers of The Red Balloon to mark boundaries in the events. A cut that continued an action was associated with more fine-grained boundaries, but not with reliably more coarse boundaries. An editor can change the viewer’s physical vantage without requiring the viewer to understand that a new part of the story has begun. In a study with another set of films, keeping the shot fixed but replacing the soundtrack changed where people looked: 48% of fixations landed on faces during their speaking intervals with the original sound, compared with 38–40% with three replacement soundtracks (Coutrot & Guyader, 2014). Even unrelated speech in the same language did not have the same effect.

A curator or an editor can choose where an encounter begins, what is visible from there, and when to bring attention to another part. None of those choices specifies the viewer’s eventual reading. A guide can be useful precisely when two visitors stop following it and use the work to explain their difference. A label can earn its place by sending a reader back to a feature the reader can now describe. The more revealing measure is often what someone is able to point out next.
