The triple integral is three-dimensional, and dV denotes a small positive volume element. The usual theorem requires a sufficiently smooth field throughout the volume and a suitable piecewise smooth closed boundary. A singularity inside, such as an ideal point source, must be handled separately; it cannot simply be ignored because derivatives away from it vanish.

This theorem is geometric bookkeeping, not yet an electromagnetic law. It applies to many vector fields. Gauss’s electric law will supply the physical statement that the divergence of D equals charge density. Also, the theorem concerns the entire closed boundary: one cannot replace flux through a single open face by a volume integral without including the remaining faces.

1.1.6 Curl of a vector field

A field can enter a region and leave in equal amounts while still showing a preferred circulation around a loop. Our flux balance would miss that organisation. To describe it, turn from a closed surface to an oriented path.

Local turning is different from the shape of a long field line. Divergence asks about crossing a closed surface. Curl asks about following a small closed path: does the field help a traversal around the loop more than it opposes it? Imagine a tiny freely turning paddle wheel in a velocity field. Translation can carry its centre along without spinning it, whereas differences in velocity on opposite sides can produce local rotation. The wheel picture is an intuition for local circulation, not a claim that electromagnetic fields are literally a moving liquid.

The following three patterns are deliberately different. Rigid rotation has curved field lines and nonzero curl. A shear flow has straight field lines but nonzero curl. A field circulating more slowly on larger circles can have curved field lines and zero curl away from a singular axis. Thus the curvature of a field line is not a test for curl.

At each one, F · dr selects the field component along that displacement and multiplies by its length. 1.1.7 Stokes’s theorem

Neighbouring loops cancel along shared edges. Cover an oriented surface with many small adjacent patches. Give every patch the circulation direction required by its chosen normal. Along an edge shared by two patches, one boundary runs one way and the other runs the opposite way. Their line-integral contributions cancel. Once all patches are added, only the outside boundary remains. This is the surface-and-edge counterpart of the volume-and-face cancellation in Gauss’s theorem.

Viewed from the tip of a local positive normal towards its plane, the positive boundary direction is counterclockwise. For a planar xy-surface with ˆ, that means counterclockwise as seen from +z. Reversing both the normal and the traversal multiplies both sides by −1 and preserves

the equality; reversing only one makes the signs inconsistent. A curved surface is handled by the same local orientation rule and its induced boundary direction.

Two suitable surfaces with the same oriented boundary give the same integral of curl when the field is smooth on each spanning surface. One may therefore choose whichever surface makes calculation easiest. This does not say that is independent of the spanning surface, and it does not permit deforming a spanning surface through an excluded singularity. What the local operations measure

∇f is a vector describing a scalar field’s first-order slope. Its projection onto a unit direction gives the slope along that direction. ∇· F is a scalar describing local net outward flux per volume. Gauss’s theorem adds that local quantity over a volume to obtain total outward flux across its closed boundary. ∇× F is a vector describing local circulation per area and its axis. Stokes’s theorem adds its normal component over an oriented surface to obtain circulation around that surface’s boundary. For sufficiently smooth fields, ∇× ∇f = 0 and ∇· (∇× F ) = 0.
