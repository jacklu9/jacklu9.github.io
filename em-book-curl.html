The triple integral is three-dimensional, and dV denotes a small positive volume element. The

ﬁelds. Gauss’s electric law will supply the physical statement that the divergence of D equals
charge density. Also, the theorem concerns the entire closed boundary: one cannot replace ﬂux
through a single open face by a volume integral without including the remaining faces.

1.1.6
Curl of a vector ﬁeld
A ﬁeld can enter a region and leave in equal amounts while still showing a preferred circulation
around a loop. Our ﬂux balance would miss that organisation. To describe it, turn from a closed
surface to an oriented path.
Local turning is diﬀerent from the shape of a long ﬁeld line.
Divergence asks about
crossing a closed surface. Curl asks about following a small closed path: does the ﬁeld help a
traversal around the loop more than it opposes it? Imagine a tiny freely turning paddle wheel in
a velocity ﬁeld. Translation can carry its centre along without spinning it, whereas diﬀerences in
velocity on opposite sides can produce local rotation. The wheel picture is an intuition for local
circulation, not a claim that electromagnetic ﬁelds are literally a moving liquid.
The following three patterns are deliberately diﬀerent. Rigid rotation has curved ﬁeld lines and
nonzero curl. A shear ﬂow has straight ﬁeld lines but nonzero curl. A ﬁeld circulating more slowly
on larger circles can have curved ﬁeld lines and zero curl away from a singular axis. Thus the
curvature of a ﬁeld line is not a test for curl.

1.1.7
Stokes’s theorem
Neighbouring loops cancel along shared edges.
Cover an oriented surface with many small
adjacent patches. Give every patch the circulation direction required by its chosen normal. Along
an edge shared by two patches, one boundary runs one way and the other runs the opposite way.
Their line-integral contributions cancel. Once all patches are added, only the outside boundary
remains. This is the surface-and-edge counterpart of the volume-and-face cancellation in Gauss’s
theorem.

Two identities connect the geometric operations.
For ﬁelds with continuous second partial
derivatives, the order of mixed partial diﬀerentiation can be exchanged. For a scalar ﬁeld f, the
z-component of the curl of its gradient is
(∇× ∇f)z = ∂x(∂yf) −∂y(∂xf) = 0.
The other components cancel in the same way, so ∇× ∇f = 0. A gradient records changes of one
scalar; around a closed path its net change is zero when that scalar is single-valued. This explains
the absence of electrostatic circulation for E = −∇V .
For a vector ﬁeld, expand the divergence of its curl:
∇· (∇× F ) = ∂x∂yFz −∂x∂zFy + ∂y∂zFx −∂y∂xFz
+ ∂z∂xFy −∂z∂yFx = 0.
Each mixed derivative cancels another term.
Geometrically, the ﬂux of curl through a closed
surface has no remaining outer edge on which a net boundary circulation could survive. These
identities will become useful consistency checks on Maxwell’s equations:
What the local operations measure
∇f is a vector describing a scalar ﬁeld’s ﬁrst-order slope. Its projection onto a unit direction
gives the slope along that direction.
∇· F is a scalar describing local net outward ﬂux per volume. Gauss’s theorem adds that
local quantity over a volume to obtain total outward ﬂux across its closed boundary.
∇× F is a vector describing local circulation per area and its axis. Stokes’s theorem adds
its normal component over an oriented surface to obtain circulation around that surface’s
boundary.
For suﬃciently smooth ﬁelds, ∇× ∇f = 0 and ∇· (∇× F ) = 0.
