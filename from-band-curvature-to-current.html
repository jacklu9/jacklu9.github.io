From band curvature to current

Use the curvature of a band to find how carriers accelerate, then keep particle motion and conventional current separate when drift and diffusion compete.

An electron can move to the left while the meter assigns its current to the right. In a nonuniform semiconductor the same meter can read zero even when a local field is pushing carriers and a concentration gradient drives them the other way. The route from a band plot to that reading has three steps: find the acceleration of a wave packet, decide what scattering does to the average motion, and convert each particle flux into conventional current using its charge.

Notation for this lesson: the horizontal direction is +x+x, q>0q>0 is the elementary charge, and E\mathcal E is the electric field along xx. The letter EE denotes electron energy, not field. The carrier concentrations nn and pp come from counting available states and their occupation.

Why curvature sets the response to a force

In a one-dimensional band, a narrow wave packet has group velocity v=(1/ℏ)dE/dkv=(1/\hbar)dE/dk. Under a weak force the semiclassical equation is ℏ dk/dt=F\hbar\,dk/dt=F. Differentiating the velocity with respect to time gives

dvdt=1ℏd2Edk2dkdt=Fℏ2d2Edk2≡Fm∗.\frac{dv}{dt}=\frac{1}{\hbar}\frac{d^2E}{dk^2}\frac{dk}{dt}=\frac{F}{\hbar^2}\frac{d^2E}{dk^2}\equiv\frac{F}{m^*}.

The slope says how fast the packet moves at its present kk. Curvature says how quickly that velocity will change under a force. At a parabolic minimum the curvature is positive. For example, with E(k)=Ec+A(k−k0)2E(k)=E_c+A(k-k_0)^2, the effective mass is ℏ2/(2A)\hbar^2/(2A). Double AA, leaving the edge and its position fixed, and the mass halves. A valence-band electron near a maximum has negative curvature. It is more convenient to describe departures from a filled valence band as positively charged holes, with a positive mass set by the magnitude of the valence curvature. Neither description changes the free-electron mass. Outside the local parabolic region the response need not be constant, and in three dimensions it can depend on the direction of the force.

Scattering can make a steady drift

Apply a field to a sample with no net velocity initially. In a parabolic conduction valley, a free interval changes an electron’s velocity according to dv/dt=−qE/mn∗dv/dt=-q\mathcal E/m_n^*. That would produce a growing bias if there were no scattering and the model remained valid for arbitrarily long flights. Momentum-relaxing scattering weakens the ensemble’s directional bias. The relaxation-time model represents that loss by −vˉn/τn-\bar v_n/\tau_n in the equation for the average velocity:

dvˉndt=−qEmn∗−vˉnτn,vˉn(t)=−qτnmn∗E(1−e−t/τn).\frac{d\bar v_n}{dt}=-\frac{q\mathcal E}{m_n^*}-\frac{\bar v_n}{\tau_n},\qquad \bar v_n(t)=-\frac{q\tau_n}{m_n^*}\mathcal E(1-e^{-t/\tau_n}).

The solution assumes the field was switched on at t=0t=0 and is then held fixed. Long afterward vˉn=−μnE\bar v_n=-\mu_n\mathcal E, where μn=qτn/mn∗\mu_n=q\tau_n/m_n^* is positive. The hole model gives vˉp=+μpE\bar v_p=+\mu_p\mathcal E. These are small net velocities of populations, not the speeds of individual carriers. A shorter relaxation time can lower mobility even if the mass is unchanged. Conversely, comparing effective masses alone is not enough to compare mobilities across materials with different scattering rates. Treat constant mobility as a low-field approximation.

For a uniform sample, the conventional current from electrons is (−q)nvˉn=qnμnE(-q)n\bar v_n=qn\mu_n\mathcal E. Holes give qpμpEqp\mu_p\mathcal E. Both drift currents point along a positive field even though the carriers travel in opposite directions. The conductivity is q(nμn+pμp)q(n\mu_n+p\mu_p). Equal carrier numbers therefore need not imply equal contributions to current.

A gradient introduces a second arrow

Convert mobility before combining an SI field and a density quoted in cm−3^{-3}. For example, 500 cm2^2/(V s) is 0.050 m2^2/(V s), while 2.0×10152.0\times10^{15} cm−3^{-3} is 2.0×10212.0\times10^{21} m−3^{-3}. Alternatively, keep centimeters throughout the calculation: a mobility in cm2^2/(V s) and field in V/cm produce a mean velocity in cm/s. Multiplying by charge and number per cm3^3 then produces A/cm2^2. Dimensional consistency and current direction are separate checks; a correctly converted electron flux still needs its minus sign.

With more electrons on the right than the left, random motion transfers more electrons across a plane from right to left than from left to right. The diffusion particle flux is −Dn dn/dx-D_n\,dn/dx, with Dn>0D_n>0. Multiplying by the negative charge changes the sign: electron diffusion current is +qDn dn/dx+qD_n\,dn/dx. For holes the particle flux is −Dp dp/dx-D_p\,dp/dx, and the positive charge leaves its sign unchanged. Adding field-driven and diffusion contributions gives

Jn=qnμnE+qDndndx,Jp=qpμpE−qDpdpdx.J_n=qn\mu_n\mathcal E+qD_n\frac{dn}{dx},\qquad J_p=qp\mu_p\mathcal E-qD_p\frac{dp}{dx}.

For electrons, divide by the positive factor qnμnqn\mu_n to compare the terms in field units. The equivalent diffusion field is (Dn/μn) dln⁡n/dx(D_n/\mu_n)\,d\ln n/dx; the electron current has the sign of its sum with the electric field. A positive density gradient helps a positive field drive positive electron current. The particle motions are both to the left in that case.

Start with a positive gradient and zero field. Then change the field until net current vanishes. The arrow associated with diffusion need not shrink to zero. The field contributes an equal current in the opposite direction. Flip both signs and compare the result.

Why the two currents cancel at equilibrium

In a uniform host with a slowly varying potential ϕ(x)\phi(x), an electron’s band-edge energy is shifted by −qϕ-q\phi. Therefore dEc/dx=qEdE_c/dx=q\mathcal E, because E=−dϕ/dx\mathcal E=-d\phi/dx. A flat equilibrium Fermi level does not imply flat bands. At uniform temperature with a fixed local NcN_c, the dilute occupation law gives dln⁡n/dx=−(q/kBT)Ed\ln n/dx=-(q/k_BT)\mathcal E. Inserting that gradient into the electron equation gives

Jn=qnE(μn−qDnkBT).J_n=qn\mathcal E\left(\mu_n-\frac{qD_n}{k_BT}\right).

At equilibrium Jn=0J_n=0 even where the field is nonzero. Within this isothermal, nondegenerate drift–diffusion model, the required ratio is Dn/μn=kBT/qD_n/\mu_n=k_BT/q. The hole calculation has the opposite concentration gradient for the same field and yields the same ratio for holes. A uniform, zero-field sample gives no information about this balance, since both components vanish individually.

Example: at 300 K, take μn=500\mu_n=500 cm2^2/(V s), so Dn≈12.9D_n\approx12.9 cm2^2/s. If dln⁡n/dx=+80d\ln n/dx=+80 cm−1^{-1}, its equivalent field is +2.07+2.07 V/cm. An electric field of −2.07-2.07 V/cm cancels it locally. If instead the field is −1.00-1.00 V/cm at a point where n=2.0×1015n=2.0\times10^{15} cm−3^{-3}, the net electron current density is about +0.17+0.17 A/cm2^2. Its positive sign is conventional; the net motion of the electrons is leftward. This is a local prediction, not a claim that the density profile will persist if it is not held in place.

Check your understanding

A band minimum has zero slope. Is its effective mass infinite?

Not necessarily. The group velocity is zero at the minimum, but the effective mass depends on curvature. A parabolic minimum with nonzero curvature has finite mass.

The electric field is zero, and hole concentration increases to the right. Which way do the hole particles and their conventional current go?

Both point left. Particles diffuse down the gradient, and multiplying by a positive charge does not reverse their flux.

If Ec(x)E_c(x) rises to the right in an isothermal, nondegenerate sample at equilibrium, what signs do the electron drift and diffusion current terms take?

The field is positive, so electron drift current is positive. With flat EFE_F, electrons become less abundant to the right. Thus dn/dx<0dn/dx<0, and electron diffusion current is negative. They cancel locally.
