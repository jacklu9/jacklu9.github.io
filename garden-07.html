The evidence that could overturn an answer

In an experiment with management consultants, the most convincing document did not always contain the best decision. The consultants had to recommend a brand from a spreadsheet and a set of interviews. The figures seemed to point to an answer on their own, but the interviews changed how those figures should be read. About 85 percent of the consultants without GPT-4 chose correctly. The two groups with GPT-4 access scored about 71 and 60 percent, even as their written answers improved on the study’s rubric. In a separate set of product-development tasks in the same experiment, access to the model improved both speed and assessed quality. The strategy case was different because one source could overturn the conclusion that another source appeared to support (Dell’Acqua et al., 2026).

That pattern is easy to miss if the document is judged first and the choice second. A reviewer can admire the order and precision of an argument before asking what would make it wrong. The question for a system that supplies recommendations is whether it helps the reviewer reach the evidence that could change the decision.

A reason someone can inspect

One way to examine that problem is to strip everything down to a maze. In an experiment by Vasconcelos et al. (2023), an assistant recommended an exit and offered directions. One version drew the route directly on the maze. Another listed the corresponding turns beside it. In the medium and hard mazes, participants accepted fewer wrong exits when they could inspect the overlaid route. The proposed destination was still only a recommendation. The drawn path made it easier to see whether it could actually be reached.

For a different set of tasks, Bansal et al. (2021) found that adding an explanation to an answer and confidence estimate made people more inclined to agree with the assistant, including on trials where it was wrong. Participants classified the sentiment of book and beer reviews and answered logical reasoning questions. The explanation condition did not reliably improve accuracy over seeing the assistant’s answer and confidence alone. That result is compatible with the maze experiment. The studies did not manipulate the same thing: one compared different costs of checking a route, while the other examined what happened when explanations were added to a recommendation. An explanation can make a choice easier to understand without making it easier to dispute.

Time matters too. Buçinca et al. (2021) asked people to identify which ingredient to remove from a meal and then choose a replacement. The participants in some conditions saw an assistant’s advice immediately; in others they could request it or had to wait or make an initial choice before it appeared. On trials where the assistant named the wrong ingredient, the estimated rate of repeating that mistake fell from 64 percent with immediate explanatory advice to 48 percent across the three alternatives. The corresponding improvement was less clear for the whole two-part answer. Changing when the advice appeared affected a particular decision inside the task. A final score can obscure that because it counts choosing the ingredient and choosing the replacement together.

Check what the system never sends to review

A review process can change who sees the evidence at all. Bondi et al. (2022) used camera-trap photographs for an experiment in which a model deferred some animal-or-no-animal decisions to a person. Reviewers sometimes saw only the fact that the model had deferred, and sometimes saw no message about the model. The former group identified the images correctly about 62 percent of the time, compared with about 58 percent in the latter condition. The experiment used a set of deferred images balanced by whether the model’s proposed answer was right. That makes the comparison useful for judging what the notice did. It does not say how well a deployed process would do across all photographs: the set left with the model and the frequency of referral would also count.

The distinction helps explain a broader result. In 106 experiments comparing people, AI and people using AI, Vaccaro et al. (2024) found that the combined arrangement outperformed people alone on average. It fell short of the stronger of the two solo contributors on average, by a standardized effect size of 0.23. Those baselines answer different questions. Better than unaided people can be an achievement worth having. It is not evidence that giving both contributors a role improved on whichever could already do the task better.

There is a parallel problem when people supervise the evaluation itself. A collection of minimally modified summaries can expose models that miss a single factual substitution while responding to the surrounding fluent text (Laban et al., 2023). But if auditors look only at cases on which automatic judges disagree, they can tell where the judges differ without knowing how often the judges are wrong together. Fisch et al. (2024) give one way to use automated scores without treating them as ground truth: use them to help estimate a population quantity, then correct it against a sample reviewed by people. Checking an informative set of difficult failures and estimating the prevalence of failure are distinct jobs.

An image can recur without making the same claim

Researchers studying visual propaganda encounter a version of the problem before there is a recommendation to review. Lutz et al. (2026) followed anti-immigrant imagery on TikTok and X over fifty weeks in 2024. The team examined roughly 1,500 items qualitatively and about 50,000 computationally. Initial coding tracked subjects, actions and backgrounds alongside what the posts claimed, while computation made it possible to look for repeated and remixed images in the larger set. Bringing related images together gave analysts material to compare; it did not make them interchangeable. A post can use an image to support a claim and another can reuse it to challenge the claim. An automated description of the shared image could be accurate for both and still miss why the two posts matter.

A comparison for a reviewer could therefore begin with two versions located through their shared imagery, then show separately what a crop, visible caption or soundtrack adds. An audit could record which details viewers can locate, what claim they think the post makes, and what outside evidence they would need to establish that claim. In the original research, analysts also had to manage exposure to disturbing imagery. Making the exact source interval easier to inspect can reduce unnecessary exposure; it is also a way to avoid asking someone to judge a claim from a caption alone. The important saving is a shorter route to the relevant evidence, not a reason to show only the version the system already thinks is representative.

Design the review around the exception

Return to the consultants’ spreadsheet and interviews. A test of a better research assistant might give three groups the same case: a person solving it alone, a model answering alone, and a person reviewing the model. In one assisted version, the reader sees the brand recommendation first. In another, the interface brings forward the interview passage that bears on the spreadsheet before showing the recommendation. Some cases contain evidence that reverses the apparent choice; in others the same kind of interview passage confirms it. The result to record is not whether the reviewer quotes a passage, opens a source or praises an explanation. It is whether the reviewer changes a recommendation when the exception applies and keeps it when it does not.

There is a further test after the model is gone. Give a reviewer another case in which a table and an interview point in different directions. If the person now recognizes which assumption needs checking, the interface may have helped them learn something about the task. If their decisions were better only while the source passage was selected for them, the system may still be a useful review tool. Those are different accomplishments. Both depend on making it possible to do something when the evidence and the answer come apart.
