1.2.1
Drift, Drude model and mobility
What exactly passes through the cross-section?
Take the coordinate x to increase to the right and call a rightward transfer positive. Give the
crossing plane an area A. During a short time ∆t, record ∆N+ particles crossing right and ∆N−
crossing left. The net count is ∆N+ −∆N−. Dividing it by area and time defines the signed
particle flux:

Here Γ (capital Greek gamma) counts moving particles; it is not the symmetry point Γ on a band
diagram. The units distinguish them. Subscripts n and p will identify electron and hole fluxes. In
our first sample, the average crossing rates are equal, so Γ = 0 even though neither rate vanishes.
The ammeter measures charge transfer. If each counted particle carries charge q, the net charge
crossing per area and time is q times the particle count. This is the current density:
J = qΓ ,
[J] = C m−2s−1 = A/m2.
The current I is charge crossing the entire area per time. If J is uniform across that area, I = JA.
A wider sample can therefore carry more current while having the same local current density.

Change the carriers while keeping the geometry fixed
Next keep the geometry and field
fixed, but change the carriers. In an electron-dominated sample, σ ≃enµn = ne2τn/m∗
n. We can
raise the current by increasing n, so more carriers take part, or by increasing µn, so their mean
response becomes larger. A small population of very mobile carriers can still conduct less than a
much larger population with modest mobility. Metals illustrate the importance of the population
factor.
There are also two ways to change µ = eτ/m∗. At fixed τ, a smaller transport mass gives a larger
acceleration. At fixed mass, a longer relaxation time allows a larger directed bias to build up.
Changing material can change both quantities; a mass comparison alone is therefore incomplete.
The DOS mass used to count available states need not equal the transport mass appropriate to
this acceleration.
Try to improve the same sample by doping it
Doping seems an obvious way to raise the
carrier count. But ionised dopants also scatter carriers through their electric potentials. More
dopants may raise n while shortening τ and reducing µ. Lattice vibrations, or phonons, provide
another scattering mechanism.
Where phonons dominate, stronger thermal vibrations tend to
reduce mobility; where impurity scattering dominates, the temperature trend can differ.
The
conductivity formula helps us identify the competing factors, but does not justify extending one
scattering regime to every temperature.
Before using enµ, check the density conversion as well as mobility:
1 cm−3 = 106 m−3,

For positive densities and mobilities, σ > 0. A negative electron charge therefore does not make
the conductivity negative: its negative drift direction cancels its negative charge.
