1.2 Transport phenomena

We can now use a band diagram to identify available energies, combine the density of states with the occupation function, and find how many electrons and holes a sample contains. Put that sample between two contacts. Have we already learned enough to predict a current?

Begin with a uniform sample at a common temperature, with no applied voltage or other source driving it out of equilibrium. Its mobile carriers have nonzero velocities, yet an ammeter reads zero. To understand that result, imagine watching an internal cross-section. Electrons cross it in both directions. Count both streams before deciding what the meter should show.

The arrows represent opposing streams, not an electron sitting still. Increasing both streams equally would make the microscopic motion busier without changing the meter reading. Applying a voltage, however, can make one stream slightly larger than the other. Our calculation must retain that difference.

We will change one feature of this sample at a time. First apply a voltage while keeping its carrier concentration uniform. Then remove the field and make one side more crowded. Finally, use a spatial band diagram to examine a sample in which both effects occur together.

1.2.1 Drift, Drude model and mobility

Here Γ (capital Greek gamma) counts moving particles; it is not the symmetry point Γ on a band diagram. The units distinguish them. Subscripts n and p will identify electron and hole fluxes. In our first sample, the average crossing rates are equal, so Γ = 0 even though neither rate vanishes.

The ammeter measures charge transfer. If each counted particle carries charge q, the net charge crossing per area and time is q times the particle count. This is the current density:

The current I is charge crossing the entire area per time. If J is uniform across that area, I = JA. A wider sample can therefore carry more current while having the same local current density.

Use the carrier count we already know We already know the number of carriers per volume from the occupation calculation. How does that help with a crossing count? Denote the electron number density by n, measured in m−3. First isolate a group moving right at speed v. During ∆t, only particles within v∆t of the plane can cross. Follow what happens when the bias is removed Imagine removing the field after a small leftward mean has developed. Momentum scattering weakens that directional bias, even while the carriers continue moving. The time over which the directed mean relaxes is the momentum-relaxation time τn. In the simplest isotropic collision model, it is a mean time between effective momentum-randomising events. In general it is not the interval between every possible collision, and a collision need not stop a carrier or remove all its kinetic energy.

Change the carriers while keeping the geometry fixed Next keep the geometry and field fixed, but change the carriers. In an electron-dominated sample, . We can raise the current by increasing n, so more carriers take part, or by increasing µn, so their mean response becomes larger. A small population of very mobile carriers can still conduct less than a much larger population with modest mobility. Metals illustrate the importance of the population factor.

There are also two ways to change µ = eτ/m∗. At fixed τ, a smaller transport mass gives a larger acceleration. At fixed mass, a longer relaxation time allows a larger directed bias to build up. Changing material can change both quantities; a mass comparison alone is therefore incomplete. The DOS mass used to count available states need not equal the transport mass appropriate to this acceleration.

Try to improve the same sample by doping it Doping seems an obvious way to raise the carrier count. But ionised dopants also scatter carriers through their electric potentials. More dopants may raise n while shortening τ and reducing µ. Lattice vibrations, or phonons, provide another scattering mechanism. Where phonons dominate, stronger thermal vibrations tend to reduce mobility; where impurity scattering dominates, the temperature trend can differ. The conductivity formula helps us identify the competing factors, but does not justify extending one scattering regime to every temperature.

For positive densities and mobilities, σ > 0. A negative electron charge therefore does not make the conductivity negative: its negative drift direction cancels its negative charge.
