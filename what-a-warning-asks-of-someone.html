The warning at the door

A small timing exercise: the same information can arrive before or after a person has the option it was meant to protect.

Imagine a person heading to a station with a folding bicycle. The lift is out. Three versions of the notice state the same fact:

• On the route planner before departure, it might change their choice of station.

• At the station entrance, it might leave them time to turn back or ask about another route.

• At the bottom of the stairs, it might only explain why the lift doors did not open.

Imagine changing the screen at the bottom of the stairs to make the notice twice as large. The sentence gets easier to read. The passenger still has to get back to the entrance.

Three clocks

A warning has at least three relevant times: when someone can know about the problem, when the intended audience gets the message, and when the audience’s options narrow. McBride and colleagues measured one of those intervals for wireless earthquake alerts in controlled and community tests. Median delivery times fell between six and twelve seconds across the tests. The measurement was for messages sent through the alert system, not the total time a person gained before shaking or what the person did next.

The station example has a fourth time: when a usable alternative becomes available. If there is no accessible route, moving the notice to the top of the stairs gives the passenger time to make a different plan but cannot repair the missing route. A trial could record whether people notice the restriction, can locate another option, and actually change course. For a different way to test what readers can use, see how much a reader learns from a message.
