A diagram makes an argument

The helpful picture is the one that lets its reader find the reason, even when the reason goes against the recommendation.

A volunteer is looking at a diagram of a railway station. Two routes connect the street entrance to the same platform. The thinner route crosses a narrow footbridge; the thicker one runs through an underpass. The drawing puts the properties that matter in each traveler’s path: a passenger with luggage might first look for the lift, while someone trying to catch a train might first look for the shorter walk. If an outdated map fails to mark a lift as closed, the best-looking route can be the one no one can take.

Same facts, different work

Larkin and Simon (1987) drew a distinction between the information a representation contains and the work it takes to use it. A list and a diagram can describe the same arrangement. The list might force a reader to move between entries before finding the two that share a location. A diagram can group those entries on the page, at the cost of making some other relationship less explicit. The advantage is not the presence of a picture. It lies in putting the constraints for an inference where the reader can use them together.

The distinction has a practical test. In an experiment by Vasconcelos et al. (2023), people decided whether to accept an assistant’s proposed exit from a maze. One display showed the assistant’s route laid onto the maze; the other described the turns in words. Participants using the overlay were less likely to accept an incorrect exit on the harder two levels of maze. The drawing made it easier to follow the recommendation against the available paths. A better-looking arrow would have accomplished something else: making the recommendation more conspicuous without making it easier to check.

What is the diagram hiding?

For an accessible-route aid, a useful view would give the traveler enough detail to challenge the computation: the marked lift, the point where the underpass branches, and when each closure was last checked. Those three items belong at different scales. The lift should be on the plan, the facility’s status needs a clear timestamp, and the calculation needs to show which branch it used. A designer who puts all three on the same static map may create a legibility problem; omitting the last two can create a more serious illusion of completeness.

The same trap appears when a record looks more conclusive than the claim it supports. A picture can make a relation immediately visible without supplying its missing premise. A route overlay is therefore one particular answer to whether an explanation helps a person revise a verdict. That is why a good diagram does not merely get a reader to the recommended route. It gives the reader a place to disagree with it.
