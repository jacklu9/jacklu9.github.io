How many seconds a warning gives back

In July 2019, two earthquakes near Ridgecrest, California, registered on the ShakeAlert network. The public app then serving Los Angeles County sent no notification for either event. As far as an app log was concerned, the two cases ended the same way. Seismologists looking back at the observations found a different picture. For the magnitude 6.4 event, the observed shaking in the county did not warrant a public alert at the app’s threshold. For the magnitude 7.1 event it did. The system had underestimated the larger event’s magnitude, which contributed to its predicting weaker shaking in the county than was observed (Chung et al., 2020).

Detection had worked in one sense: the network registered both events. A warning has a different job. It has to identify a condition that justifies a signal for somebody in a particular place, get the signal to them, and leave enough time for a response. Those three jobs are easiest to see when a notification never appears. They remain separate when the notification appears exactly as designed.

The time between the server and the phone

An earthquake early-warning system is responding to a rupture already in progress. It tries to use available observations to estimate where strong shaking will reach next. Minson et al. (2018) modeled how early the growing rupture could support a warning for different shaking levels. Raising the level a system waits to predict can leave little lead time where shaking will be strongest. Lowering the threshold allows earlier issuance but brings in events with weaker shaking, for which a particular precaution may not be warranted. The person running the system faces this trade-off before any message has been sent.

A separate experiment measured how much time sending it can take. In a test of Wireless Emergency Alerts in San Diego County, McBride et al. (2023) obtained 1,401 reports with reliable receipt times within the first two minutes. The median delay from initiation to receipt among those reports was eleven seconds; the median absolute deviation was three seconds. Instrumented phones provided a separate controlled test of receipt. This was a test of a delivery channel rather than a reconstruction of Ridgecrest or a measure of anyone’s reaction time. Its practical significance is clearest for an estimate issued only seconds before local shaking. An eleven-second delay might preserve a useful interval at one location and consume the entire interval at another. A network-wide average would hide the difference.

An alarm in a room where someone is asleep

Even proof that a signal reached the device can be one step short. In an inpatient study of an early glucose monitor, only 53 of the 180 individual alarms issued during sleep were followed by the young participant waking. Combining nearby signals into episodes gave another answer: the participants awoke during 29 of 44 alarm events (Buckingham et al., 2005). The first number describes an individual signal; the second credits any eventual awakening in the episode. Neither describes immediate action. An evaluation that switches between those units can make an alarm appear more or less effective without changing what the people in the room experienced.

The alert intended to wake someone is not always the only alert asking for attention. Cobry et al. (2024) analyzed logs from 76 young people starting a first-generation hybrid closed-loop glucose system. The average number of overnight alarms rose from 3.3 at baseline in nonautomated mode to 5.4 during the first two weeks of automated use. Those totals included messages about the pump, maintenance and automated operation as well as glucose warnings. Counts fell later while sensor and automated use also declined. The study does not tell which current notification is likely to wake a particular person. It illustrates what else a correct warning competes with over a night and why alarms per event and interruptions per night describe different experiences.

Some warnings change the thing they predict

A model that predicts low glucose can be evaluated without anyone hearing its warning. Fleischer et al. (2022) trained a model to forecast up to forty minutes ahead and tested it on held-out people’s recorded sensor data. The study counted episodes with sensor readings below 70 mg/dL for at least fifteen minutes. The method identified 90 percent of 3,725 such episodes. Its would-be alerts preceded the first low reading of a detected episode by 17.5 minutes on average, and roughly two-thirds of would-be alerts were followed by a qualifying episode. The recorded trajectory did not change in response to those alerts. In addition, a result on one set of recordings cannot promise the same timing elsewhere. In a comparison of eight forecasting methods across three datasets, Lu et al. (2026) found that errors differed across datasets even when training and testing within a dataset used different sets of participants. Reproducing a predictor and knowing what it will encounter in service are separate matters.

A trial at home can measure an actual response instead of acting out one in replay. Davey et al. (2010) compared two three-day periods for fourteen people with type 1 diabetes. In one period they had a live glucose display and threshold alarm; in the other the device recorded without feedback. Sensor-wear time at or below 65 mg/dL averaged 0.8 percent with the display and alarm against 2.2 percent without feedback. The package changed what information people could use; the experiment did not vary its display and alarm separately. Maahs et al. (2014) tested a different assignment of responsibility. Each night the system either could or could not suspend insulin delivery in response to a prediction, with the audible alarm setting held the same. A sensor reading at or below 60 mg/dL occurred on 21 percent of enabled nights and 33 percent of disabled nights. Morning blood glucose above 180 mg/dL was more frequent after enabled nights, 27 versus 21 percent. Recording both outcomes shows what can be missed when an intervention is judged against only the event it was built to prevent.

After a protective intervention, no low reading can mean that the forecast was mistaken or that the intervention worked. Shaking, in contrast, would arrive at a location whether or not anyone acted on the alert. The same apparent observation, a signal followed by no recorded event, cannot be interpreted the same way in the two cases. When action can alter the reference outcome, an evaluation must compare outcomes with and without the intervention rather than label every unfulfilled forecast a false alarm.

Start the clock where the decision begins

A useful record keeps the time of the first qualifying forecast, the time of issue, and the time of delivery or perception separate. It also specifies when the opportunity for a particular response ends in the location or situation being studied. It distinguishes one late alert from a series of repeated ones, so that ten successful transmissions do not look like ten people newly warned. Where the person or device is able to act, the record then follows the response and its consequences.

The practical question is how much of an estimated lead time a design gives back to the person who needs it. More sensitive detection may not help if a notification route is slow. A faster route may not help if the estimate never qualifies for delivery. A quiet device may be getting everything right, or may have left people without notice. Ridgecrest made that last ambiguity visible because there were two silences and two different reasons for them.
