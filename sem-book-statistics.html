Make the reference piece reproduce equal populations

Return first to the undoped reference. We know that its electron and hole concentrations must be equal, but the host need not offer identical conduction and valence state counts: Nc and Nv can differ. Placing the filling level exactly halfway between the band edges would not generally compensate for that difference. We must locate the level that actually produces equal areas.

Call that intrinsic filling level Ei, defined for the same host and temperature. Substituting EF = Ei and imposing n = p = ni in the two concentration expressions gives

The first term is the midgap energy. The second is precisely the adjustment needed when the two bands offer unequal effective state counts; its logarithm uses the dimensionless ratio Nv/Nc. If Nc = Nv, the adjustment vanishes and Ei lies at midgap. Otherwise the level moves until the electron and hole areas match. This is why our intrinsic reference was defined by equal populations, not by drawing a line at the geometric midpoint. Midgap remains a useful approximation when an exercise explicitly adopts it.

The cancellation requires thermal equilibrium with a common Fermi level and the nondegenerate filling approximation in the chosen band model. Outside thermal equilibrium, electron and hole populations need not satisfy this mass-action relation.

If we heat all three pieces, this common reference changes. The exponential penalty for spanning the gap becomes less severe and the effective state counts increase, causing a strong rise in ni. The gap and masses can themselves depend on temperature and add material-specific changes. Thus the earlier observation that a doped piece can approach intrinsic behaviour at high temperature is consistent with the state-count calculation: the reference population can eventually overwhelm the fixed net doping.

The same band diagram has now supplied an energy difference, the state count has supplied available places, and equilibrium filling has turned them into a carrier population. We can explain why our three otherwise similar silicon pieces contain different numbers of electrons and holes, and can recover those numbers either from impurity balance or from a consistent filling level.

Those counts still do not tell us the current through a wire made from any of the pieces. Carriers moving in opposite directions can cancel even when many are present. To continue the story, we must follow the directions of motion and ask what changes when a field or a concentration gradient makes the crossings unequal.
