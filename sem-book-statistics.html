The second common factor, Nv, is the effective valence-band density of states, also a concentration.
A larger thermal window includes more available states, which is why both coeﬀicients scale as
T 3/2 when their effective masses are held fixed. Additional valleys or valence bands modify the
coeﬀicients through their counts. We have evaluated the single-band model explicitly; those refine-
ments must be included when its scalar parameters are used for a real material. The Boltzmann
filling used to obtain both formulas still requires the nondegenerate regime.
Make the reference piece reproduce equal populations

be equal, but the host need not offer identical conduction and valence state counts: Nc and Nv

compensate for that difference. We must locate the level that actually produces equal areas.

If we heat all three pieces, this common reference changes. The exponential penalty for spanning
the gap becomes less severe and the effective state counts increase, causing a strong rise in ni. The
gap and masses can themselves depend on temperature and add material-specific changes. Thus
the earlier observation that a doped piece can approach intrinsic behaviour at high temperature
is consistent with the state-count calculation: the reference population can eventually overwhelm
the fixed net doping.

The same band diagram has now supplied an energy difference, the state count has supplied
available places, and equilibrium filling has turned them into a carrier population. We can explain
why our three otherwise similar silicon pieces contain different numbers of electrons and holes, and
can recover those numbers either from impurity balance or from a consistent filling level.
Those counts still do not tell us the current through a wire made from any of the pieces. Carriers
moving in opposite directions can cancel even when many are present. To continue the story, we
must follow the directions of motion and ask what changes when a field or a concentration gradient
makes the crossings unequal.
