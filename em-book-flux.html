1.1.3
Flux of a vector ﬁeld
A direction at one point was enough to ask about slope. For the space between the plates, we will
also need to ask what a ﬁeld does across an entire surface. Merely adding arrow lengths would
lose the diﬀerence between crossing the surface and running along it.
A surface asks what crosses it, not what runs along it.
Imagine a small ﬂat window in a
ﬂow. If the ﬂow is perpendicular to the window, it crosses eﬀectively. Tilting the window reduces
its presented area. If the ﬂow is parallel to the window, it passes beside it without crossing. This
picture motivates ﬂux: a signed measure obtained by adding the normal component of a ﬁeld over
a surface.
The surface is usually an imaginary measuring surface, not a physical wall. A normal is perpen-
dicular to its local tangent plane. On a curved surface the normal generally changes from point to
point, so we must ﬁrst work with small patches that are approximately ﬂat.

Flux has units “ﬁeld units times area”. For a velocity ﬁeld it has units m3/s, a volume ﬂow rate.
For electric displacement D it has units of coulombs. Thus the ﬂow picture explains the geometry,
but ﬂux does not always mean a material transport rate. Reversing the normal reverses the sign
of the same surface’s ﬂux.

edge and encloses a volume, such as the entire surface of a sphere. For a closed surface we write

and exit give zero imbalance, even when the ﬁeld is nonzero everywhere in the box.

Here ∆V is a small volume and ∂(∆V ) its closed boundary, with outward normals. The region

A source-like pattern does not automatically mean that a physical substance is being created.
The interpretation depends on the ﬁeld. Divergence of an electric displacement ﬁeld is related to
free charge; divergence of a velocity ﬁeld describes local expansion or compression of a moving
material. Neither is a measurement of ﬁeld magnitude. Also, visibly outward-pointing arrows do
not by themselves establish positive divergence: an inverse-square radial ﬁeld has balanced ﬂux
through every small region away from its singular source.

1.1.5
Gauss’s divergence theorem
Local imbalances add up to the outer-boundary imbalance.
Divide a volume into many
small neighbouring boxes. On a face shared by two boxes there is only one ﬁeld value, but there
are two outward normals: what leaves one box enters the other. Consequently the two signed
contributions cancel when the boxes’ ﬂuxes are added. No cancellation occurs on an outer face,
because it has no neighbouring box outside the chosen volume.
