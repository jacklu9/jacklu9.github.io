1.1.3 Flux of a vector field

A direction at one point was enough to ask about slope. For the space between the plates, we will also need to ask what a field does across an entire surface. Merely adding arrow lengths would lose the difference between crossing the surface and running along it.

A surface asks what crosses it, not what runs along it. Imagine a small flat window in a flow. If the flow is perpendicular to the window, it crosses effectively. Tilting the window reduces its presented area. If the flow is parallel to the window, it passes beside it without crossing. This picture motivates flux: a signed measure obtained by adding the normal component of a field over a surface.

The surface is usually an imaginary measuring surface, not a physical wall. A normal is perpendicular to its local tangent plane. On a curved surface the normal generally changes from point to point, so we must first work with small patches that are approximately flat.

A double integral adds contributions from a two-dimensional surface, which can lie in three-dimensional space. The dot product makes outward or chosen-side crossing positive, opposite-side crossing negative, and tangential motion zero. For a constant field through a flat area A, the integrand is constant and ΦS = |F |A cos θ, where θ is the angle between F and ˆn, not the angle to the surface itself.

Flux has units “field units times area”. For a velocity field it has units m3/s, a volume flow rate. For electric displacement D it has units of coulombs. Thus the flow picture explains the geometry, but flux does not always mean a material transport rate. Reversing the normal reverses the sign of the same surface’s flux.

An open surface has a boundary curve, such as a disk with a circular rim. A closed surface has no edge and encloses a volume, such as the entire surface of a sphere. For a closed surface we write

∂V: V denotes its interior and ∂V its boundary. Its normal is chosen outwards. The result is net outward flux, so a strong field can enter one side and leave another while the net result is zero.

1.1.4 Divergence of a vector field

Does a tiny closed region have an outward imbalance? Place a small imaginary closed box around a point. Add the signed crossing of all its faces. If more leaves than enters, the field has a positive outward imbalance there. If more enters, it has a negative imbalance. Equal entry and exit give zero imbalance, even when the field is nonzero everywhere in the box.

Net flux alone depends on the size of the measuring region. Divide it by the region’s volume and shrink the region around the point. The limiting outward flux per unit volume defines divergence:

Here ∆V is a small volume and ∂(∆V ) its closed boundary, with outward normals. The region shrinks in all directions to the point r, where the field is differentiable. Divergence is a scalar because net outward crossing has a sign but no remaining direction. Its units are field units per metre: flux has field units times area, then we divide by volume.

A source-like pattern does not automatically mean that a physical substance is being created. The interpretation depends on the field. Divergence of an electric displacement field is related to free charge; divergence of a velocity field describes local expansion or compression of a moving material. Neither is a measurement of field magnitude. Also, visibly outward-pointing arrows do not by themselves establish positive divergence: an inverse-square radial field has balanced flux through every small region away from its singular source.

1.1.5 Gauss’s divergence theorem

Local imbalances add up to the outer-boundary imbalance. Divide a volume into many small neighbouring boxes. On a face shared by two boxes there is only one field value, but there are two outward normals: what leaves one box enters the other. Consequently the two signed contributions cancel when the boxes’ fluxes are added. No cancellation occurs on an outer face, because it has no neighbouring box outside the chosen volume.
