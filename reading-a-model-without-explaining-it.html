Reading a model without explaining it

A pattern can be recoverable from a representation without being the thing that makes the system act.

An analysis compares two ways of placing short films on an emotional scale. One comes from a direction extracted from language-model activations when the models process stories. The other uses EEG recordings from viewers watching the films. Radwan et al. (2026) averaged EEG within each film and reported held-out-film correlations of .80 with the Qwen3-4B-derived direction and .87 with a CLIP-text variant. At the level of individual viewer–film observations, the reported correlations were .17 to .21. Changing the observation from a film to an individual response changes the question. A shared ordering across stimuli is not an estimate of how accurately a system can tell what a particular viewer felt. The mismatch is particularly consequential when a plausible simulation becomes the basis for a number someone will use.

The same preprint reported 25 variants of an EEG classifier trained to align with the direction. None improved balanced accuracy over its respective baseline in those experiments; sixteen significantly worsened it. So a representation can bring a relationship into view without improving the task selected for the next system. That distinction makes the decision to treat a signal as expressive material separate from the decision to use it as evidence for a specific classification.

What a readout reads

Belrose et al. (2025) trained small mappings from different hidden layers of a frozen transformer to predict the final next-token distribution. Their tuned lens estimates how that distribution develops across the computation. The target of the trained mapping is the model’s final distribution, not the accuracy of its answer. A readout can therefore be faithful to behavior that is itself wrong. It can also show that a later result is recoverable from an intermediate state without establishing that the model needs that information there to produce its result.

Ghandeharioun et al. (2024) transplanted a state from a source prompt into a target prompt that supplied examples of descriptions. The resulting description came from the state placed into that prompt, the examples in the target prompt and the rest of the model’s computation. A recognizable description shows that this arrangement can extract something useful; it would not justify saying that the description already existed in its finished form at the source. A comparison with the same target prompt receiving another state helps identify which part of the result the source made available.

What an intervention shows

Meng et al. (2022) used a different procedure for tracing facts. They disrupted the representation of the subject in a prompt, then selectively restored hidden activations from an uncorrupted run. Restoring some states recovered the original factual answer. The contrast shows which restored information made a difference under that particular disruption. Editing a weight to change the model’s subsequent behavior is a different experiment. Hase et al. (2023) compared tracing results with the success of edits and found that, for ordinary factual replacement in their experiments, tracing did not identify the best layer to edit. In one variant where they observed a relationship, layer identity remained a stronger predictor.

These distinctions matter outside a model's hidden layers. A tool may retrieve a group’s last decision, explain why it was made and nonetheless operate on an earlier version of the file. Tests could keep the project history constant while changing which file was authorized, then compare the system’s answer about intent with the result of the requested operation. Finding a stored decision is a useful intermediate observation. Demonstrating that it governs the next action requires observing the next action. That is also why recovering the relevant fact and explaining how it should shape an exchange are separable goals.
