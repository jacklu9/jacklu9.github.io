A suggestion the other person can decline

Why practical conversation uses hints, and what a messaging assistant can get wrong by making them more explicit.

A ceramics instructor walks past a student setting out three bowls for a show.

“The smaller bowl would fit on the front shelf.”

It could be a useful observation about the space. It could be a suggestion the student is free to ignore. With a different tone or a relationship in which the instructor decides the display, it could sound like a correction. A bystander could hear every word and still miss what the student needs to know: whether to move the bowl now.

Being understood without settling the matter

An indirect request can work precisely because it stops short of committing both people to one interpretation. The student can acknowledge the observation and carry on, leaving room for the instructor to make a preferred arrangement explicit. Or the student can try the small bowl temporarily and invite a comparison. In either case the remark has done more than convey dimensions, without yet selecting a permanent arrangement.

James Lee and Steven Pinker studied some of the consequences of indirectness through scripted encounters. Participants read scenarios involving socially delicate requests, saw formulations that varied in directness, and judged aspects of how the people in them would interpret and respond. The researchers used those judgments to distinguish being able to infer an intention from having an openly acknowledged request that could change the relationship. That distinction also fits the bowl example, although a ceramics shelf is not one of their study settings. An assistant can reasonably predict that someone has a suggestion and still need evidence before presenting it as a decision.

Which history belongs in this turn?

Susan Brennan and Herbert Clark studied pairs who repeatedly described objects to each other. A description established during one exchange could persist after it ceased to be the cheapest way to identify the object. The pair had a history behind the term; someone else might have used different words. This makes ordinary conversation less self-contained than a transcript looks. In a different study, Michael Schober and Herbert Clark gave addressees and overhearers the same background information and had both arrange complex figures. Addressees did better even though overhearers heard the conversation. The addressee could take part in establishing whether a description was understood.

The same issue arises when an assistant tries to complete a message. Stephanie Valencia and colleagues asked twelve users of augmentative and alternative communication to try generated suggestions, including expansions of short replies. Participants saw ways to save effort, but also wanted suggestions they could recognize as fitting how they communicated. In the shelf example, expanding “it might fit” to “put it there” changes the role assigned to the recipient. Changing the style back afterwards would not restore that role. There is a separate question about whether a detail is relevant at all, explored in when memory should stay quiet.

Three outputs from the same line

A transcript can preserve the sentence as spoken. A note for the team can record the front shelf as an option and say whether anyone chose it. An interface that moves the bowl in a shared plan needs a basis for making that change, such as an explicit selection. Each output claims a different relation between the speaker, the recipient, and the proposed action.

A useful trial would keep the words constant and change only what happens next. In one version the student says they want to compare both positions; in another the instructor asks them to move the bowl; in a third the student later says the front shelf is unavailable. Score whether an assistant preserves the original suggestion, the later decision, and the current constraint as distinct facts. When only the referent is unclear, a small repair can settle it. Asking what was meant and asking what is authorized are different questions, and an assistant can answer the first without answering the second.
