1.1 Basics of electronic band structures

One picture, two different energy questions

Imagine that this diagram is all you have been given about a semiconductor. You want light to lift an electron from the lower group of curves into the upper one. How much energy must the light supply? The red arrow looks like a useful place to start. Yet the smallest gap in this drawing and the length of that arrow are different.

Keep three questions beside the picture: what does a point on a curve represent, why does the horizontal axis change from ⟨100⟩ to ⟨111⟩, and which two heights should be subtracted? We will follow the drawing until each question has a physical answer.

The diagram is a schematic exercise, not an identification of a particular material. The curves, their labels and the energy table will remain our common reference. We will later introduce silicon and GaAs examples explicitly when their material properties are needed.

Read the point on the drawing, one choice at a time

The vertical axis gives electron energy, E. The horizontal coordinate is an address for now; we will uncover its physical meaning below. The two annotated copies perform the reading instead of asking you to imagine it.

Two readings of the original curves. Colour selects the address, band and height. The marked point is an allowed energy, not an electron at a physical location. X and L label vertical slices through every band.

The four colours name four bands, each a family of allowed electronic energies. Band (1) is black, Band (2) blue, Band (3) orange dashed and Band (4) teal dotted. An upper band number in is a label, not a power.

Turn a positive table entry into a signed height

The horizontal line through the blue maximum at Γ (“Gamma”) is our energy zero. The exercise lists positive distances from it. Watch the lower X level change from a positive depth in the table to a negative height on the plot:

The enlarged curves show the same two selected levels; only their stated energies are quantitative. ∆ means a difference. The arrow spans both the height above zero and the depth below it.

The more explicit notation E2(X) puts the band label below E and the address in parentheses. It refers to the same plotted level as the table entry , but here we supply its signed energy. The sign convention, not the typography alone, determines whether an entry is positive or negative.

The unit is the electronvolt (eV). A volt is energy per charge, 1 V = 1 J/C. Multiplying a one-volt potential difference by the magnitude of one electron’s charge converts it to an energy scale:

e: one elementary charge 1 V

This small unit fits electronic energy differences. It is not another name for a volt.

Move one endpoint and watch the answer change

Both comparisons below use the opening drawing. In A, select two states at X. In B, search the entire drawing for the lowest upper-band state and highest lower-band state. The coloured dots show exactly which states change.

Same curves, different comparisons. In B the ruler compares heights; it is not a proposed transition at the ruler’s horizontal position. No diagonal electron path is implied.

The upper group is the conduction band; the lower group contains the valence bands. In the ideal undoped crystal at zero temperature, the latter are filled and the former empty. These are statements about occupation, additional to the allowed curves.

Write Ec for the lowest conduction energy and Ev for the highest valence energy. The labels c and v mean conduction and valence. The fundamental band gap is

Here the black valleys at X, Γ and L have energies 1.04, 1.92 and 1.22 eV. Comparing all three selects Ec = 1.04 eV. The blue maximum gives Ev = 0. A valley is a neighbourhood of a minimum on an energy curve. A local minimum is lowest among nearby states; the global minimum is lowest over the full band.

The two gap-defining dots need not share a horizontal address. That is the unresolved question for light: may an optical excitation change this address, or must it keep the endpoints on one vertical line?

Relabelling the zero changes both energies by the same constant. The arrow length remains (E1 + C) − (E2 + C) = E1 − E2. This reference line does not specify which states are occupied.

Why a crystal offers bands of possible states

A state is one allowed quantum description of an electron: its band, spatial wave pattern and spin label. Spin has two possible projections; it is an intrinsic property, not a tiny classical orbit. An available state need not be occupied:

Each marker represents a selected complete state, with its spin label understood. A curve gives allowed energies; it is neither an electron trajectory nor an occupation count.

An isolated atom provides a simpler starting point. Its allowed electron energies are discrete. The spatial part of an atomic state is an orbital, described by a wavefunction; the wavefunction’s squared magnitude gives a probability density for finding the electron. The labels s and p name

different orbital shapes and angular behaviour. In 3s and 3p, the number 3 is an atomic shell label. Valence electrons are the outer electrons that participate in bonding; the tightly bound inner electrons are core electrons.

When neighbouring atomic wavefunctions overlap, their amplitudes combine. The two sketches show how the relative phase changes the probability between the atoms. The bonding combination is normally lower in energy than its antibonding partner.

Schematic amplitudes along the line joining two atoms; the plotted scale is arbitrary. Probability density is |ψ|2. The sign of ψ records phase, not positive or negative electric charge.

With many interacting atoms, each atomic contribution generates many distinct allowed combinations. Their energies crowd into dense groups: the bands in the next picture.

The horizontal progression in this small sketch is from one atom to many interacting atoms, not the horizontal axis of our opening figure. It explains why a solid has groups of allowed energies. The opening figure goes further: it separates those allowed energies according to the electron’s wave pattern.
