Electromagnetic Theory · Chapter 1

Phasors and what can jump at an interface

Keep frequency and phase explicit, then use a pillbox and a shrinking loop to see which field components can change at a material boundary.

A single frequency can drive two contributions to current in the same material. An ohmic contribution follows the electric field. A contribution from changing electric displacement follows its slope: it is strongest near a zero crossing, not at the field's largest value. Phasors keep those contributions separate without making an analyst differentiate a new trigonometric expression for every field component. At a material boundary, the same bookkeeping preserves another distinction: a matched normal component of displacement need not imply a matched normal electric field.

One convention, one frequency

Adopt the convention E(t)=Re⁡{E~ejωt}\mathbf E(t)=\operatorname{Re}\{\widetilde{\mathbf E}e^{j\omega t}\}, with j2=−1j^2=-1. Here the complex vector E~\widetilde{\mathbf E} carries peak magnitude and phase. A component E0∠ϕE_0\angle\phi represents E0cos⁡(ωt+ϕ)E_0\cos(\omega t+\phi), not the RMS amplitude. Taking a derivative of the common exponential gives

∂∂tRe⁡{E~ejωt}=Re⁡{jωE~ejωt}.\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\operatorname{Re}\{\widetilde{\mathbf E}e^{j\omega t}\} =\operatorname{Re}\{j\omega\widetilde{\mathbf E}e^{j\omega t}\}.

That is why ∂/∂t\partial/\partial t becomes jωj\omega for this frequency. Curl and divergence still act on space, so they retain their form. For example, the two curl laws become

∇×E~=−jωB~,∇×H~=J~f+jωD~.\nabla\times\widetilde{\mathbf E}=-j\omega\widetilde{\mathbf B},\qquad \nabla\times\widetilde{\mathbf H}=\widetilde{\mathbf J}_f+j\omega\widetilde{\mathbf D}.

Choosing e−jωte^{-j\omega t} instead reverses the derivative signs. Expressions written with the two conventions cannot be combined unchanged. Superposition can handle several different frequencies in a linear system, but each frequency needs its own phasor and the real fields are added at the end.

Worked example: two equal current amplitudes do not sum to twice either one

In a homogeneous, linear material, take D~=ϵE~\widetilde{\mathbf D}=\epsilon\widetilde{\mathbf E} and J~f=σE~\widetilde{\mathbf J}_f=\sigma\widetilde{\mathbf E} with real ϵ\epsilon and σ\sigma. In this model all dissipation enters through σ\sigma. The phasor of the total current density in the magnetic curl equation is then

J~total=(σ+jωϵ)E~.\widetilde{\mathbf J}_{\rm total}=(\sigma+j\omega\epsilon)\widetilde{\mathbf E}.

At f=1.00 kHzf=1.00\,\mathrm{kHz}, choose ϵ=6ϵ0\epsilon=6\epsilon_0 and σ=3.34×10−7 S m−1\sigma=3.34\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{S\,m^{-1}}. Then ωϵ=2π(1000)(6ϵ0)≈3.34×10−7 S m−1\omega\epsilon=2\pi(1000)(6\epsilon_0)\approx3.34\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{S\,m^{-1}}. For a field of peak magnitude 120 V m−1120\,\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}} pointing along x^\hat{\mathbf x} with phase zero, each contribution has peak magnitude approximately 4.01×10−5 A m−24.01\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{A\,m^{-2}}. They are ninety degrees apart. Their phasor sum has peak magnitude 2(4.01×10−5)≈5.67×10−5 A m−2\sqrt{2}(4.01\times10^{-5})\approx5.67\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{A\,m^{-2}} and phase approximately +45∘+45^\circ. At t=0t=0, the field is at its positive peak. The displacement contribution is zero at that instant, even though its peak is just as large as the conduction peak.

The ratio ωϵ/σ\omega\epsilon/\sigma compares their sizes in this model. It is not a second conduction path: the charge-conservation lesson explains why the displacement term can exist without free charge traversing the material. If loss has already been represented by complex permittivity, check what it includes before adding a separate conductivity.

At the interface: choose the geometry that isolates the component

Let a stationary interface separate media 1 and 2, with unit normal n^\hat{\mathbf n} from 1 to 2. First straddle the interface with a thin pillbox. As its height shrinks, flux through its side vanishes for bounded fields. The outward normal is +n^+\hat{\mathbf n} on the face in medium 2 and −n^-\hat{\mathbf n} on the other face. Free volume charge in the shrinking box vanishes; free sheet charge ρ~s\widetilde{\rho}_s retains a finite charge per unit area. Dividing the integral electric Gauss law by the area gives

n^⋅(D~2−D~1)=ρ~s.\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot(\widetilde{\mathbf D}_2-\widetilde{\mathbf D}_1)=\widetilde{\rho}_s.

Likewise the normal component of B~\widetilde{\mathbf B} is continuous. To isolate a tangential electric component, use a narrow rectangular loop with parallel long edges on either side. The short edges contribute vanishingly little, and the finite magnetic flux density passes through an area tending to zero. Faraday's law leaves equal tangential electric fields. Applying the magnetic curl law to the same construction allows a sheet current K~f\widetilde{\mathbf K}_f through the vanishing loop; a bounded displacement term contributes zero in that limit. Together the conditions read

n^⋅(B~2−B~1)=0,n^×(E~2−E~1)=0,n^×(H~2−H~1)=K~f.\begin{aligned} \hat{\mathbf n}\cdot(\widetilde{\mathbf B}_2-\widetilde{\mathbf B}_1)&=0,\\ \hat{\mathbf n}\times(\widetilde{\mathbf E}_2-\widetilde{\mathbf E}_1)&=\mathbf 0,\\ \hat{\mathbf n}\times(\widetilde{\mathbf H}_2-\widetilde{\mathbf H}_1)&=\widetilde{\mathbf K}_f. \end{aligned}

These are local constraints, not enough to determine a full wave on both sides without additional geometry, excitation and radiation or boundary conditions.

Worked example: the field direction can change across a dielectric

Two isotropic dielectrics have permittivities 2ϵ02\epsilon_0 and 5ϵ05\epsilon_0, with no free sheet charge. At a point on the interface, take E~1=(30n^+40t^) V m−1\widetilde{\mathbf E}_1=(30\hat{\mathbf n}+40\hat{\mathbf t})\,\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}, where t^\hat{\mathbf t} is a unit tangent and both components share the reference phase. The tangential component in medium 2 is still 40 V m−140\,\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}. Normal displacement is continuous, not necessarily normal field. Therefore E~2n=(2/5)(30)=12 V m−1\widetilde E_{2n}=(2/5)(30)=12\,\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}. The magnitudes and directions differ: the local angle from the normal changes from arctan⁡(40/30)≈53.1∘\arctan(40/30)\approx53.1^\circ to arctan⁡(40/12)≈73.3∘\arctan(40/12)\approx73.3^\circ. These are angles of the electric field vector, not angles of propagation or ray refraction.

An interface can thus maintain the same normal displacement while changing the force per unit charge measured by E\mathbf E. The pillbox accounts for free charge; replacing D\mathbf D by ϵ0E\epsilon_0\mathbf E on both sides would erase the different material responses. If free charge is deliberately deposited on the interface, the calculation instead starts with the jump in D\mathbf D. Its sign is read with respect to the normal drawn from material 1 to material 2.

A different jump: a current sheet

The magnetic boundary law requires another sign check. Take n^=z^\hat{\mathbf n}=\hat{\mathbf z} and suppose a prescribed radio-frequency surface current has phasor K~f=0.20y^ A m−1\widetilde{\mathbf K}_f=0.20\hat{\mathbf y}\,\mathrm{A\,m^{-1}}. Imagine isolating this jump at a separate interface from the dielectric example. Because z^×x^=y^\hat{\mathbf z}\times\hat{\mathbf x}=\hat{\mathbf y}, the tangential field difference in the boundary equation is +0.20x^ A m−1+0.20\hat{\mathbf x}\,\mathrm{A\,m^{-1}}.

This determines the difference in tangential magnetic field, not the absolute field on either side. If the sheet current vanishes, tangential H~\widetilde{\mathbf H} becomes continuous; if the two media have different permeability, tangential B~\widetilde{\mathbf B} can still differ. The cleanest way to avoid interchanging H\mathbf H and B\mathbf B is to start with the law that has the source: the sheet current belongs to the circulation of H\mathbf H, whereas the zero magnetic flux condition refers to B\mathbf B.

Check your understanding
