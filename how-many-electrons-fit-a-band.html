How many electrons fit a band?

Count the available states in three dimensions, then use the Fermi level to find how many of those states contain electrons or holes.

Two wafers have the same host material and almost the same conduction and valence band edges. At the same temperature, one can have a million times as many conduction electrons as the other. A sketch of E(k)E(k) gives the electron energy for each allowed wavevector; it does not say which states are occupied. To find a carrier concentration, count the available states first, then find the fraction of them that is occupied.

Before starting, distinguish the horizontal axes. In a band diagram E(k)E(k) the horizontal coordinate is a wavevector, as in reading the extrema of a band. In a density-of-states plot the independent variable is energy. Neither is a graph of where the electron is in a crystal.

Count wavevectors in a shell

Take a large cube of side LL and volume V=L3V=L^3. Impose periodic boundary conditions as a device for counting. Each component of an allowed wavevector is an integer multiple of 2π/L2\pi/L, so one grid point takes up reciprocal-space volume (2π/L)3(2\pi/L)^3. The grid becomes very fine in a macroscopic crystal. Counting grid points in a shell can then be approximated by dividing the shell volume by the volume per point. With two spin states at each point, the number per unit sample volume in a spherical shell of radius κ\kappa and thickness dκd\kappa is

dNV=2 4πκ2 dκ(2π)3=κ2π2dκ.\frac{dN}{V}=\frac{2\,4\pi\kappa^2\,d\kappa}{(2\pi)^3}=\frac{\kappa^2}{\pi^2}d\kappa.

Here κ=∣k−k0∣\kappa=|\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k}_0| is the distance from a conduction minimum. Moving the center of that shell away from zero wavevector changes none of the counting. The shell model assumes a three-dimensional, isotropic, parabolic valley. Distinct equivalent valleys each contribute states; spin has already been counted, so it must not be included again.

Near the minimum, write ε=E−Ec=ℏ2κ2/(2me∗)\varepsilon=E-E_c=\hbar^2\kappa^2/(2m_e^*). Then dε/dκ=ℏ2κ/me∗d\varepsilon/d\kappa=\hbar^2\kappa/m_e^*. Divide the shell count per volume by its energy width:

gc(E)=1VdNdE=12π2(2me∗ℏ2)3/2 ⁣E−Ec,E≥Ec.g_c(E)=\frac{1}{V}\frac{dN}{dE}=\frac{1}{2\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m_e^*}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}\!\sqrt{E-E_c},\qquad E\ge E_c.

For an analogous valence maximum, use the positive hole mass mh∗m_h^* and replace E−EcE-E_c with Ev−EE_v-E. Each contribution vanishes on the gap side of its band edge in this model. Equal energy intervals near and far from an edge contain different numbers of states: the shell grows as κ2\kappa^2, but becomes thinner for a fixed energy increment as κ\kappa grows. This is the origin of the square root. A DOS is a concentration per energy, not a concentration. Use joules in the formula above when the masses and ℏ\hbar are in SI. If the result is expressed per electronvolt instead, change the energy unit of the DOS as well as the axis.

Availability is not occupation

For a quick unit check, imagine a narrow interval 4 meV wide near one conduction edge. Across it the DOS is almost constant at 3.0×10193.0\times 10^{19} cm−3^{-3} eV−1^{-1}. It offers approximately (0.004  eV)(3.0×1019  cm−3eV−1)=1.2×1017(0.004\;\mathrm{eV})(3.0\times 10^{19}\;\mathrm{cm}^{-3}\mathrm{eV}^{-1})=1.2\times 10^{17} states per cm3^3. If one state in a hundred is occupied, the density of electrons in that interval is only 1.2×10151.2\times10^{15} cm−3^{-3}. Narrowing the bin would lower both counts without changing the local occupation probability. Integrating over energy removes the dependence on an arbitrary bin width.

The equilibrium probability for a single electron state of energy EE to be occupied is the Fermi–Dirac function. The two possibilities, occupied and empty, have relative statistical weights exp⁡[−(E−EF)/(kBT)]\exp[-(E-E_F)/(k_BT)] and 11. Normalizing those weights gives

f(E)=11+exp⁡[(E−EF)/(kBT)].f(E)=\frac{1}{1+\exp[(E-E_F)/(k_BT)]}.

The chemical potential EFE_F is called the Fermi level. At nonzero temperature, f(EF)=1/2f(E_F)=1/2, but that does not guarantee a state at that energy. A Fermi level in a clean gap can lie where the host DOS is zero. A conduction state contributes an electron when occupied; a valence state contributes a hole when empty. In the parabolic-edge approximation the resulting counts are

n=∫Ec∞gc(E)f(E) dE,p=∫−∞Evgv(E)[1−f(E)] dE.n=\int_{E_c}^{\infty}g_c(E) f(E)\,dE,\qquad p=\int_{-\infty}^{E_v}g_v(E) [1-f(E)]\,dE.

First put the Fermi level near the middle of the gap and move it upward while keeping temperature fixed. At any fixed energy ff increases, so the conduction band gains electrons while the valence band loses holes. Then vary temperature with EFE_F fixed in the gap. The edge occupations change as the transition in ff broadens. Moving the slider is not a self-consistent doping calculation: in a real isolated sample the Fermi level may shift with temperature to keep the material electrically neutral.

The dilute limit and a compensated crystal

When the Fermi level is several thermal energies from both edges, the conduction tail is approximately e−(E−EF)/(kBT)e^{-(E-E_F)/(k_BT)}, and the empty fraction in the valence band is approximately e−(EF−E)/(kBT)e^{-(E_F-E)/(k_BT)}. For example, five thermal energies above EFE_F, exact occupation is about 0.00669 against the exponential estimate 0.00674. Near EFE_F the approximation fails: at the Fermi level that same exponential gives 1 instead of one-half.

Insert the conduction tail into the integral and take the factor involving Ec−EFE_c-E_F outside. Check your understanding

Per unit sample volume, two narrow bins have equal energy width. One lies four times as far from the conduction edge as the other. What is their availability ratio in the parabolic model?

About 2:1 because availability per energy is proportional to the square root of the distance from the edge. This says nothing yet about the ratio of occupied populations.

What changes if the Fermi level is raised by kBTln⁡10k_BT\ln 10 while both dilute approximations remain valid?

The electron concentration is multiplied by 10, and the hole concentration is divided by 10. Their product does not change at fixed temperature and gap.

In a gap with no available states at EFE_F, how many states there are half occupied?

None. One-half is the occupation probability for a hypothetical state at that energy; multiplying by a zero DOS gives zero occupied-state density.
