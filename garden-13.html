The work that only exists between teammates

Over four meetings, a team rethinks how a television remote should work. Someone proposes putting advanced buttons inside a drawer. Later, the group considers an LCD for programme listings but drops the idea when nobody can say where the listings will come from. A prototype nevertheless appears at the final meeting with a small screen and a menu. Its designer explains that the menu hides the advanced functions until needed. The team then considers moving feedback from the remote to the television. Taken as a series of decisions about whether to include a screen, the meetings seem inconsistent. Taken as a record of a problem, they are remarkably coherent: how can the remote look simple while leaving advanced controls available? The staged design meetings are part of the public AMI Meeting Corpus (AMI Corpus, n.d.-a, n.d.-b, ES2002b–d).

A handoff that says “remove the LCD” is accurate at one moment and misleading at another. It is also silent about which part of the work should survive the removal. A later designer needs the rationale, not just the last thing someone said about a component. That distinction recurs in software projects, flight decks, and committees: the task that falls between contributors is to carry decisions across changes in form, across different local views of the work, and into the combined result.

When separate work is not independent

David Parnas (1972) described two designs for the same small program for indexing lines of text. In one, modules corresponded to processing steps. Several components therefore knew details of how the lines were stored. In the other, the responsibility for storing those lines belonged to a single module; others could ask it for what they needed without depending on the storage layout. Both programs could do the job. Only the latter let the storage representation change without requiring the people responsible for the rest of the program to follow it.

This is a practical distinction between dividing tasks and dividing decisions. A storage format can be changed locally as long as its clients still receive what they expect. A change in what the clients receive, even under the same names, is a different matter. A chart and an input file can both pass their local tests even if the chart expects a rate per hour and the file now counts events per day. A list of assignments will not by itself show who has to check the agreement.

A historical study of nine open-source projects examined what happens when branches are combined (Brun et al., 2013, §4, Figure 4). For three projects whose builds and tests could be rerun, the researchers observed 266 textual conflicts. They found 24 build failures and 109 test failures in combinations for which the text could be joined cleanly. Those 133 failures occurred after the step that ordinarily tells contributors whether their edits can be merged. A trial integration offers a concrete answer to the authors of both changes: not that the two edits touched the same place, but that the result no longer works under an observable check.

A trial can only catch an expectation somebody has expressed. Interviews with 24 GitHub users found one case in which a third party alerted a project owner to a dependency: their company relied on the behavior that the proposed change would alter (Dabbish et al., 2012, pp. 1282–1283). People can see exactly what changed in a proposed contribution without seeing who depends on it. The two studies therefore point to different uses of visibility. A trial combination shows an incompatibility already represented in a check. A visible proposal gives someone a chance to state the dependency nobody thought to check.

Place the decision where it can be used

During a landing approach, an aircraft crew does not keep all relevant speeds in memory. Hutchins (1995, pp. 274–282) followed how a crew selected the card corresponding to the expected landing weight, read the values aloud, and set markers on the airspeed indicators. The card made it possible to check the selected weight against the aircraft’s weight display. The readback exposed errors in what a pilot had heard. Setting the markers made a different check possible: the pilot could see the distance between actual and target speed without retrieving a number and subtracting it mentally. The same decision took a succession of forms, each suited to a different comparison.

A meeting archive is closer to the booklet in Hutchins’s account than to the marker on the instrument. It preserves the information from which a decision can be reconstructed. To turn that decision into a check, someone has to bring it alongside the artifact that will be used. For the designers of the remote, that could mean testing whether a user can reach an advanced function on the prototype, rather than verifying only that the latest mock-up omits the LCD. The meeting record establishes why access matters; the prototype provides a place to see whether it has been preserved.

Give each piece of evidence a route into the choice

Sometimes the missing part of a joint result is neither a broken component nor a forgotten decision. It is a fact somebody knew but the group did not use. In Stasser and Titus’s (1985) candidate-selection experiments, different members received different candidate information. Where the decisive facts were shared, 15 of 18 groups selected the candidate favored by the full materials. Where key positive facts were known to different members, only 7 of the 38 groups that reached a decision chose that candidate. Distributing a fact does not guarantee that it enters the discussion, much less that it changes the comparison.

The distinction becomes especially clear in a theatre-planning experiment involving 77 teams (Hoever et al., 2012). Each team had people assigned to artistic, finance, and events roles; the researchers varied whether everyone received all priorities or each person brought a different set. Assigning different priorities was associated with more creative plans when the teams had been asked to consider one another’s perspective. The researchers coded not only whether the priorities were mentioned but whether the team used them to develop its plan. A group might acknowledge that performance times matter to ticket sales and then do nothing with that constraint. Another might use it to arrange a sequence that also preserves an artistic requirement. A meeting summary that credits both teams with having “discussed ticket sales” misses the result worth explaining.

The work that exists between teammates is easiest to see when something changes. A component is revised, a shelved mechanism is reconsidered, or somebody introduces an observation the others did not have. The question is what happens next: can the group identify what still holds, find the parts that depend on it, and show that the revised pieces serve the same purpose together? A record of who finished their assignment cannot answer that question. A combined result can.
