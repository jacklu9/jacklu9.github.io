A convincing simulation can still give the wrong number

A fictional party, human survey answers, and a sample-size calculation show three different things a model can get right or wrong.

Twelve residents of a simulated town called Smallville were invited to a Valentine’s Day party. Five arrived. Some absentees later cited conflicts in generated interviews; others expressed interest without having made a plan to attend. Park et al. (2023) checked statements about hearing of the party against the simulation’s stored dialogue. The record could distinguish an invitation actually appearing in a conversation from a convincing account constructed later. Five arrivals were an observable result in the town the researchers had built, not an estimate of how many people would come to a real party.

Three jobs for a simulated person

A model might be asked to produce a plausible account, to identify questions worth putting to people, or to predict what a particular person would say. In the Smallville work, one comparison asked human raters to rank generated answers for believability. The alternative architectures answered from the same history, so the comparison addressed the rendered responses rather than how separate towns would evolve over two days. A later study by Park et al. (2026) used a different target: predict held-out answers from 1,052 people whose interviews, surveys or demographic descriptions were available to the model. On one set of survey questions, accuracy rose from 60.4 percent using demographics to 72.8 percent using the richer material; the participants’ own answers agreed 79.5 percent of the time after two weeks. On five incentivized economic games, however, the study’s average within-person correlations were lower with survey and interview information than with demographics alone. More detail about a person improved one target and did not improve the other.

A simulation can be useful for a different job even when it does not reproduce a distribution. Xu et al. (2026) gave a model six questions previously answered by 38 teachers who had used augmented reality in class. The model-generated respondents surfaced all fifteen themes the researchers had coded in teachers’ accounts of implementation problems and added others, including privacy and ergonomics. Those are topics an investigator can bring to an interview and ask about. The answers to a closed question told a different story: sixteen teachers selected the lowest rating of institutional support, while the synthetic responses clustered one step higher. A list of plausible difficulties is a source of interview prompts; it does not supply proportions for a report on how teachers experienced an institution.

The intended decision supplies the test. To use a generated teacher response as a prompt, an interviewer can ask whether it uncovered a specific issue missing from the interview guide. To use it in an estimate of institutional support, the researcher needs to compare the ratings with the people who actually answer the question and inspect where the distributions differ. Asking independent readers whether the generated response sounds plausible cannot serve as a substitute for either comparison. The work of choosing a target is part of setting up the model evaluation, not a change of mind made after the score comes back.

A wrong number can change the study

The consequence appears before recruitment in work by Bisbee et al. (2024). The authors compared responses generated from American National Election Studies profiles with reported attitudes from the survey. Similar overall orderings hid differences in spread and in some relationships between attitudes and respondent characteristics. In one power calculation for a change in partisan polarization, the synthetic estimates implied a sample of 35 partisan respondents for 99 percent nominal power. The estimates based on the human survey implied 299. The generated data showed a larger expected change and less variation, both of which pushed the recommended sample size down.

The same model might supply an excellent follow-up question and a misleading answer about how many people need to be asked. That is why the evaluation must state what the substitution is for: inventing a possibility, estimating an effect, or predicting a person’s action. It is also why an audit needs cases that look unremarkable. A simulated result cannot be validated only against examples chosen because the machine found them surprising. A useful question stays useful after it is asked of real people; a useful estimate needs to survive that change in who supplies the answer.
