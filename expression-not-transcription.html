Expression is not transcription

Sometimes a tool needs to preserve a decision the person has already made. Sometimes it should help them discover that decision.

A researcher asks a person who uses a communication device a question. The answer takes time and comes out brief. The researcher offers the person an opportunity to finish by email, where there will be no pressure to keep up. In an interview case reported by Beneteau (2020), the participant explained that the short replies were how they would ordinarily answer. There was a real cost to composing them. The interviewer’s mistake would have been assuming that the cost had prevented a longer answer the person wanted to give.

Three different kinds of fidelity

Fidelity to the wording. If the intended message is already settled, keeping it intact matters. A rewriting tool must distinguish an optional improvement from a change in who is promising what. A small, editable correction may be more important than generating a replacement paragraph.

Fidelity to the occasion. A reply can preserve the wording and still attach it to the wrong part of the conversation. In the small set of interviews analyzed by Valencia et al. (2024), many device-mediated turns were responses to the last speaker before the device user started typing. There were also occasions on which someone erased a reply after the group had moved to another topic. An interface can help here without putting more words in the reply. It can keep the original question in view or show that someone is already composing a response.

Fidelity to an emerging thought. Giving a thought a form can make something newly apparent to the writer. Galbraith and Baaijen (2018) distinguish the emergence of ideas in writing from the more deliberate work of shaping what the text says. The first rough version need not be a complete specification for the second. A sketch, a contrasting phrase, or a reader’s question can give the author a chance to see a distinction they had not made earlier.

A line the author wants to keep

Nicholes (2025) asked university writers to create a poem about a difficult experience and to prompt a text generator to write about the same event. Writers judged their own poems closer to what they had lived through; half nevertheless preferred the generated version. A person can like someone else’s rendition more and still be entitled to say which part gets the experience wrong. A polished draft can make that judgment harder if it hides the point where the description shifted.

The three kinds of fidelity give a writer, designer, or reader different choices to watch. Did the wording change? Did the audience hear it as a response to the intended question? Did the person discover a direction worth taking, and could they leave the suggestion behind if it did not fit? A better tool need not always find the sentence already waiting to be spoken. It should let the person decide which new form is worth making their own.
