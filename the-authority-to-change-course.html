Who can change the result?

Seeing why a choice was made, objecting to it, and changing what happens next are different powers.

A robot carried a cup too high above the table. The person guiding it could push its arm lower, but what happened next depended on its controller. In an experiment by Bajcsy et al. (2017), ten people corrected this and other cup-carrying movements. Two controllers let the arm yield when the person pushed on it. One then returned to the original trajectory; the other incorporated the intervention into the rest of its movement. Participants spent less time intervening and used less force with the latter. They found it more responsive to what they wanted, without judging its reactions more predictable. A correction can become effective without making the new behavior obvious in advance.

Both controllers accepted the physical intervention. Only one made it part of what the system would do next. That distinction matters whenever a person can interrupt an automated process without necessarily changing the instruction, queue, or rule behind it.

What is still queued after a stop?

A related complication arises when the path onward is already queued. In a simulation of moving-target control, Endsley and Kaber (1999) interrupted automation and measured time until the operator took an appropriate manual action. Recovery was slower in configurations that queued actions, including one that let the person choose the order. Who selected the target did not answer who could promptly act once the system stopped. In a public service, the analogous question is whether a decision under review is still being carried out and what, if anything, pauses it. An exception needs to reach the reviewer before commitment; a correction also needs a route to any action that has already been committed.

The record of a correction could therefore show three outcomes without conflating them: the original action was stopped; a different action took its place; and the record or rule that produced it was changed. An arm can stop while retaining the original target for a later restart. An agency can amend a person’s benefits without changing the rule for the next applicant. These outcomes may all be reasonable on their own terms, but a message saying only that the issue was handled leaves the most consequential distinction unavailable to the person who raised it.

Where a correction has to go

A similar distinction separates an objection from an operative change in public services. In K.W. v. Armstrong (2015), an Idaho Medicaid hearing officer could send a disputed budget back for recalculation or assessment but could not set it or authorize a denied service. How an appeal can change the record follows that case from notice to the office that can act.

Across 71 court dockets about disability, unemployment and nutrition assistance, Gules-Guctas (2026) distinguished mistakes in records from problems in how rules were represented. A correction to a factual record can change the result of a new calculation. If the rule itself excludes a relevant need, recalculating with better data can leave the problem intact.

Returning to the cup, the operator can see whether the arm stays low once their hand is gone. In an institution, the equivalent feedback is a concrete account of what changed: which action was paused, who decided what replaces it, and whether the record or rule has changed for the next decision. A message saying the input was received supplies none of that information.
