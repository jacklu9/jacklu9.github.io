Semiconductor doping
Follow the electron after absorption
Return to the optical transition we have just drawn. A photon has promoted one electron from
the valence band into the conduction band. The photon is gone, but the crystal has changed in
two places in its electronic population: there is now an electron in the conduction band and an
empty state in the valence band. If we want to know what can subsequently carry charge, we must
follow both changes.
Let e > 0 be the elementary charge: an electron carries −e. On the left, Ec and Ev mark the
conduction-band bottom and valence-band top; their separation is Eg = Ec −Ev. On the right,
watch what happens when a neighbouring valence electron fills the vacancy.

A missing negative charge behaves as a positive mobile carrier; no proton has been added.
Relative to a filled valence band, removing one electron changes the charge by +e. The vacancy
moves opposite to the electron that fills it, so tracking it as a positive carrier reproduces the charge
rearrangement. This moving vacancy is a hole. Its positive effective mass near the valence-band
maximum is the description obtained earlier from the electron band’s downward curvature.
In a fully occupied band, contributions from opposite velocities cancel in the simple bulk picture.
Removing one electron removes one contribution; hole motion tracks the resulting current. The
absorption event therefore creates two mobile excitations, although only one electron crossed the
gap. In the ideal undoped crystal at zero temperature the valence band is full and the conduction
band empty.
Thermal excitation can also create pairs at nonzero temperature; recombination
reverses the process when a conduction electron fills a valence vacancy.
To compare whole samples, counting one excitation at a time becomes inconvenient. Write n for
the concentration of conduction electrons and p for the concentration of holes, each measured per
real-space volume, usually in cm−3 or m−3. Thus n = 1010 cm−3 corresponds to an average of 1010
conduction electrons in 1 cm3. The symbol n leaves out the much larger background of electrons
in filled valence states; it counts the mobile conduction population relevant here.
Establish a reference before adding impurities
For the numerical comparison that follows,
begin a new example: three pieces of silicon at 300 K. One is an ideal undoped reference; the other
two will contain phosphorus or boron. These silicon samples have their own material data. We do
not transfer the illustrative band energies from the preceding optical-transition exercise to them.
First leave the reference piece in the dark long enough to reach thermal equilibrium. Thermal
generation and recombination then have equal average rates, although individual events and carrier
motion continue. An ideal undoped material whose population is set by thermal excitation of the
host bands is called intrinsic.
Since those excitations create electrons and holes in pairs, its
equilibrium concentrations satisfy
n = p = ni.
Here ni is the intrinsic concentration of the host at the specified temperature. We will use
the classroom room-temperature silicon value ni = 1010 cm−3 for this comparison. It describes
both populations in the reference piece; it is not a universal constant of all semiconductors, nor
a temperature-independent constant of silicon. The equality counts particles and does not imply
equal electron and hole masses, speeds or mobilities. While light continuously drives extra carriers,
the sample need not obey this equilibrium population balance; the comparisons below concern
thermal equilibrium.

The single-photon picture has now answered one part of the counting problem. Each promotion
adds one conduction electron and one hole, with charge changes −e and +e that cancel. It has
not explained how a material could have many more of one carrier than the other. That is the
difference we will look for in the two doped pieces.

Both short energy arrows avoid the full gap. More importantly, each event supplies one mobile
carrier balanced by one fixed ion, rather than a mobile electron–hole pair. This explains how
doping changes the mobile populations without substantially changing the host band diagram in
the dilute-doping model.
If the phosphorus piece has more conduction electrons than holes at equilibrium, we call it n-type.
Its electrons are the majority carriers, and its holes the minority carriers. The boron piece
is p-type when holes outnumber electrons. A population substantially controlled by electrically
active impurities is in the extrinsic regime. Thermal pair creation still occurs in both pieces. If
the temperature rises until the intrinsic population overwhelms the net doping, even a chemically
doped piece approaches intrinsic behaviour.

Count the charges in the same piece
Take the boron piece and suppose it contains 1016 acceptor atoms per cm3, with negligible donors.
If those acceptors have captured electrons, there are approximately 1016 fixed negative ions per
cm3. Positive holes can balance them. But thermally excited conduction electrons have not ceased
to exist; their negative charge also belongs in the balance. We need an equation that keeps all four
populations, even if some later turn out to be small.
