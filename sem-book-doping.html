1.1.2 Semiconductor doping

Follow the electron after absorption

Return to the optical transition we have just drawn. A photon has promoted one electron from the valence band into the conduction band. The photon is gone, but the crystal has changed in two places in its electronic population: there is now an electron in the conduction band and an empty state in the valence band. If we want to know what can subsequently carry charge, we must follow both changes.

Let e > 0 be the elementary charge: an electron carries −e. On the left, Ec and Ev mark the conduction-band bottom and valence-band top; their separation is Eg = Ec − Ev. On the right, watch what happens when a neighbouring valence electron fills the vacancy.

Left: a band-energy change. Right: vacancy bookkeeping, not a literal path of a Bloch electron.

A missing negative charge behaves as a positive mobile carrier; no proton has been added.

Relative to a filled valence band, removing one electron changes the charge by +e. The vacancy moves opposite to the electron that fills it, so tracking it as a positive carrier reproduces the charge rearrangement. This moving vacancy is a hole. Its positive effective mass near the valence-band maximum is the description obtained earlier from the electron band’s downward curvature.

In a fully occupied band, contributions from opposite velocities cancel in the simple bulk picture. Removing one electron removes one contribution; hole motion tracks the resulting current. The absorption event therefore creates two mobile excitations, although only one electron crossed the gap. In the ideal undoped crystal at zero temperature the valence band is full and the conduction band empty. Thermal excitation can also create pairs at nonzero temperature; recombination reverses the process when a conduction electron fills a valence vacancy.

To compare whole samples, counting one excitation at a time becomes inconvenient. Write n for the concentration of conduction electrons and p for the concentration of holes, each measured per real-space volume, usually in cm−3 or m−3. Thus n = 1010 cm−3 corresponds to an average of 1010 conduction electrons in 1 cm3. The symbol n leaves out the much larger background of electrons in filled valence states; it counts the mobile conduction population relevant here.

Establish a reference before adding impurities For the numerical comparison that follows, begin a new example: three pieces of silicon at 300 K. One is an ideal undoped reference; the other two will contain phosphorus or boron. These silicon samples have their own material data. We do not transfer the illustrative band energies from the preceding optical-transition exercise to them.

First leave the reference piece in the dark long enough to reach thermal equilibrium. Thermal generation and recombination then have equal average rates, although individual events and carrier motion continue. An ideal undoped material whose population is set by thermal excitation of the host bands is called intrinsic. The equality counts particles and does not imply equal electron and hole masses, speeds or mobilities. While light continuously drives extra carriers, the sample need not obey this equilibrium population balance; the comparisons below concern thermal equilibrium.

The single-photon picture has now answered one part of the counting problem. Each promotion adds one conduction electron and one hole, with charge changes −e and +e that cancel. It has not explained how a material could have many more of one carrier than the other. That is the difference we will look for in the two doped pieces.

Change one silicon atom and follow the charge

Now replace a small fraction of the silicon atoms. In the phosphorus piece, an extra electron can become mobile without an electron first leaving the valence band. In the boron piece, a valence electron can be captured by an impurity, leaving a mobile hole without sending an electron into the conduction band. The two short arrows below show the changes we need to explain. The host edges remain Ec and Ev; the added impurity levels are labelled ED and EA.

Consider the left-hand arrow first. Phosphorus has one more valence electron than the silicon atom it replaces. Four participate in the usual bonding arrangement; the additional electron can be weakly bound to the impurity. The energy of that bound state, ED, lies slightly below Ec. It needs only the binding energy Ec − ED to reach an extended conduction state. An impurity that supplies an electron this way is a donor, and ED is its donor level. Introducing such electrically active impurities is called doping.

In the right-hand picture, boron has one fewer valence electron than silicon. An electron from the valence band can occupy an impurity state at EA, slightly above Ev. Supplying the smaller energy difference EA−Ev transfers the electron into that state. An impurity that accepts a valence electron this way is an acceptor. The neutral acceptor A0 gains a negative charge and becomes A−; the state from which the electron came becomes a hole:

Here is the valence-band hole: the electron captured by the acceptor is the one now missing from the valence band. Other valence electrons can move that vacancy onward while A− stays fixed.

Both short energy arrows avoid the full gap. More importantly, each event supplies one mobile carrier balanced by one fixed ion, rather than a mobile electron–hole pair. This explains how doping changes the mobile populations without substantially changing the host band diagram in the dilute-doping model.

If the phosphorus piece has more conduction electrons than holes at equilibrium, we call it n-type. Its electrons are the majority carriers, and its holes the minority carriers. The boron piece is p-type when holes outnumber electrons. A population substantially controlled by electrically active impurities is in the extrinsic regime. Thermal pair creation still occurs in both pieces. If the temperature rises until the intrinsic population overwhelms the net doping, even a chemically doped piece approaches intrinsic behaviour.

There is now no contradiction in saying that the phosphorus piece contains many mobile electrons and is electrically neutral. Each released electron can be balanced by a donor ion that does not move with it. To calculate the populations, we need to write that balance explicitly.
