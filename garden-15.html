A simulated participant is not a recruit

A research team can ask a language model to play the part of a visitor who sounds enthusiastic about a demonstration but never comes back. The answer may be useful immediately: the visitor could not find the room, did not know how long the event would last, or had to leave early to catch a bus. Each suggestion gives the researchers a possible question or observation. The problem comes when the same simulation is asked how often those things happen. A hundred fluent answers can look like a hundred observations even though they all came from one model and one choice of prompts.

Research on simulated people has put three kinds of claims to quite different tests. A simulation can unfold coherently enough to suggest behavior worth investigating. It can reproduce an average, an individual’s response, or a distribution in data that already exist. It can make a forecast about a new comparison before its results are known. These jobs sound similar in conversation. They have different consequences when the team must decide what to test, how many people to recruit, or whether a small expected improvement is worth pursuing.

Believable actions, observed actions

In the simulated town of Smallville, 25 language-model agents had records of observations, reflections, and plans. When one agent organized a Valentine’s Day party and word spread, five of the twelve invited agents attended (Park et al., 2023). A simulated interview afterward could be checked against the simulation’s own records: some absentees said they had a conflict; others expressed interest despite not having made a plan to go. The authors also asked 100 people to judge answers for believability. Responses from the full architecture ranked highest. The alternatives in that comparison answered from the history generated by the full system rather than each simulating a separate two days. The attendance count was an observation of that world; the human evaluation concerned how convincingly systems answered from its history.

A later Park et al. (2026) study had something Smallville did not: the withheld answers of the real people being modeled. The team collected interviews, surveys, and behavioral data from 1,052 U.S. adults. With the targets kept out of the prompts, Claude Opus 5.5 predicted first-wave General Social Survey responses with 60.4% accuracy from demographics, and 72.8% when given interview and survey answers. The people themselves agreed with their own earlier answers 79.5% of the time on a two-week retest. On five incentivized economic games the comparison went the other way: mean within-person correlations were .56 with demographics and .45 with interview and survey answers. Richer descriptions improved prediction on one set of outcomes while doing worse on another. A model that plausibly explains what someone would choose and a model that predicts what that person chooses are not interchangeable instruments.

When an average hides the missing people

Bisbee et al. (2024, §§2.1–2.3) prompted GPT-3.5 Turbo with profiles based on U.S. survey respondents and asked it to rate warmth toward eleven groups. Its average ratings put groups in broadly the same order as the human survey did. But the model’s distributions were often narrower, particularly for ratings of racial and religious groups, and comparisons by political party uncovered disagreements the aggregate obscured. The authors used the data to calculate how many partisans a study would need to detect a change in affective polarization. At 99% nominal power, the synthetic effect and spread implied 35 participants. The corresponding numbers from the human survey implied 299. Both the larger synthetic effect and the smaller synthetic variance made the smaller study look sufficient.

There are more useful ways to carry a generated answer into a real study. Xu et al. (2026) compared responses generated for 38 simulated teachers with responses from 38 teachers who had used augmented reality in class. The generated responses covered all 15 themes coded in the teachers’ accounts of implementation problems and added ten, including concerns about privacy and ergonomics. On a closed question about institutional support, however, 16 of the real teachers chose the lowest rating. The simulated sample most often chose the second-lowest rating, and none chose the highest. A new theme can earn a place in an interview guide: ask who can see data from the device, or observe how long students can use it comfortably. That use does not require treating the model’s distribution of ratings as the experience of a population.

Right order, wrong size

A forecast can get the order of two effects right and get both sizes wrong. Suppose one invitation is predicted to raise attendance by eight percentage points and another by four. If the observed gains are two points and one, the ranking would still favor the first invitation. A sample designed to detect an eight-point gain might nevertheless be far too small to detect a two-point gain. The decisions “which to test first?” and “how many participants are needed?” require distinguishable forecast qualities.

Ashokkumar et al. (2026) gave GPT-4 the materials from 70 past U.S. survey experiments and asked it to forecast between-condition differences. In resampled comparisons across hundreds of contrasts, the median correlation with observed effects was .85 for GPT-4 and .84 for pooled lay forecasts. The model still tended to overestimate effect sizes. Human forecasters have a related problem. Across the initial projects on the Social Science Prediction Platform for which forecasts could be matched to results, DellaVigna and Vivalt (2026) found mean standardized forecasts around .18 and mean observed effects around .10. Pooled estimates provided information about which effects were larger, but their spread across questions was greater than the spread of the corresponding effects.

A team about to run a study can record the predicted order and the smallest gain that would justify changing the invitation. It can estimate recruitment around that second quantity, examine how the estimate would change if attendance or variability were different, and keep the model’s forecast for a separate comparison once the data exist. This setup preserves the question the model helped pose even if the expected effect proves too large. It also protects a decision that no convincing simulated answer can settle: whether enough real visitors would change what they do for the intervention to be worth adopting.
