How much did the reader learn?

The surprise of a sentence, a reader’s confidence, and a correct choice need not move together.

Imagine a public building that posts a notice about an evening entrance. On some nights the lobby stays open; on others, visitors need to use the side door. The notices have the same layout and differ only in what they say about that night's entrance. One visitor reads a notice and becomes almost certain the lobby is open. A second becomes equally certain it is closed. If both started equally unsure, they have gained the same amount of certainty. They cannot both be right about this notice.

That is one reason “More people felt informed” is not a complete evaluation. Shannon’s (1948) measure expresses uncertainty over a distribution of possible outcomes; it does not ask whether someone’s revised belief points toward an observed event. On a closed-lobby night, someone who puts the chance of an open lobby at 90% scores .81 on the binary Brier loss; someone who puts it at 10% scores .01 (Brier, 1950). The numbers come from squaring the difference between each prediction and the actual result, zero for a closed lobby. Both readers end up less uncertain than at a probability of 50%, but the direction of one change makes the prediction worse.

The same phrase can make different claims to its readers

Bonnefon and Villejoubert (2006) gave respondents two hypothetical consultations in which a condition was described as possible, one concerning deafness and the other insomnia. Of the 810 respondents who considered deafness the worse news, 60% interpreted the wording as tact in that scenario, compared with 17.4% for insomnia. In the retained sample as a whole, numerical interpretations of the probability peaked higher for deafness. Among those reading the wording as uncertainty in both scenarios, the peaks were nearly the same. The wording had not changed. Readers were drawing on what a person might be trying to do by saying it.

Mandel and Irwin (2021), studying communication against an intelligence probability scale, found closer agreement with the scale when 1,202 participants saw numerical ranges rather than exclusively verbal categories. Adding the verbal phrase to the range did not improve agreement. If the goal is to convey a particular probability scale, a numeric range is a candidate for that purpose. It is not a prediction of whether a person will change their plans. Someone who needs an accessible entrance may take a 20% chance of closure differently from someone who can easily walk around the building.

Separate noticing the restriction from guessing that one exists

An entrance study could include both open and closed nights. It could ask a reader which entrance they expect to use and how certain they are, then observe where they go. Keeping the correct entrance in mind still does not show whether they could complete the visit. Nor does completing it show which improvement made the trip worth undertaking. With only closed-lobby examples, a new design might appear better simply because readers learned to choose the side door more often. Signal detection distinguishes recognizing the relevant condition from becoming more willing to answer that it applies (Tanner & Swets, 1954). The open nights show what happens to false alarms. Confidence judgments allow a comparison across possible thresholds rather than only at the point where one person is willing to act.

The decision carries another clock: someone who reads the sign after taking a wrong bus has encountered the evidence too late for the first choice. That is the same distinction as the lead time someone can actually use. A careful test would track what readers inferred and what they did when that inference reached them. If asking for an initial probability would draw attention to the notice, a separate group can supply the baseline. The group observed in the wild need not be told in advance which detail the study hopes they will notice.
