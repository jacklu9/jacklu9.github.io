When memory should stay quiet

The right thing to remember is not always the right thing to mention.

A writing assistant retrieves a standing preference for a comedian’s point of view. Its next task is to prepare a rental history for a tenant screening company. The preference is real and correctly recalled; applying it here is the error. Yoon et al. (2026) used this kind of mismatch in a benchmark that gives a model the same profile while varying whom a draft is for and why it is being written. A lively voice that suits a creative exercise can be a poor fit for an audience trying to confirm dates.

BenchPreS paired ten constructed profiles with 39 recipient–task combinations. Human annotators classified whether preferences were suitable in each setting; a model judge assessed whether a preference appeared in the draft. An instruction to choose suitable preferences reduced Claude 4.5 Sonnet’s use of those labelled inappropriate from about 53% to 14%, while the rate for appropriate ones shifted from about 88% to 85%. The size of the effect differed across models. A system can therefore retrieve something correctly and still need a separate test of how it chooses to use it.

Answering a memory question is a different task

Sumida et al. (2026) compared direct questions drawn from a four-month deployment with the natural exchanges corresponding to those questions. Across 316 questions, GPT-4.1-mini was correct about 79% of the time when asked explicitly. In the conversational answers it referred to the particular fact in about 8% of cases. The same model and reconstructed history were used for both. That gap is not a claim that every conversation required repetition of the fact. The study also scored contextual integration, which can happen without mentioning the detail. It shows what an evaluation must specify before counting a recovered fact as useful: what the current request made relevant and what the reply needed to accomplish.

Hu et al. (2026) constructed cases where surfacing a remembered detail could harm the reply. A personal detail may be unrelated to the request. A preference may fit one audience but not another. Or a system may let its familiarity with the user’s perspective displace an accurate answer. These are not the same as failing to retrieve a fact. A richer memory supplies more of the material that can go wrong, and an evaluation that treats every repetition as success will miss all three.

The distinction resembles repair in a conversation. A useful response preserves the part that is already understood and does something with the part that is relevant now. It need not replay the entire history to show that the history mattered. The same applies to a handoff where some decisions are still open: stating an interpretation as final can close a choice that the next person was meant to make. If the person's preference controls a real decision, a quiet reference to the resulting constraint may be enough. If it does not belong, leaving it out is a meaningful behavior rather than an absence of memory.

Test the transfer, not the amount recalled

A study could hold a history fixed and vary the recipient, task and authority. Suppose one person asks for a playful social invitation, a formal housing document and a message about a disputed appointment in turn. Before testing the assistant, evaluators can identify which earlier preferences should affect each text and which earlier agreements determine what the sender can promise. The immediate outcomes include applying a fit preference, leaving a mismatched one unused and asking a narrow question where the record is ambiguous. The later outcomes include whether a commitment survived the rewrite. A system that embellishes every draft with remembered facts can look attentive in the first interaction while failing the handoff.
