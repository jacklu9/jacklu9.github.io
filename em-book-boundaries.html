1.2.8 Boundary conditions

What can happen when the material changes? Return to the gap and replace part of its filling with a different material. The space remains connected, but the material response changes at an internal surface. A field component may now jump as we cross it. Which jumps are possible, and which would contradict the laws we have just learned?

A boundary condition answers that question by relating the two sides of the interface. Some components must be continuous; other jumps are supported by surface charge or current. We can discover the rules by taking our familiar loops and closed surfaces and making them very thin across the boundary.

The fields labelled 1 and 2 below mean the limiting values just next to the same interface point on the two sides. They are not values at two widely separated locations. In the bulk of either material, ordinary spatial derivatives can be used. At an ideal sharp jump, shrinking integral loops and volumes provide the appropriate description without treating the field as differentiable across that jump.

Why some terms disappear and others do not

A bounded volume density integrated through a layer of thickness h gives a contribution proportional to h; it vanishes as the layer shrinks. A surface density is already an integral through the thin layer, so its contribution is proportional to face area or cut length and remains finite. This is why ρs and Js survive the limiting arguments while ordinary bounded bulk densities do not.

A perfect electric conductor: how the surface supports exterior fields. An ideal perfect electric conductor (PEC) is the limit in which a nonzero bulk electric field would require unbounded conduction current. The bulk field is taken as zero. Real conductors can have small electric fields, finite losses, and finite penetration depths; these are outside the PEC idealisation.

An electric field tangent to the exterior surface would equal the interior tangential field by the shrinking-loop argument. It must therefore vanish. A normal exterior electric field can remain, ending on the conductor’s surface charge. Similarly, an exterior tangential magnetic field can remain and is supported by a surface current.
