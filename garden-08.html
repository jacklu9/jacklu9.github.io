A turn of one’s own

During a cooking activity in a UK classroom, a teacher asked whether to add salt. A seven-year-old child looked repeatedly toward her speech-generating device. The teacher took the exchange as assent and went to get the salt. A camera could record the direction of the child’s gaze and the progress of the recipe; it could not record the answer she had in mind. That distinction was part of the research design. Over fourteen weeks, Ibrahim et al. (2024) recorded about ten and a half hours of classroom interactions involving three children who used augmentative and alternative communication, or AAC, and their teaching staff. The researchers coded movement toward a task’s goal separately from evidence that the participants understood one another. Fetching the salt established that cooking went forward. It did not settle what that child wanted to contribute.

Conversational participation leaves similar gaps in other records. A transcript begins where a message is released. It does not include the unfinished reply, or show what happened in the few seconds between deciding to speak and having the means to do it. A useful communication tool has to make a turn possible, keep the intended meaning available to the speaker, and give other people a way to recognize what the turn is doing.

When a sentence misses its place

In ordinary conversation, people begin preparing to speak before the other person has finished. Sacks et al. (1974) described the organization that makes this possible: listeners anticipate where a turn might end, the current speaker can select who goes next, and, in the absence of a response, the current speaker can continue. Those practices help conversations move quickly. They also explain how a person using an AAC device can lose a turn without anyone consciously interrupting them. The rest of the group has no way to see a reply just beginning to take shape.

Valencia et al. (2024) explored this timing in work on COMPA. In two formative interviews supporting the project, 27 of 34 device-spoken turns addressed the utterance immediately preceding the start of composition. The other speakers waited in those instances. The researchers separately saw occasions when unfinished material was erased after the group had moved to another topic. The prototype gave the group a visible sign that a reply was being composed and could place a released reply next to the part of the conversation it answered. That would let a conversation continue without requiring that an eventual answer be interpreted as a response to the latest thing said.

In a trial of three prototype versions involving five AAC users, a participant changed a suggested line about disliking seafood to say she liked it. The words were convenient material, but the standpoint required correction. Another pair disagreed about whether the prototype should mute other speakers during composition. A visible sign and a compulsory wait offer different things: the first gives listeners something to coordinate around, while the second sets a rule about when others may speak. The small trial tested versions that bundled features rather than the effect of a context marker separately. It did, however, make two concrete decisions visible: who gets to decide what the contribution says, and who gets to decide what the group does until it is ready.

Less typing can leave more to review

SpeakFaster tackles the effort of composition itself. A person can enter the initials of words through eye gaze, inspect candidate phrases, provide part of a word if a suggestion is close, and choose when to send the result to speech. In the scripted lab task reported by Cai et al. (2024), one experienced gaze typist went from 4.05 to 6.54 words per minute using the system. Six of eighteen assisted turns differed from the script, against none of twelve baseline turns; researchers judged two of the assisted differences to affect meaning or comprehensibility. A separate study with nineteen touchscreen users found fewer actions without a reliable overall speed gain.

The difference between those results matters in practice. The saved movement has a different cost for a person using a finger and a person using gaze. Time can also move from composing to checking. During the lab session, this gaze typist spent longer scanning whole-sentence alternatives than choosing from alternatives for one word. An interface can make a near match useful by allowing the speaker to repair its one consequential error, rather than making the speaker choose between accepting a sentence and starting it again. In a separate field comparison, one gaze typist produced 8.1 words per minute over 856 ordinary utterances with relatives and carers, and 10.4 across 27 unscripted utterances with an experimenter using SpeakFaster. The figures show both what can be measured away from a script and why partner and setting still matter when estimating an interface’s contribution.

Who does the work of checking?

Conversation can also ask too much of a listener or leave them with too little to check. Gao et al. (2015) paired native English speakers and bilingual native Mandarin speakers in a navigation task. In translated conditions, people wrote in their own language and received either one machine translation or two. With two, pairs included an average of 65 percent of the required landmarks, up from 53 percent with one. Pairs who used English without translation averaged 66 percent.

The checks needed to get there did not change in the same direction for everybody. Mandarin-speaking followers used about 67 words in questions and checks with one translation and about 30 with two; English-speaking followers used about 22 with one and 55 with two. A second rendering can settle a question before it is asked, or make a previously hidden problem clear enough to ask about. A higher joint route score does not establish that each person’s contribution became easier. For a communication interface, these different demands are part of the design. A speaker can keep the shorter or more idiomatic wording if the listener has the means to recognize where understanding failed and ask about that part. A replacement sentence that simply sounds fluent offers no such guarantee.

The speaker’s choice may be the shorter one

A tool need not make a sentence more elaborate to make it more expressive. Beneteau (2020) describes an interview in which a participant gave short answers that took time to compose. Offered the chance to elaborate by email, the participant explained that those were the answers they would ordinarily give aloud. Their method of entering text added effort. It did not make the researcher a better judge of how much the person wanted to say.

Jamie Preece makes that point from the author’s side. Preece and his coauthors (2024) distinguish the collaboratively assembled first-person passages in their article from sections initially drafted by the design researchers. They combined prior writing and points made in discussion, then revised the first-person passages with Preece until he was satisfied. Preece also describes setting up pages of swear words on his device. Other people could convey his basic attitude while toning down a word he wanted to use. More accurate prediction of his general style would not give him back that choice. In a separate autoethnography, personalized AAC suggestions followed Tom Weinberg’s Spanish–English code-switching, but also surfaced accurate family details he found intrusive in context (Weinberg et al., 2026). Both experiences point toward controls that distinguish offering a phrasing from deciding what someone wants to disclose.

That freedom extends to when the equipment is used. In Broomfield et al. (2024), one participant used new AAC equipment for online dance classes and social events while continuing to use natural speech at home with her partner. Another returned a new device after difficulty using it with his existing equipment and kept using his earlier AAC. The longitudinal study followed individual trajectories, not just a decision to keep a device. It makes visible an outcome that high activation counts could miss: having a better way to participate when the person wants it, while retaining an easier or more fitting alternative at other times. A measure of participation needs to leave space for that choice, just as a conversation needs to leave room for an answer that has not arrived yet.
