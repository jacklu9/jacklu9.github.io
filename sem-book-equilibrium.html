1.2.3 Equilibrium conditions and current flow

The two silicon profiles in Example 2-5 put the last question in a concrete form. Both are drawn at 300 K, and both have a horizontal EF . In (a), the bands rise steadily from left to right. In (b), they fall in the outer regions and remain level in the middle.

Start at the left edge of each drawing and follow Ec across the sample. Where would you expect a field? Next compare EF with Ei: where should electrons be more numerous? The two observations concern different features of the same drawing. A slope tells us how an energy changes with position; a separation tells us how strongly states are occupied.

There is a puzzle here. Sloping bands suggest a force, and a varying carrier population suggests diffusion. Yet these diagrams describe equilibrium. We will first translate their slopes into field and force, then use the population variation to see how a zero total current is possible. Keep (a) and (b) in view as the formulas acquire meaning.

Read the horizontal axis before reading the slope

The rising line in (a) may resemble a piece of an earlier band-structure plot. Look at the horizontal label: it is now position x, not wavevector k. Moving right across this drawing means moving to another place in the sample. On an E–k plot, it means selecting a different electronic state.

The first slope gives a band-state velocity, and the curvature of that same E(k) curve gives effective mass. The second slope will give an electrostatic force. We cannot obtain the field in (a) by treating its spatial axis as a wavevector axis.

Does a rising electron band mean a rising voltage? Compare two neighbouring positions on the rising bands of (a). The electron’s band-edge energy is higher on the right. An electron has negative charge, so this need not mean that voltage is higher there.

To settle the sign, define the electrostatic potential V (x) as potential energy per unit positive charge, with units J/C = V. The electric field points toward decreasing potential:

A particle of charge q has electrostatic potential-energy contribution qV , and the force on it is −d(qV )/dx = qE. Thus increasing V raises a hole’s electrostatic energy by +eV but lowers an electron’s by −eV .

Return to (a): its constant positive slope gives a constant rightward field. In (b), each falling segment gives a leftward field, while the flat middle gives zero field. The potential and field are properties of the same sample; changing the test particle changes its charge and force, not the field.

This equal-shift argument assumes electrostatic band variation in one host material. A band offset at a material interface is not automatically an electrostatic field; it requires separate material information.

Follow a carrier through the same drawing The next request in Example 2-5 is to follow a hole in (a) and an electron in (b) at constant total energy. Before drawing their kinetic energies, decide what energy the vertical coordinate represents.

Near a conduction minimum, the electron’s energy above Ec(x) is its kinetic energy in the parabolic-band approximation. A hole is a missing valence electron, and its kinetic excitation energy is measured downward from the valence top. Let E label an electron-energy coordinate: for a hole it labels the missing valence-electron state. Use the drawn, flat equilibrium EF as a fixed reference:

Conduction electronValence hole
Potential energy Un = Ec − EFUp = EF − Ev
Kinetic energy Kn = E − Ec ≥ 0Kp = Ev − E ≥ 0
Total relative energy Un + Kn = E − EFUp + Kp = EF − E

The chosen potential-energy zeros contain arbitrary constants, but the spatial changes and forces are physical. With flat EF , −dUn/dx = −eE and −dUp/dx = +eE, matching the two charges.

First trace the hole in (a). As Ev rises, Up = EF − Ev falls. If the missing-electron coordinate E is a fixed horizontal line, Kp = Ev − E rises by the same amount. The total remains constant. For an electron moving toward rising Ec, the exchange goes the other way: Un rises and Kn falls. In (b), the falling Ec therefore lowers the electron’s potential energy and raises its kinetic energy, with neither changing in the flat middle.

Choose the horizontal line inside the allowed band throughout the path. If E < Ec, it cannot represent a conduction state at that position; if E > Ev, it cannot represent a valence hole there. These fixed-total-energy sketches describe motion between energy-changing scattering events, not

every carrier having one energy. We will choose allowed test lines when drawing the complete solution.

How can these sloping bands still give zero current?

Our prediction for (a) includes a rightward field, hence a positive electron drift current. But its Ei also rises while EF stays fixed. From n = ni exp[(EF − Ei)/(kBT)], the electron density decreases to the right. The gradient is negative, so the electron diffusion current is negative. The two contributions oppose one another.

This is how a nonuniform sample can differ from the uniform one at the start of the section. In thermal equilibrium, opposing microscopic processes balance at a common temperature. Carriers still move and scatter. Within the drift–diffusion setting, each carrier’s net current vanishes:

Particle arrows show direction only: the drift velocity is the same at both positions when µn and E are uniform. Conventional electron-current arrows reverse the particle-flow directions.

The host and temperature are uniform, so ni and T are constant.

Change the population without keeping equilibrium

So far, flat EF has tied the density variation to the band variation. What if an applied bias or light changes the populations? The sample may no longer be described by one global equilibrium distribution. A varying density alone does not prove this: our doped equilibrium sample already had one. We must ask whether the populations still share the same equilibrium occupation parameter.

If electrons and holes each relax rapidly enough within their own bands, each population can still have a local thermal form at the lattice temperature. We then use an electron quasi-Fermi level EFn(x) and a hole quasi-Fermi level EFp(x). In the nondegenerate regime, their population meanings are

Raising the electron occupation parameter raises f(E) at a given energy and increases n. These parameters describe the local populations; they are not additional band edges. In thermal equilibrium they coincide with one spatially constant EF . Injection or optical generation can instead make them separate and vary with position.

Qualitative local quasi-equilibrium picture at uniform temperature; the energy scale is schematic.

Only the portions at E ≥ Ec describe conduction-band states.

Compare this figure with case (b). Here Ec is flat, so its slope supplies no field, but EFn falls to the right. The separation Ec − EFn grows, reducing electron density and occupation at a fixed conduction energy. Electrons therefore diffuse right and conventional electron current points left. This is our density-driven current with its local occupation parameter made visible.

The interpretation relies on a local thermal form within each carrier population. If a strongly driven distribution has not relaxed to that form, a single local quasi-Fermi level is insufficient. Nor does a time-independent appearance establish equilibrium: under an applied bias, a steady current can persist. To identify equilibrium in the earlier diagrams we needed the common flat EF , not

just a stationary-looking band profile.
