How can these sloping bands still give zero current?
Our prediction for (a) includes a rightward field, hence a positive electron drift current. But its Ei
also rises while EF stays fixed. From n = ni exp[(EF −Ei)/(kBT)], the electron density decreases
to the right.
The gradient is negative, so the electron diffusion current is negative.
The two
contributions oppose one another.
This is how a nonuniform sample can differ from the uniform one at the start of the section. In
thermal equilibrium, opposing microscopic processes balance at a common temperature. Car-
riers still move and scatter. Within the drift–diffusion setting, each carrier’s net current vanishes:
Jn = 0,
Jp = 0.
Merely requiring Jn + Jp = 0 is weaker: equal and opposite nonzero electron and hole currents
would satisfy that sum without establishing thermal equilibrium.

Change the population without keeping equilibrium
So far, flat EF has tied the density variation to the band variation. What if an applied bias or light
changes the populations? The sample may no longer be described by one global equilibrium dis-
tribution. A varying density alone does not prove this: our doped equilibrium sample already had
one. We must ask whether the populations still share the same equilibrium occupation parameter.
If electrons and holes each relax rapidly enough within their own bands, each population can
still have a local thermal form at the lattice temperature. We then use an electron quasi-Fermi
level EFn(x) and a hole quasi-Fermi level EFp(x). In the nondegenerate regime, their population
meanings are
n(x) = nie[EF n(x)−Ei(x)]/(kBT),
p(x) = nie[Ei(x)−EF p(x)]/(kBT).
Raising the electron occupation parameter raises f(E) at a given energy and increases n. These
parameters describe the local populations; they are not additional band edges. In thermal equi-
librium they coincide with one spatially constant EF . Injection or optical generation can instead
make them separate and vary with position.
