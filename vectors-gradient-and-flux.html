Electromagnetic Theory · Chapter 1

Vectors, gradients, and flux

An arrow at a point, a direction of change, and a field crossing a surface answer different questions.

A shallow tank has an electric potential that varies from one point to the next. A probe at one position reports a single voltage relative to a reference. Move the probe a short distance and the reading changes. Put a small oriented surface into a field diagram and a different question arises: how much of the field crosses it? The three quantities in this lesson are designed for these three jobs: a value at a position, a rate of change in a direction, and a signed total through a surface.

Unless a physical unit is stated, the worked examples use dimensionless Cartesian coordinates. Think of each field as a snapshot. A spatial derivative compares nearby positions at the same time, not successive readings at one probe.

A vector value and the point that carries it

The point P=(1,2,0)P=(1,2,0) specifies where to look. The field F(x,y,z)=(2y,−x,3)\mathbf F(x,y,z)=(2y,-x,3) specifies what to find there. Substitution gives F(P)=(4,−1,3)\mathbf F(P)=(4,-1,3), so ∣F(P)∣=26|\mathbf F(P)|=\sqrt{26}. The position is not the field, and drawing the arrow at PP does not make its tip a later position of a particle.

The dot product compares a vector with another direction: a⋅b=axbx+ayby+azbz=∣a∣∣b∣cos⁡θ\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=a_xb_x+a_yb_y+a_zb_z=|\mathbf a||\mathbf b|\cos\theta. For a unit vector n^\hat{\mathbf n} the signed component of a\mathbf a in that direction is a⋅n^\mathbf a\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}. The sign tells which way along the chosen normal the component points. An ordinary vector such as (3,4,0)(3,4,0) must be divided by its length before its dot product measures the component along that direction.

The cross product serves a different purpose. Two tangent vectors p\mathbf p and q\mathbf q span a parallelogram. The vector p×q\mathbf p\times\mathbf q is perpendicular to it and has magnitude equal to its area. Reversing the order reverses the orientation without changing the area. This is why a cross product is useful for constructing an oriented surface element.

Derive a directional derivative

Let V(x,y,z)V(x,y,z) be differentiable. Beginning at PP, walk along a unit direction u^=(ux,uy,uz)\hat{\mathbf u}=(u_x,u_y,u_z) by a signed distance ss. The reading along the path is g(s)=V(P+su^)g(s)=V(P+s\hat{\mathbf u}). The chain rule compares the reading at neighboring points on that path:

g′(0)=∂V∂x(P)ux+∂V∂y(P)uy+∂V∂z(P)uz=∇V(P)⋅u^.g'(0)=\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}(P)u_x+\frac{\partial V}{\partial y}(P)u_y+\frac{\partial V}{\partial z}(P)u_z=\nabla V(P)\cdot\hat{\mathbf u}.

The vector ∇V\nabla V collects three partial derivatives. The directional derivative is a scalar: pick a direction, then project that vector onto it. If the gradient is nonzero, the largest rate is ∣∇V∣|\nabla V| and occurs along the gradient. This follows directly from cos⁡θ≤1\cos\theta\leq1. Along a direction tangent to a smooth constant-potential surface, the directional derivative is zero, so the gradient is perpendicular to the surface. At a point where the gradient vanishes, all first-order directional rates are zero.

For a concrete calculation, take V(x,y,z)=x2+2xy−zV(x,y,z)=x^2+2xy-z. At P=(1,2,0)P=(1,2,0) the gradient is (6,2,−1)(6,2,-1). Along u^=(3,4,0)/5\hat{\mathbf u}=(3,4,0)/5, it gives (18+8)/5=26/5(18+8)/5=26/5. Along v^=(−1,3,0)/10\hat{\mathbf v}=(-1,3,0)/\sqrt{10}, it gives zero. The latter walk need not stay on the same level surface for a finite distance: the absence of first-order change at one point is a local statement.

The distinction between tangent direction and level surface can be checked without a sketch. Write Δx=−s/10\Delta x=-s/\sqrt{10} and Δy=3s/10\Delta y=3s/\sqrt{10} for the second walk. The linear term is zero, but the quadratic part is Δx2+2ΔxΔy=−s2/2\Delta x^2+2\Delta x\Delta y=-s^2/2. A probe taking a finite step therefore reads a slightly lower potential even though the initial slope was zero. The tangent to the contour and the contour itself agree only to first order.

In electrostatics E=−∇V\mathbf E=-\nabla V. The minus sign points the field towards decreasing potential. If VV is in volts and a Cartesian coordinate in metres, each component of E\mathbf E is in volts per metre. The size of a direction vector should never change this physical rate; that is another reason to normalize before taking a directional derivative.

Choose a normal before calculating flux

Through a flat patch of area AA, a uniform field has flux Φ=F⋅n^ A\Phi=\mathbf F\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\,A. A field tangent to the patch gives zero flux even when its magnitude is large. Flipping the normal negates the answer. A closed surface comes with a natural convention: choose the outward normal.

When either the field or the normal varies, split the surface into small patches. At each patch multiply the local normal component by its area; then add the signed contributions. In the limit the sum is

Φ=∫SF⋅n^ dS.\Phi=\int_S\mathbf F\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\,dS.

For a parametrized surface r(u,v)\mathbf r(u,v), the vector element dS=(ru×rv) du dvd\mathbf S=(\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v)\,du\,dv already includes the area scale. If it is used, there is no additional factor of ∣ru×rv∣|\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v|. A unit normal, by contrast, needs the separate area element dSdS.

Compare the two projections

The two uses of the dot product can look identical on paper. In ∇V⋅u^\nabla V\cdot\hat{\mathbf u} the chosen direction is a direction of travel. In F⋅n^\mathbf F\cdot\hat{\mathbf n} it is the perpendicular to a surface. They need not be the same arrow. A path lying entirely in a patch uses a tangent for the first comparison and a normal for the second.

Before rotating the patch in the diagram, predict when its signed projection is largest, when it vanishes, and when it becomes negative. The angle is between the field and the normal, not between the field and the face. If the patch area stays fixed, these same changes govern the flux.

Worked example: a sloped patch

Consider the patch r(u,v)=(u,v,u)\mathbf r(u,v)=(u,v,u) for 0≤u,v≤10\leq u,v\leq1, oriented upward, and the field F(x,y,z)=(2x,0,3z+1)\mathbf F(x,y,z)=(2x,0,3z+1). Its tangents are (1,0,1)(1,0,1) and (0,1,0)(0,1,0), so their cross product is (−1,0,1)(-1,0,1). The positive third component confirms that this order gives the required orientation. On the patch, F=(2u,0,3u+1)\mathbf F=(2u,0,3u+1), so the local dot product is −2u+3u+1=u+1-2u+3u+1=u+1. Integrate over the parameter square:

Φ=∫01∫01(u+1) dv du=∫01(u+1) du=32.\Phi=\int_0^1\int_0^1(u+1)\,dv\,du=\int_0^1(u+1)\,du=\tfrac32.

To check the area scale independently, the unit normal is (−1,0,1)/2(-1,0,1)/\sqrt2, so the scalar projection is (u+1)/2(u+1)/\sqrt2. The sloped patch has dS=2 du dvdS=\sqrt2\,du\,dv and the factors cancel in the integral. Replacing the sloped patch by its floor projection and simply using the vertical component would miss the horizontal field crossing the sloped face.

Check your understanding

At P=(1,2,0)P=(1,2,0) in the worked potential, what is the rate of change along (0,3,4)(0,3,4)?

Normalize first: (0,3,4)/5(0,3,4)/5. The gradient is (6,2,−1)(6,2,-1), so the directional derivative is (0+6−4)/5=2/5(0+6-4)/5=2/5.

Use the same sloped patch but the constant field G=(2,0,2)\mathbf G=(2,0,2). Is its upward flux positive, zero, or negative?

Zero. The oriented element is (−1,0,1) du dv(-1,0,1)\,du\,dv and G⋅(−1,0,1)=−2+2=0\mathbf G\cdot(-1,0,1)=-2+2=0 everywhere. A positive vertical component does not imply positive flux through a tilted surface.
