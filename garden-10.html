A sentence in time to act

A shopper searches for a product, chooses a seller, and clicks through. A privacy grade appears before the store opens. It is legible, and the shopper understands it. But the competing stores and their grades are no longer on the screen. To act on what the label says, the shopper must abandon a choice already under way, return to the results, and try again. The same sentence beside the original results would have become part of the first choice. Nothing about reading ability has changed. The information has arrived at a different point in an action.

A label, instruction, or reply has a useful life of its own. It may come too late to guide a comparison; it may be found in time but call for a step the reader cannot perform; it may be legible and actionable while appearing to promise something the writer never meant. These are different ways for correct-looking text to fail, and each becomes visible at a different point in the exchange.

Where someone will look

In an experiment with 89 shoppers, Egelman et al. (2009) displayed online sellers’ privacy grades in different places. Some shoppers saw grades alongside search results; others saw them after clicking a seller, either in a frame or in a message before reaching the site. Among people who ultimately picked the highest-privacy seller, those who encountered the grades after clicking visited more results along the way. Their purchases could look identical at the end. What differed was the work required to find and compare the stores. The analysis is conditional on reaching that seller, so it does not show that all shoppers wanted the highest privacy grade.

In a medicine leaflet, the cost of a misplaced fact can look like a misunderstanding even when the sentence itself is easy to read. Pander Maat and Lentz (2010) asked people to use three leaflets to answer questions drawn from practical scenarios. With the original leaflets, readers found about three quarters of the material they needed. When they had located it, they understood about nine tenths. Asking people to paraphrase a paragraph that the researcher has already found removes the harder part of the task.

A follow-up study tested ways of organizing the standard European leaflets with 128 Dutch and British readers (Pander Maat et al., 2015). Its mistakes reveal two ways to organize a document: according to where each provision belongs in a regulatory template, or according to the problem that brought the reader to the page. Participants looking for what to do about stopping a medicine often expected to find the answer under a heading about problems. The relevant subsection was under the heading about taking the medicine. A person with a question does not start with a mental map of the document. The heading has to work from the question forward.

Explaining is one step; doing is another

At three primary care clinics, Davis et al. (2006) asked 395 English-speaking adults to interpret instructions on prescription labels. One task also required participants to demonstrate the total taken in a day. Among readers at or below a sixth-grade reading level, 70.7% correctly stated the instruction for two tablets twice daily; 34.7% correctly demonstrated the daily total. The instruction was already in front of them in both tasks. The difference appeared after the reading, when the words had to be used to calculate a quantity.

What is available at that point can matter as much as the wording. In a randomized study of liquid medication measurement, 491 parents and caregivers encountered labels with or without pictograms and measured different targets with a cup and two syringe sizes (Yin et al., 2017). The task was judged by what participants actually dispensed, not what volume they could say aloud. For the 2 mL target, deviations of more than 20% occurred on 10.9% of trials with the 5 mL syringe and 26.5% with the 10 mL syringe. At 7.5 mL the order reversed, 28.4% versus 9.8%. The smaller syringe could not hold the larger target in a single filling. A tool that helped with one part of the task increased the work of another.

An improvement in demonstrating a regimen need not last outside the room. Wolf et al. (2016) assigned 845 patients to receive standard labels or more readable labels that described prescription timing in named parts of the day. At the nine-month assessment, 85.9% in the redesigned group demonstrated proper use, compared with 77.4% in the standard group. The researchers did not detect a corresponding overall improvement on adherence measures. The redesign had made it easier for some patients to show how a set of prescriptions fit into a day. Whether the schedule was followed across many days remained a separate question.

The agreement that exists only on one side

In one of the translated conversations studied by Robertson and Díaz (2022), a parent asked a prospective caretaker about a child’s cashew allergy and the use of an epinephrine autoinjector. The Igbo translation led the caretaker to think the parent was asking about a stubborn child. The response reassured the parent, who understood it as a reply about the allergy. The caretaker discovered the mismatch during the researchers’ later review. The parent had not simply failed to understand a sentence. Each speaker understood a different encounter, and the exchange gave neither an immediate reason to stop.

A polished reply can hide the same kind of break. Suppose a venue’s staff member says that a side entrance is usually open and that they will check whether it is open this evening. If a rewrite instead says the visitor can use the side entrance, the hesitation has become permission. Nothing in the second sentence is hard to read. Its clarity may even make it harder for the visitor to recognize that waiting would be sensible. An evaluation could ask what the writer meant to confirm, ask what the reader understood to be confirmed, and see which route the reader took.

Even a single word about uncertainty can do more social work than its writer expects. In Bonnefon and Villejoubert’s (2006) consultation scenarios, “possible” described either deafness or insomnia. Among respondents who considered deafness the worse news, 60% took the wording as tact in that scenario, compared with 17.4% in the insomnia scenario. A message may need to distinguish what is known from how bad the outcome would be. Otherwise the reader may use the seriousness of the news to infer how certain the sender really is.

A useful reading test starts with a person who has a reason to look for something, or a reason not to know that there is anything to find. It follows what happens after the answer appears. Did the shopper have the alternatives in view? Could the reader perform the next step with the available tool? Did both speakers know what had been agreed? These questions take text seriously by treating it as part of an event that can go differently because someone read it.
