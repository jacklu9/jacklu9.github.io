1.1 Revision of basic mathematical concepts

Suppose we could inspect the field everywhere around the capacitor. Recording the arrows would be a beginning. We would still need to say how nearby readings differ, how much crosses a surface, and how the field aligns with a path around a loop. These are different questions; no single measure of “field strength” answers all of them. The operations below will let us ask each question without losing its geometry. Purely mathematical examples use dimensionless coordinate numbers unless units are stated; physical examples specify their quantities and units.

1.1.1 Vectors, fields, and differential operators

A field is a map of values over space. The measuring-probe map has a familiar counterpart: place small wind sensors at many positions in a room and read them at the same instant. At each position the sensor reports a speed and a direction. Drawing one arrow for each reading gives a vector field. The arrow starts at the sampling position; its length represents the measured magnitude. It does not show where a particular air particle has travelled. An electric-field map has the same mathematical structure, although its arrows describe force per unit charge rather than wind velocity.

A scalar is a single signed value, such as temperature or electric potential. A vector has components that specify magnitude and direction. A scalar field assigns a scalar to every position, and a vector field assigns a vector. With Cartesian position coordinates x, y, z and time t, write

For example, F = (−y, x, 0) means Fx = −y, Fy = x, and Fz = 0: at (1, 0, 0) its arrow points along +y, while at (0, 1, 0) it points along −x.

A field line is a curve whose tangent follows the field direction at each point of one snapshot. A trajectory records the positions occupied by an object as time passes. They coincide for particles carried by a steady velocity field, but need not coincide for a changing velocity field. An electric-field line is not generally the trajectory of a charged particle: the electric field affects its acceleration, and its existing velocity matters.

The position vector tells us where we sample. The field vector F (r, t) tells us the value there. These vectors need not point in the same direction. A field is uniform if its value does not change with position, and static if it does not change with time. 1.1.2 The gradient of a scalar field

Some maps begin with numbers rather than arrows. Electric potential is one such map: before it can tell us an electrostatic field direction, we need to understand how a number changes as we move. A contour map makes that question visible.

Read a contour map before calculating a derivative. A scalar field gives a value at every location, but supplies no arrow of its own. For a field on a plane, draw contours joining locations with equal values. Walking along a contour changes position without changing the field value. Crossing contours changes the value. For equal contour increments, closely spaced contours mean a larger change per unit distance.

One can also draw the scalar value as the height of a graph. That height axis represents the value f, not a third physical position coordinate. The gradient of a two-dimensional field is a direction in the map’s plane: it tells us where to walk to make the graph rise fastest. For a physical three-dimensional scalar field f(x, y, z), the analogous equal-value objects are level surfaces, and the gradient is a vector in physical three-dimensional space.

A perpendicular direction gives zero first-order change. The steepest direction therefore follows from projection, rather than being a separate definition imposed on the formula.

Along a level surface f = constant, every tangent displacement satisfies df = 0, hence ∇f ·dr = 0. Where the gradient is nonzero it must be normal to the surface. At a point where ∇f = 0, all first-order directional derivatives vanish; this does not by itself say that the field is constant nearby, or distinguish a maximum from a minimum or a saddle point.
