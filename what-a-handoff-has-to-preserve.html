The next person needs more than “done”

A field guide to passing work between people, from a cockpit dial to an unfinished print job.

The day shift at a print shop leaves six posters beside a cutter. A note says, “First batch done.” The evening operator can count six. That will not answer whether the posters are printed, trimmed, or ready to pack. Each meaning changes what the operator should do now. A good handoff would let someone see the difference without having to call the person who left.

Put the target beside the thing being checked

Edwin Hutchins followed how an aircrew used adjustable markers on an airspeed dial. The crew had already obtained the relevant target speed. Setting a marker put that value on the display where the pilot could see it beside the moving pointer. Checking the relationship became a visual judgment rather than a matter of remembering the number and then locating it on the dial. The marker was useful because of where the crew encountered it and what it could be compared with. In the print shop, a sample poster trimmed to the agreed edge, placed beside the untrimmed stack, can support a similar comparison.

Keep the reason that might change the decision

Gabriel Murray and colleagues gave users a decision audit: work across several recorded meetings to find out how and why a particular decision was made. That task asks something more exacting of a summary than listing the topics people discussed. A decision may be intelligible only beside the alternative the group rejected. Suppose the print shop left a broad margin because the posters were originally meant to fit a particular frame. If the customer is bringing smaller frames, the operator can tell that the margin needs review. A note that says only “use the wider margin” passes on the conclusion but gives no clue about when it should be questioned.

Leave an open step visibly open

A finished print, an approved trim, and a packed order are three separate states. A late change can become a warning that arrives after the decision if it reaches the operator only once the work has been cut. A record can attach each to evidence: the stack on the bench, a confirmed sample, or a labeled carton. When the evidence is absent, recording what remains uncertain gives the next operator something specific to check. That is the same advantage as a well-aimed repair in conversation, directed here at the state of a job rather than a misheard word.

A handoff test could change one fact between shifts, such as the frame size. Then see whether a new operator can locate the current work, preserve what still fits the job, and stop the next irreversible step if the old basis no longer holds. Faster reading would be welcome. The telling observation would be whether the operator catches the change before bringing the blade down.
