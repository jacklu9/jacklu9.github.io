What a draft lets a person discover

A timber-framing student cutting a joint finds a knot at the place where the joint is supposed to carry a load. A drawing might show exactly where to cut and still have nothing to say about whether this length of wood should be used. In the workshop observed by Dew and Rosner (2018), the student returned the timber to the pile for a different purpose instead of finishing the planned joint. The encounter did not supply a new instruction for completing the same piece. It changed what the piece was for.

What counts as thinking in that moment? The student may already have understood why a knot in that location matters. Still, until the timber was handled and cut, the particular incompatibility was not a problem anyone had to solve. Sketches, prototypes and drafts matter partly because they can reveal a question before the maker has decided how it will be answered. If a tool makes it easier to produce the final artifact but harder to encounter such a question, the efficiency comes with a cost that the artifact need not show.

An action can serve as a question

In Tetris, rotating a piece may advance a placement selected a moment earlier, or it may let the player find out which orientation fits. Kirsh and Maglio (1994) used this difference to distinguish pragmatic actions, which move the game toward a chosen state, from epistemic actions, which change what can be known or worked out. The same button press can do either. An interface that skips the rotation as a redundant intermediate step might correctly infer the player's final goal while eliminating one way the player could have discovered it.

The same kind of action appears at a different scale in a design protocol analyzed by Suwa et al. (2000). While sketching a museum, an architect noticed a possible line of sight between the entrance and a sculpture garden. The elements were already present in the sketch; the discovery was the relation between them and the possibility that it could shape a visitor’s first view. The architect later used the sightline to think about another sketch. A record of initial requirements would miss that constraint, and the final sketch alone would not show how the architect arrived at it.

Neither case makes the external representation a substitute for judgment. A rotation has to be read as evidence of a fit. A sightline has to become significant to the visitor experience. A well-designed display can make a relation cheap to inspect without deciding whether it matters. Larkin and Simon (1987) make this distinction precise in computational terms: different representations can preserve the same information while changing the effort needed to identify related facts and make an inference. A diagram places some relations close together; a table may make others more explicit. The question is where each form places the work, not which medium is intrinsically superior.

Material can also reveal a limit in the plan imposed by a digital system. In one-to-one studies with a fabrication device, Devendorf and Ryokai (2015) translated a model into positions indicated by a moving laser dot. Participants advanced instructions themselves and deposited material by hand, with permission to depart from the path. A maker working with magnolia leaves followed the veins while retaining enough of the planned form for the experiment to continue. That was not simply a defect caused by imperfect compliance. It was a use of material knowledge the formal model had not encoded.

Compare what becomes easier to see

A second draft only helps if it lets a person see a difference worth considering. Hsee (1996) asked participants to value hypothetical used music dictionaries. One offered 10,000 entries and had an intact cover; the other offered 20,000 entries and had a torn cover. When people saw both, they on average valued the larger dictionary more highly. The means in separate evaluation ran in the other direction, though their difference by itself was not statistically significant. The interpretation is specific: a damaged cover can be noticed and judged without a comparison, while it is difficult to know whether 20,000 entries is a substantial advantage unless another dictionary makes the scale visible.

Guidance can affect whether the juxtaposition becomes a comparison at all. In one study of novice negotiators, Gentner et al. (2003) gave students the same two cases and varied whether they were asked to compare them or study each by itself. The comparison group was more likely to identify the shared strategy and apply it to a new written problem. In a separate face-to-face exercise, 18 of 20 guided-comparison pairs reported reaching a contingent agreement. Providing access to examples was not the full intervention. The participants were directed to inspect the relation between them and then had a different situation in which to use it.

The source of the alternatives also matters. In a crossover experiment, Madore et al. (2015) asked 23 adults either to recall details of an episode or complete a mathematics task before generating uses for common objects. The participants supplied more categories of appropriate uses after the detailed-recall condition on average. The experiment does not establish that any prompt to remember the past will improve a design. Ahmed et al. (2023) tried several retrieval prompts and did not find consistent improvements over the comparison conditions. A discovery interface can nevertheless test a narrower move: ask for one concrete occasion that might challenge a simple account, and look for which new constraint enters the draft, rather than crediting any increase in the number of suggestions.

For a tool that generates alternatives, these results suggest a distinction between a well-populated menu and a usable comparison. Asking for twelve improved opening paragraphs may produce twelve defensible options and leave the underlying disagreement hidden. A pair designed around a particular difference, such as whether the scene should reveal a motive before or after an action, makes a choice inspectable. If that choice turns out not to matter, the writer can formulate another. The value of the alternatives is partly that they give the maker a way to find a better question, not that they deliver a winner from the initial list.

See who could still change course

The artifact alone rarely identifies who discovered the relevant difference. Baaijen et al. (2014) measured university students’ self-rated understanding and the quality of their drafted articles separately after assigning different preparations. Baaijen and Galbraith (2018) reanalyzed the same cohort using process patterns inferred from keystrokes. Greater revision across the whole draft was associated with increased self-rated understanding; conditions also mattered for the relationship between relatively spontaneous sentence production and understanding. The same patterns did not explain all variation in rated text quality. A revision log can reveal that someone returned to a passage and reordered it. It cannot, without further evidence, say what they noticed or whether the change was caused by that return.

AI suggestions offer a more specific version of this measurement problem. The CoAuthor dataset records what 63 writers did with four configurations of GPT-3 across 1,445 writing sessions, distinguishing interactions such as opening a suggestion, accepting one and editing the resulting words (Lee et al., 2022). An accepted sentence may then be recast into a role the suggestion did not anticipate; a rejected one may give a writer a useful contrast. A later exploratory deployment with sixteen writers found that they ignored most surfaced suggestions across 66 sessions, including in a system where opening a suggestion and using it were separate choices (Zhang et al., 2026). That figure gives no simple estimate of quality. The relevant question is which opportunity to think or make a change opened, including when the writer chose to proceed alone.

It is also possible for assistance to make production easier while shifting work away from a capability that will be needed later. Grinschgl et al. (2021) studied people copying arrangements of pictured objects from a model. Easier access improved copying speed without a detected accuracy change in the first experiment; an unexpected memory test found weaker memory for objects and their positions. If a designer expects the reference to stay available, quicker access may be exactly the right improvement. If future work depends on recalling the relation without it, copying performance is not enough evidence.

A useful test for an exploratory tool can therefore give people a draft or sketch built around a genuine unresolved choice, record which alternatives they inspect and ask what became clearer before the final decision. An evaluator can compare that account with what the person does in the next version. The difference might have been made easy to notice, or the tool might simply have made one version more attractive. This is where the chapter connects to judging evidence: the record needs to show not just what was selected, but whether the new representation gave the selector a reason they could inspect and use.
