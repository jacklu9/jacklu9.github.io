Electromagnetic Theory · Chapter 1

A current without charge crossing the gap

Derive charge conservation, then use two surfaces on the same loop to see why a charging capacitor needs the displacement term.

A wire feeds the top plate of a capacitor. Across the gap, an ideal insulating layer lets no free charge through. Place a closed loop around the wire and ask what magnetic field circulates along it. A flat surface bounded by that loop cuts the wire. Another surface, stretched out like a cup from the same loop, can pass between the plates without cutting a conductor. The contour did not move, so its magnetic circulation cannot have two values. The apparent contradiction comes from counting only conduction current.

Count what stays inside a volume

Take a fixed volume VV with outward normal on its boundary. Let ρf\rho_f denote free charge per unit volume and Jf\mathbf J_f free current density. Positive outward current corresponds to charge leaving, so the charge inside changes with the opposite sign:

ddt∫Vρf dV=−∮∂VJf⋅dS.\frac{d}{dt}\int_V \rho_f\,dV=-\oint_{\partial V}\mathbf J_f\cdot d\mathbf S.

The divergence theorem converts the right-hand side into an integral of ∇⋅Jf\nabla\cdot\mathbf J_f. Since this accounting holds for any fixed volume, smooth densities obey

∇⋅Jf=−∂ρf∂t.\nabla\cdot\mathbf J_f=-\frac{\partial\rho_f}{\partial t}.

Consider a small region around one plate and the end of its wire. If 3 mA3\,\mathrm{mA} enters and none exits, the plate's free charge rises by 3 μC3\,\mathrm{\mu C} per millisecond. Charge does not have to pass through the dielectric for it to build up on the plate. Keep the minus sign tied to the outward normal: a net inward current is negative when substituted on the right-hand side of the integral balance.

Why the curl equation needs another term

The magnetostatic law ∇×H=Jf\nabla\times\mathbf H=\mathbf J_f applies when the fields and currents are steady. Taking the divergence of its two sides gives 0=∇⋅Jf0=\nabla\cdot\mathbf J_f, since the divergence of a curl vanishes. That condition is incompatible with accumulating free charge. The electric Gauss law supplies the quantity whose time derivative has precisely the missing divergence:

∇⋅D=ρf⟹∇⋅∂D∂t=∂ρf∂t.\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=\rho_f\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\nabla\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf D}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial\rho_f}{\partial t}.

The Ampère–Maxwell law includes that term. Its divergence is now zero because the two current densities satisfy the charge balance together:

∇×H=Jf+∂D∂t.\nabla\times\mathbf H=\mathbf J_f+\frac{\partial\mathbf D}{\partial t}.

This calculation checks consistency; it does not, by itself, derive the entire electromagnetic law from conservation. A divergence condition constrains one property of a field but does not determine its curl or its divergence-free part. Maxwell's law specifies the actual field relation. The unit check is instructive: D\mathbf D is measured in C m−2\mathrm{C\,m^{-2}}, so its time derivative is measured in A m−2\mathrm{A\,m^{-2}}, just like free current density.

Four laws, two distinct jobs

The two Gauss laws constrain flux through closed surfaces. Electric displacement has free charge as its source; the total magnetic flux through a closed surface is zero. The two curl laws concern circulation around a boundary. A changing magnetic flux drives electric circulation with Faraday's minus sign; free current and changing electric displacement drive magnetic circulation.

∇⋅D=ρf,∇⋅B=0,∇×E=−∂B∂t,∇×H=Jf+∂D∂t.\begin{aligned} \nabla\cdot\mathbf D&=\rho_f, &\nabla\cdot\mathbf B&=0,\\ \nabla\times\mathbf E&=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t}, & \nabla\times\mathbf H&=\mathbf J_f+\frac{\partial\mathbf D}{\partial t}. \end{aligned}

Here E\mathbf E is electric field and B\mathbf B is magnetic flux density. In simple linear, isotropic media, D=ϵE\mathbf D=\epsilon\mathbf E and B=μH\mathbf B=\mu\mathbf H. Those material relations are separate from the four field laws. Likewise, if a material conducts ohmically, Jf=σE\mathbf J_f=\sigma\mathbf E describes the conduction part only where there are no additional impressed currents.

A separate check on Faraday’s sign

Place a stationary loop in the xyxy plane and choose its normal along +z^+\hat{\mathbf z}. Viewed from above, the positive contour runs anticlockwise. Let magnetic flux in the positive direction increase at 2.0 mWb s−12.0\,\mathrm{mWb\,s^{-1}}. The circulation ∮E⋅dℓ\oint\mathbf E\cdot d\boldsymbol{\ell} is then −2.0 mV-2.0\,\mathrm{mV} in the chosen orientation. Reversing the normal reverses the orientation of the contour and the sign of the reported circulation, not the electric field itself.

This is a different comparison from finding the voltage at one point. Faraday’s law gives the sum of tangential field contributions around the closed path. In a changing magnetic field that sum can be nonzero; the familiar zero circulation of an electrostatic field is recovered only when the linked flux is constant.

Worked example: put the surface through the dielectric

Imagine square plates of area A=0.010 m2A=0.010\,\mathrm{m^2} and separation d=0.50 mmd=0.50\,\mathrm{mm}, with a linear dielectric of permittivity ϵ=4ϵ0\epsilon=4\epsilon_0. In the uniform-field approximation away from the edges, Dn=ϵV/dD_n=\epsilon V/d with the voltage sign chosen to make the field point from the positive plate into the gap. Let the applied voltage rise at a fixed rate of 100 V s−1100\,\mathrm{V\,s^{-1}}. With the surface oriented to count the charging current positively, the gap surface gives

Id=∫A∂D∂t⋅dS=ϵAddVdt=4(8.85×10−12)(0.010)5.0×10−4(100)≈70.8 nA.\begin{aligned} I_d&=\int_A\frac{\partial \mathbf D}{\partial t}\cdot d\mathbf S =\frac{\epsilon A}{d}\frac{dV}{dt}\\ &=\frac{4(8.85\times10^{-12})(0.010)}{5.0\times10^{-4}}(100) \approx70.8\,\mathrm{nA}. \end{aligned}

The same idealization gives C=ϵA/d≈708 pFC=\epsilon A/d\approx708\,\mathrm{pF}. On the wire side, Q=CVQ=CV, so the free current is dQ/dt=C dV/dt=70.8 nAdQ/dt=C\,dV/dt=70.8\,\mathrm{nA}. Instead of asking which surface is correct, use the integral curl law on either surface. The integrand is the sum of free current density and the displacement term. In the lumped, quasistatic model the wire cut counts free current and the gap cut counts the changing displacement flux. In a real field calculation fringing or displacement outside the gap can matter, and it is always the full sum that must yield the same circulation for consistently oriented surfaces with the same boundary.

∮∂SH⋅dℓ=∫S(Jf+∂D∂t)⋅dS.\oint_{\partial S}\mathbf H\cdot d\boldsymbol{\ell}=\int_S\left(\mathbf J_f+\frac{\partial\mathbf D}{\partial t}\right)\cdot d\mathbf S.

The geometry makes the surface comparison explicit. Join the gap surface to the reversed wire surface along their common rim. They now enclose a volume, with opposite orientations on the original contours. The difference between the two currents is the net flux of Jf+∂D/∂t\mathbf J_f+\partial\mathbf D/\partial t through this closed surface. Its divergence vanishes by charge balance and Gauss’s law, so the difference is zero. That argument does not depend on the surfaces being flat or perpendicular to the local field.

A constant nonzero voltage on an ideal capacitor produces a nonzero electric field but no displacement current: D\mathbf D only contributes through its derivative. Contrast that with changing the polarity. During a downward voltage ramp an oriented calculation gives a negative displacement flux rate, in step with the reversal of the wire current. An insulation model that includes leakage adds conduction through the gap as a distinct contribution. To compare leakage and charging at a specified frequency, use the phasor lesson.

Check your understanding

A fixed test volume initially contains 1.0 μC1.0\,\mathrm{\mu C}. Conventional current of 4 mA4\,\mathrm{mA} enters and 1 mA1\,\mathrm{mA} leaves for 0.20 ms0.20\,\mathrm{ms}. What is the final charge?

The net outward current is 1−4=−3 mA1-4=-3\,\mathrm{mA}, so enclosed charge grows by (3 mA)(0.20 ms)=0.60 μC(3\,\mathrm{mA})(0.20\,\mathrm{ms})=0.60\,\mathrm{\mu C}. The final charge is 1.60 μC1.60\,\mathrm{\mu C}.

In the plate example, what happens to the gap displacement term immediately after the applied voltage finishes ramping and stays fixed, assuming the field has settled within the model?

It becomes zero because ∂D/∂t=0\partial\mathbf D/\partial t=0. The electric displacement itself can remain nonzero, and so can the free charge on the plates.
