The part that went wrong

A missed word, an orangutan’s second gesture, and the difference between repeating a signal and fixing an exchange.

The stagehand calls for the blue strip. At the other end of the workbench, a colleague picks up a blue strip of tape.

“Blue fabric?”

“Yes, the long piece.”

That second turn tells the stagehand what survived: the colleague heard the color and probably understood “strip.” Only the material was in doubt. A general “What?” could have worked, but more of the original request would have had to be said again. The exchange stays small because one person can name the part that went wrong and the other can work from that diagnosis.

How much needs fixing?

Mark Dingemanse and colleagues compared recordings of ordinary conversations in twelve languages. In each, listeners could flag a problem broadly, ask for a particular part, or offer an interpretation for the speaker to confirm. The choice changes the next person’s work. A broad request may call for repeating an entire turn. A candidate interpretation can make a yes or no sufficient. The researchers found that people tended to use the most specific form the situation let them use. Someone whose hearing was blocked could have no useful candidate to offer.

Erica Cartmill and Richard Byrne set up a different comparison with captive orangutans gesturing to a human experimenter for food. When the ape received part of the preferred food, it tended to keep using gestures it had already tried. When it received an unwanted kind, it tended to try a wider range of gestures. The outcome of the first attempt changed what happened in the second. The useful comparison with human conversation is narrow: a response can carry information about what to try next. In the human recordings, the listener could also point to exactly which part of an utterance was doubtful. The food experiment did not test anything like asking which word was meant.

A work surface can answer back

In a series of studies by Darren Gergle, Robert Kraut, and Susan Fussell, one person guided another through a collaborative puzzle. The researchers varied whether and how promptly the guide could see the other person's workspace. With an immediate view, the guide could see whether the piece had landed where expected. A change in the arrangement was itself a reply, one that could prompt the next instruction or show why a correction was needed. The actor’s purpose need not be inferred from every trace of their movement; the difference between a trace, a cue, and an intentional control matters whenever the evidence starts with a body in motion. With a delayed or missing view, part of that work returned to conversation.

Those studies suggest a distinction for systems that mediate talk: confidence about the sender’s sentence is different from evidence about where the exchange broke. A captioning tool might preserve the line the listener caught and highlight only the uncertain noun. If the listener sees that two items have the same color, the system could offer them side by side instead of making the speaker restate the request. In the design of a handoff people can act on, the shared view matters for a related reason: it shows what already changed and what still needs a response.

When an apparent repair makes things worse

Now make the stagehand a person using a speech-generating device. The next suggested turn might be a smooth elaboration, with an explanation that was never intended. A repair interface can offer a narrow correction or keep the original wording editable; either would leave the speaker a way to put back the part that mattered. The question is not simply whether the system can say more, but whether it can leave enough of the live exchange available for the people in it to fix. A hint can leave a choice open; a system that paraphrases the hint into an instruction may remove that choice before anyone can correct it.
