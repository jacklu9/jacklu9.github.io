When a machine can be redirected

A robot reaching for a cup can make progress before the person working beside it knows which cup it intends to pick up. Once the person recognizes the target, they can begin collecting what goes with it. If the robot reaches for the wrong cup, however, the same insight has a different value: it creates time to intervene. The two situations can look identical until the person acts. A machine can communicate its intention early without yet giving a person a useful way to change it.

A direction becomes visible before the destination

The shortest or most customary path to a cup may also be the least informative one. Two cups can be close enough that a reach begins in much the same way for either. A movement that first veers toward one cup can communicate the goal sooner, even when the route itself is less expected. Dragan et al. (2013) separated the ability to infer the goal from the ability to predict a trajectory given the goal. A movement that is good for one judgment need not be good for the other.

In a collaboration experiment, Dragan et al. (2015) asked 18 people to gather ingredients while a robot selected the appropriate cup. With a reach designed to reveal the goal, participants began reaching for the correct ingredients or named the intended cup 33% sooner on the study’s coordination-time measure than with the more predictable motion. The difference in full task time between those two designs was not statistically reliable. Watching the start of the movement and recording when the person could act exposed something the endpoint alone would have missed.

Being able to act on a movement also requires that the physical mechanism respond as expected. Icli et al. (2020) calibrated a six-axis industrial arm with a portable fixture and independently checked 500 other poses with a laser tracker. The new calibration yielded a mean position error of 0.326 mm and a maximum of 0.624 mm. A model can predict that its own estimated joint angles reach its intended location; the laser measurement tests the location the arm physically reaches. A favorable result there answers a question about the mechanism. It does not say whether a person has time to recognize a problematic action.

A gesture can move the arm or change its objective

When a person pushes down on an arm carrying a cup high above a table, the robot might yield and then return to its previous path. It might instead keep carrying the cup lower after the person lets go. Bajcsy et al. (2017) compared those two ways of responding in a laboratory study with ten participants. They demonstrated what should happen: keep a cup upright, keep it low, or avoid passing over a laptop. Where the controller adjusted its subsequent path in response to the person’s touch, participants spent less time intervening and came closer to the demonstrated motion. They did not rate that controller as more predictable. Seeing whether the change has taken remains part of the work, even when it takes less effort to produce.

There is more than one way to misunderstand a push. In a follow-up experiment with twelve participants, Bobu et al. (2018) designed cup-carrying tasks in which the requested correction concerned either a property the robot could represent or a property missing from its model. For example, a model that has no setting for preferred height has to explain a downward movement using the properties it does have. A controller that reduced the weight given to poorly explained corrections made fewer unwanted changes in the missing-property tasks. The system did not acquire the missing concept. It was less likely to treat the person as requesting a change to some other part of the movement.

Other gestures make sense only together. Li et al. (2021) asked nine participants to correct two robot arms transporting a grocery bag. The person had to change the destination and preserve the bag’s shape, but could move only one arm at a time. A push bringing one arm toward the destination could temporarily squeeze or stretch the bag; moving the other arm then restored the intended spacing. Reading each push as a self-contained instruction lost that relationship. On the recorded bag trials, a model that read the full sequence identified the assigned objective in 82% of trials; a model treating each correction separately reached 31%, and one reading only the final corrected trajectory reached 53%. Those numbers describe recognition of the objective, not overall success carrying a bag in someone’s kitchen. The experiment shows what the sequence carries that the isolated gesture does not.

Control someone is ready to use

A person who requested an action may not be ready to take over its execution. Endsley and Kaber (1999) compared ways of assigning parts of a computer-based target task to people and automation. When automation stopped, a message and a tone announced it. The researchers measured how long the person took to make an appropriate manual move. Recovery was relatively slow in two configurations that let actions queue, even though the person chose the order in one and the system chose it in the other. Preparing a plan is one way to stay involved. Executing it one action at a time leaves the operator in a different position when automation becomes unavailable.

An assistive arm shows why people need the option to make that division at a smaller scale than an entire task. Twelve power-wheelchair users in Styler et al. (2025) compared continuous joystick control with a mode in which they steered toward a marker and triggered visual guidance for particular actions. All guided attempts at opening a cabinet succeeded, against about four in ten manual attempts. The difference was far smaller for filling and retracting the cup. Six people preferred guidance and five preferred manual control; one had no clear preference. Participants could value handing off a difficult wrist movement and still want to choose how to approach the object. Their reasons do not fall neatly into a single preference for or against automation.

It is tempting to treat a person’s right to override as settled once a system provides a button. The button is useful only in relation to an activity. The operator needs to see what is happening, distinguish it from instructions that have not yet run, and make a change before the particular opportunity passes. If a person steers the arm aside and then releases it, the next motion shows whether the intervention applied only to that instant. If the task later changes, the new situation shows whether the earlier correction has been carried too far. Knowing how the arm moves, knowing what the controller inferred, and retaining the option to change its course are different parts of making the same machine usable.
