Who gets to name an improvement?

A technology can succeed at the task on the score sheet and miss the reason someone wanted it.

A consultation ends with a recommendation for an assistive device. The follow-up form can ask whether a person received it, whether they use it, and whether it helps them get around. One Australian client described something more specific: being able to go out with their grandson without arranging for a third person to accompany them (De Jonge & Stevens, 2016). A shorter trip or a higher rating for mobility would not by itself say who could now come along, or who could decide to go.

De Jonge and Stevens followed up with 127 people two to four months after an assistive-technology consultation. They interviewed people who had not obtained the recommended equipment as well as those who had. In some accounts the relevant obstacle was outside the device: an unfinished modification at home, or a workplace where the device had to fit alongside existing equipment. An assessment restricted to successful users would not register those obstacles as things the service needed to address. The same problem arises when a collection point appears on the map but a resident returns empty-handed.

A fair comparison can use different tasks

The Individually Prioritised Problem Assessment (Wessels et al., 2002) asks each person which activities cause difficulty and returns to those activities after they receive equipment. It does not require everyone to care about walking the same distance or performing one standard sequence. Comparability comes from asking whether the chosen difficulty changed, not from imposing a common reason for seeking assistance.

Putting more of someone’s own priorities into a session does not necessarily make their everyday activities easier. Knippenberg et al. (2021) conducted a randomized study across four centers in Belgium. One group received the centers’ usual rehabilitation. The other added a six-week program with a Kinect system configured for the activities participants selected. About 88% of personal goals entered practice in the added program, as against 46% under usual care. Participants’ ratings of how well they performed their chosen activities, and of how satisfied they were with that performance, did not differ significantly by group. More of their aims reached the sessions; the result they reported in their own terms did not establish a corresponding gain.

The appropriate test also depends on whether the aid is meant to stay. An instrument can give someone additional things to do while they play it. A practice prompt can instead help a learner recognize a step they will later need to perform on their own. In the first case, removing the equipment may remove the opportunity being tested. In the second, seeing what the learner can do on a changed problem gives evidence that the support worked. A designer can specify where the gain is supposed to appear, while the people who will live with the outcome retain a say in what gain is worth the trouble.
