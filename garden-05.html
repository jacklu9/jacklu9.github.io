A finished worksheet is not the end of the lesson

Two pupils hand in the same correct solution. One asked a chat tool what was wrong with the step she had already written. The other entered the question, copied the answer, and moved on. Nothing in the finished work identifies which pupil now knows what to do when the problem changes. A teacher who wants to find that out needs another task. The interesting question is not simply whether the technology helped. It is what the pupils still have to do for themselves while it helps, and what they can do after that moment has passed.

A completed task is a legitimate benefit. Someone filling out a form or finding an address may never need to repeat the operation without help. In a lesson, the teacher has a different reason for choosing the task. The algebra exercise can stand for a move the student will need again, perhaps in a situation where nobody points out that this is the move to make. A tool can improve today’s answer without giving the pupil much chance to practise recognizing that situation. This is why the lesson changes when the outcome changes from the answer turned in to the next problem the student meets.

One model, two places to begin

Bastani et al. (2025) studied mathematics review at a Turkish high school, where pupils could use their notes and textbooks during practice. One group also had a general GPT-4 chat interface that was given the problem. A second had a version equipped with teacher-informed solutions, likely errors, and hints, and instructed to guide the pupil rather than hand over the answer. Both groups did better during practice than pupils working without the chat tool. The estimated gains were roughly 14 and 36 points, respectively, on the 100-point practice scale.

The later exam gave a different account of the same lessons. The general chat group scored around 17 percent lower relative to the mean of the group that had practised without AI. The test did not detect a comparable loss or a clear gain for pupils using the structured tutor. The practice conversations offer some clues: requests for complete answers were more common with the general interface; responses based on an attempted solution were more common with the structured one. These are clues about both the conversation and the materials behind it. It would take a separate comparison to assign the exam difference specifically to the rule about giving answers, because the structured tutor also had access to different guidance.

Putting the model on the other side of the lesson changes who has to make the next move. In an online tutoring experiment, Wang et al. (2025) offered tutors suggestions about how to respond to students’ mistakes. A tutor could choose another teaching move or edit the wording before sending it. Students whose tutors had access passed approximately 66 percent of the checks at the end of lessons, against 62 percent in the comparison group. Tutors using the system as a group asked more probing questions and offered less generic praise. This design gave the adult a possible way to respond and left the learner with an exercise to answer. The result concerns the end of the lesson; it does not settle how those learners did months later. It does suggest that the same idea for an explanation can serve a different instructional function depending on who sees it first.

The part of the problem that travels

Consider a novice programmer learning to make two network requests run concurrently. A program can appear to do its job even if it waits for one request to finish before starting the other. A later quiz can ask the novice to tell these implementations apart. A more demanding task can give them a slow program and a timing trace without mentioning concurrency at all. The first task checks whether the learner recognizes an already named contrast. The second asks whether the learner can find that contrast in circumstances where it matters.

Shen and Tamkin (2026) put a version of the first question to programmers learning a Python library. All 26 programmers allowed to use AI finished the second practice task in the allotted time; 22 of the 26 without AI finished. There was no conclusive difference on overall completion time. When the participants then took a quiz without AI, those who had practised without it scored 4.15 points higher on a 27-point scale. The record is short: practice followed by a quiz in the same session. A finding about what novices learned during that exercise is not a finding about the disappearance of a skill they already had. Nor does it say whether they could use a reference manual well when faced with an unfamiliar program.

A test does not have to take every tool away to find out whether someone learned. In a statistics classroom, Schwartz and Martin (2004) had ninth graders either invent a way to compare scores drawn from different distributions or learn a demonstrated procedure. At test time some students also received a worked example. On a later question that did not direct students back to the example, about 61 percent of the invention group who had received it produced an acceptable answer, compared with 29 percent of the group that had practised the demonstrated procedure and received the same example. Without the example, the figures were about 30 and 32 percent. The difference is consistent with the earlier invention task giving students something to notice when they encountered the new material. If the aim is to prepare a person for future learning, letting them read a new example can reveal a difference that a closed-book test would miss. What matters is whether the reference provides information they can put to work, rather than quietly doing the operation the test was designed to observe.

When the system goes away by accident

There is no tidy exam at many workplaces. New staff learn while working, experienced staff face interruptions, and the assistance becomes part of a routine. When Brynjolfsson et al. (2025) studied the rollout of a generative assistant to more than 5,000 customer-support agents, they examined periods when major outages made the assistant unavailable. Workers who had previously received access handled chats faster during those outages than they had before the introduction. That difference was clearer after months of exposure than after the first month. Handling time was observable; whether each individual issue had been solved was not. The mix of cases during an outage could also differ. Still, it is a useful counterpart to a classroom test: a tool can be present long enough for people to pick up a way of working they later use in its absence.

The outage raises a question a workplace could test on purpose. When a new type of complaint comes in, does the agent know which records to inspect, which earlier rule no longer applies, and when to ask a colleague? A fast exchange might reflect any of these skills, or it might simply be a fast reply. The test can give everyone the same policy documents and record the decisions that connect those documents to the customer’s problem.

The aim is not to turn every useful tool into an exam. It is to decide what part of a task a person should get to practise. A worked line can free a novice from arithmetic long enough to see the comparison that matters. A fully written solution can make that same comparison disappear into a result. The difference will be easier to see on the next problem than on the one that has already been solved.
