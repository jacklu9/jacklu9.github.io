Where did the missing cases go?

An AI screening system, a benefits office, and a protected spring make the boundary of an evaluation visible.

At three clinics in Thailand, a diabetes eye-screening system rejected 393 of 1,838 photographs in six months. A nurse could take another picture; some images the system could not grade were legible to a nurse. Under the initial protocol, an ungradable picture could send a patient to a specialist. The study subsequently routed such images to an ophthalmologist for review before deciding on referral (Beede et al., 2020). Whatever accuracy the system achieved on the photographs it graded, an evaluation restricted to them would leave out the path taken by some patients and the repeated work done by clinic staff.

This is a problem with the denominator, but it starts before the arithmetic. What constitutes a case? Is it a photograph, a screening visit, or a person who is eventually screened? The report can start from any of those units and be internally correct. Each gives a different answer about the service.

Find the step that controls admission to the count

In a field experiment covering roughly 65,000 food-assistance applications in Los Angeles County, Giannella et al. (2024) changed how applicants could finish a required interview. Applicants in both groups received a scheduled appointment and faced the same verification requirement. The treatment group could also call at a time of their choosing. Approvals rose by about six percentage points, and longer-run participation rose by more than two points.

One view of the system starts after an applicant makes it through the interview. It can tell whether the office assessed documented eligibility properly. Another view begins with people who applied. It sees the effect of adding a way to supply the same information, even though the eligibility test did not change. The studies in the benefits office and the eye-screening clinic intervene at different points: one changes the opportunity to enter the decision process, while the other changes a fallback once an automated judgment cannot be made. In both, success on cases that complete the ordinary path is compatible with some people never receiving the intended service.

That missingness can look like silence. During the 2019 Ridgecrest earthquake sequence, the public app then serving Los Angeles County did not notify users for either of two events. The threshold for that county was not reached in observations for the first event, but it was for the second, which the system had underestimated (Chung et al., 2020). No notification cannot tell an investigator which situation occurred. An administrator could make the same mistake by assuming an incomplete interview means someone was not eligible. In both settings the next source has to distinguish an appropriate absence from a missed opportunity.

Follow the change to the place people experience it

In western Kenya, a study of spring protection measured water at the spring and in homes. Measured fecal contamination fell by about two thirds at protected sources and by about a quarter in water sampled at home. Households that had used several sources shifted about twenty-one percentage points of their trips toward the protected spring, without a detectable increase in their total collection trips (Kremer et al., 2011). The movement toward an improved source and the difference between source and household water are separate observations. The first says how people changed their use; the second says how much of the gain was present at the point they would encounter the water.

That distinction is useful for any service whose dashboard ends before the person does. Counting visits to an upgraded point records something real, but should not be mistaken for counting households with improved water. Counting fewer referrals could mean a better second review, or a narrowing of who reaches screening in the first place. Likewise, moving a previously scheduled interview to a time someone can use changes their opportunity before the eligibility decision; it does not describe whether every approved recipient finds the benefit straightforward to use.

A study can preserve the layers by following one group through successive steps: who approached a service, who could take part, what the system could assess, what a fallback resolved, and what outcome reached the person. It should ask whether staff had to add work and whether users had to make additional trips. Efficiency can improve for one group while costs move to another. Where the system does reach a decision, the next question is whether someone can challenge it and have the finding reach the point with power to change the result. Where it reaches no decision, the question is more basic: whether anyone is counting the person left waiting.
