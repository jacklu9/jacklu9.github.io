The audit needs ordinary cases

A worked example shows why a well-chosen set of hard cases can find failures without measuring how common they are.

Two automatic reviewers examine a thousand summaries. They disagree on one hundred. A human audit of twenty disputed cases finds eight errors. Is the error rate forty percent? That number is a useful first answer about the sample, but the audit has not looked anywhere the reviewers agreed.

Here is an illustrative second step. Inspect twenty randomly drawn summaries from the nine hundred agreements and find one error. Forty percent of the disagreements and five percent of the agreements were wrong in these samples. A rough estimate for the full collection weights those results by how common each group was: one tenth times forty percent, plus nine tenths times five percent, yields 8.5 percent. Combining the forty audited summaries without weights would give 22.5 percent, incorrectly counting agreements and disagreements as equally common. Neither value is an exact population count; the small random samples leave substantial uncertainty.

An audit has more than one job

FaithBench recruited expert annotators for cases selected because automatic detectors had disagreed about candidate summaries (Bao et al., 2025). That is a useful way to find difficult failures. It is not a mistake in the design that the resulting set does not mirror everyday traffic. Difficulty and prevalence are different questions. To measure how much of the ordinary stream is affected, auditors would also need cases for which the tools reached the same verdict. Fisch et al. (2024) show a broader way to use automatic labels in evaluation: retain their value as estimates, then use a human-reviewed sample to estimate and correct their discrepancies. The benefit depends on the agreement between the automatic scores and the outcome being estimated; another vote from a nearly identical judge cannot do the work of the human check.

There is also a decision about what counts as a case. In research on repeated and remixed visual propaganda, Lutz et al. (2026) combined qualitative review of roughly 1,500 items with computational work involving about 50,000. Reused images allowed the researchers to locate related posts, but the crops, captions and arguments could differ. If an evaluation reports performance only over individual uploads, a widely repeated image can carry more weight than an equally difficult image that appeared once. If the intended claim is performance on unfamiliar imagery, the evaluation can sample or hold out visual families; if the intended claim concerns exposure during circulation, the repeated posts may belong in the estimate. The population follows the question, not whichever rows are easiest to count.

The missing cases in a public service create a different version of the same problem: a completed assessment or an apparently unremarkable output is not a neutral cutoff. An audit that discovers an interesting failure has done one useful job. Counting how often someone faces that failure starts by asking which cases the first audit never had a chance to see.
