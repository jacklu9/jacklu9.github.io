The red arrow holds the wavevector almost fixed

Our two energy differences can finally be interpreted. The 1.04 eV gap compares an endpoint at Γ with one at X. The 1.54 eV red arrow compares two endpoints at X. Light brings energy, but the interaction must also obey a momentum rule. That second requirement determines which comparison describes a direct optical transition.

In the next picture, the red arrow supplies light energy: a photon. The brown arrow supplies energy and momentum from a lattice vibration: a phonon. Their energies are labelled Eγ and Eph; qph is the phonon wavevector. Follow the marked endpoints before reading the conservation equations.

The right panel illustrates phonon absorption; emission uses Eγ − Eph = Eg and the opposite phonon-momentum sign. The joined arrows resolve energy and momentum transfer, not two real sequential states: the open corner is not a band state. Phonon energy and the tiny photon momentum are schematic, not source data; momentum conservation is modulo a reciprocal-lattice vector.

A photon is a quantum of electromagnetic radiation. Its energy is Eγ = hf = hc/λγ, where c is the speed of light, f is optical frequency and λγ is its vacuum wavelength. The subscript γ identifies the photon. In an optical transition, an electron changes from an initial state to a final state. Energy conservation gives

Crystal momentum must also be conserved, allowing transfer of a reciprocal-lattice vector. A visible/near-infrared photon has a wavevector much smaller than the reciprocal-space scale set by an atomic lattice. Neglecting this small change gives kfinal ≃ kinitial. The transition therefore connects two bands at the same horizontal coordinate: a vertical arrow.

A phonon is a quantum of a collective lattice vibration. The lattice vibration can supply or take away crystal momentum, so an indirect transition can involve both a photon and a phonon. Its energy balance also includes the phonon energy. This extra process generally makes near-edge indirect absorption weaker. “Indirect” does not mean that light cannot be absorbed, and a fundamental gap need not equal the lowest photon energy for a phonon-free transition.

A useful scale comparison For an optical wavelength of 106 m−1. A representative atomic spacing of 0.5 nm corresponds to 2π/a ≃ 1.3 × 1010 m−1. These illustrative values explain the nearly vertical approximation; a precise optical calculation can retain photon momentum and optical selection rules. Equality of wavevectors is a momentum condition, not a guarantee that every vertical transition has a large probability.

The fundamental gap is called direct when the lowest conduction state and highest valence state have the same wavevector, and indirect when they do not. Our figure has X ̸= Γ, so its fundamental gap is indirect. On these same bands, compare the photon-only process at X with a phonon-assisted process from Γ to X.

The left panel is a direct transition in an indirect-gap material. Thus “vertical transition” and “direct-gap material” are different statements. The right panel reaches the lower global edge only by exchanging momentum with the lattice.

GaAs has its usual conduction minimum and valence maximum at Γ, so it is direct. In Si, the valence maximum is at Γ but the conduction minima lie along the Γ–X directions, before X. Do not identify the minimum with X merely because the axis ends there. Conversely, a schematic exercise may deliberately place its minimum exactly at X; that is a property of the stated diagram, not a universal property of Si.

Worked example 1-1: Wavevector and direct versus indirect gaps

Question. Define wavevector and explain the distinction between the Si and GaAs band diagrams. What consequence does it have for optical transitions?

Reasoning. k labels spatial phase variation; its direction and magnitude specify the direction and rate of phase advance of the plane-wave part. An E–k graph assigns energies to those wave patterns. GaAs has coincident edge wavevectors, whereas Si has separated edge wavevectors. A near-edge optical process in Si must therefore also exchange crystal momentum with the lattice, whereas the direct edge transition in GaAs can be nearly vertical. This is the energy-and-momentum rule we just used for the original red arrow.

Reading an absorption graph A wavelength axis increases to the right, but photon energy hc/λ decreases to the right. A larger absorption coefficient means stronger absorption. The onset wavelength depends on the transition energy; for an indirect material the fundamental-gap transition is phonon assisted, so its near-edge absorption is weaker. This connects the band diagram to the wavelength-dependent absorption curve without treating “indirect” as “cannot absorb”.

The bottom of the X valley asks a new question

Suppose an electron has entered the bottom of the black valley at X. Its wavevector is nonzero, but the curve has zero slope there. Should it be moving quickly because |k| is large, or slowly because the curve is flat at its minimum?

The free-electron relation cannot simply be carried over to every crystal band. The periodic lattice changes the relation between wavevector and energy. To describe a localised electron, combine nearby wave states into a packet and follow the packet’s motion. Its speed is controlled by how their energies change with wavevector.

Crystal momentum and velocity are not generally proportional.

At the valley bottom the velocity vanishes, but an applied force can change the wavevector and move the packet onto a sloped part of the same band. The next question is how quickly the velocity then changes. This is the role of curvature.
