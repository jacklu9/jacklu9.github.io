comparison describes a direct optical transition.

The right panel illustrates phonon absorption; emission uses Eγ −Eph = Eg and the opposite
phonon-momentum sign. The joined arrows resolve energy and momentum transfer, not two real
sequential states: the open corner is not a band state. Phonon energy and the tiny photon momentum are
schematic, not source data; momentum conservation is modulo a reciprocal-lattice vector.
A photon is a quantum of electromagnetic radiation. Its energy is Eγ = hf = hc/λγ, where c
is the speed of light, f is optical frequency and λγ is its vacuum wavelength. The subscript γ
identifies the photon. In an optical transition, an electron changes from an initial state to a final
state. Energy conservation gives
Efinal −Einitial = Eγ.
Crystal momentum must also be conserved, allowing transfer of a reciprocal-lattice vector.
A
visible/near-infrared photon has a wavevector much smaller than the reciprocal-space scale set
by an atomic lattice. Neglecting this small change gives kfinal ≃kinitial. The transition therefore
connects two bands at the same horizontal coordinate: a vertical arrow.
A phonon is a quantum of a collective lattice vibration.
The lattice vibration can supply or
take away crystal momentum, so an indirect transition can involve both a photon and a phonon.
Its energy balance also includes the phonon energy.
This extra process generally makes near-
edge indirect absorption weaker. “Indirect” does not mean that light cannot be absorbed, and a
fundamental gap need not equal the lowest photon energy for a phonon-free transition.

A useful scale comparison
For an optical wavelength of 1 µm = 10−6 m, 2π/λγ ≃6.3 ×
106 m−1. A representative atomic spacing of 0.5 nm corresponds to 2π/a ≃1.3 × 1010 m−1. These
illustrative values explain the nearly vertical approximation; a precise optical calculation can retain
photon momentum and optical selection rules. Equality of wavevectors is a momentum condition,
not a guarantee that every vertical transition has a large probability.
The fundamental gap is called direct when the lowest conduction state and highest valence state
have the same wavevector, and indirect when they do not. Our figure has X ̸= Γ, so its fun-
damental gap is indirect. On these same bands, compare the photon-only process at X with a
phonon-assisted process from Γ to X.
The left panel is a direct transition in an indirect-gap material. Thus “vertical transition” and
“direct-gap material” are different statements. The right panel reaches the lower global edge only
by exchanging momentum with the lattice.
GaAs has its usual conduction minimum and valence maximum at Γ, so it is direct. In Si, the
valence maximum is at Γ but the conduction minima lie along the Γ–X directions, before X. Do
not identify the minimum with X merely because the axis ends there. Conversely, a schematic
exercise may deliberately place its minimum exactly at X; that is a property of the stated diagram,
not a universal property of Si.
Worked example 1-1: Wavevector and direct versus indirect gaps
Question. Define wavevector and explain the distinction between the Si and GaAs band diagrams.
What consequence does it have for optical transitions?
Reasoning. k labels spatial phase variation; its direction and magnitude specify the direction and
rate of phase advance of the plane-wave part. An E–k graph assigns energies to those wave patterns.
GaAs has coincident edge wavevectors, whereas Si has separated edge wavevectors. A near-edge
optical process in Si must therefore also exchange crystal momentum with the lattice, whereas the
direct edge transition in GaAs can be nearly vertical. This is the energy-and-momentum rule we
just used for the original red arrow.

Reading an absorption graph
A wavelength axis increases to the right, but photon energy
hc/λ decreases to the right.
A larger absorption coeﬀicient means stronger absorption.
The
onset wavelength depends on the transition energy; for an indirect material the fundamental-gap
transition is phonon assisted, so its near-edge absorption is weaker. This connects the band diagram
to the wavelength-dependent absorption curve without treating “indirect” as “cannot absorb”.
For a uniform absorbing material, a simple intensity law is
I(x) = I(0)e−αabsx.
I is optical power per area, x is propagation distance inside the sample, and αabs is the absorption
coeﬀicient with units of inverse length. The product αabsx is dimensionless. A larger coeﬀicient
means a shorter distance 1/αabs for the intensity to fall by a factor e. The coeﬀicient depends on
photon energy, the available initial and final states, their occupation and the transition probability.
A band-gap value alone does not specify the whole absorption spectrum.
The bottom of the X valley asks a new question
Suppose an electron has entered the bottom of the black valley at X. Its wavevector is nonzero,
but the curve has zero slope there. Should it be moving quickly because |k| is large, or slowly
because the curve is flat at its minimum?
The free-electron relation v = ¯hk/m0 cannot simply be carried over to every crystal band. The
periodic lattice changes the relation between wavevector and energy.
To describe a localised
electron, combine nearby wave states into a packet and follow the packet’s motion.
Its speed
is controlled by how their energies change with wavevector.
