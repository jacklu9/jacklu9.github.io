How a shared decision survives a handoff

Two people are sorting illustrated cards. One calls a particular card the “firecracker bird,” a name that tells an outsider almost nothing about the picture. When the speaker turns to a different partner, the same bird gets described by its white spots and where it is looking. Both descriptions work. Horton and Gerrig (2005) used cases like this to study how a speaker’s memory for previous encounters affects the way they describe something to the person now listening.

The relevant expertise is not clever naming. It is knowing which route to the object is available to which listener. A system can retrieve the nickname and still misuse it: what once efficiently referred to something is not necessarily the best thing to say in the next conversation. A successful exchange often depends on who can correct the record, which part needs correction and who is expected to act on the result.

Make mutual understanding repairable

Brennan and Clark (1996) manipulated the displays and partners in an object-description task. In the first trial after a display changed, speakers reused their established terms more often with the same partner than when partners switched, 48% versus 18% in the third experiment. The contrast was reliable by participants but marginal by items. The result supports sensitivity to the history with the listener, not an immutable rule about how an object must be named. People can keep an established term, elaborate it or set it aside depending on what the next exchange demands.

Understanding in live conversation has an advantage that cannot always be passed along as text. In Schober and Clark’s (1989) arrangement task, designated addressees interpreted figure descriptions more successfully than overhearers who received the same words and began with the same background information. An addressee can indicate that a reference does not fit and let the speaker modify it before too much work proceeds on the wrong assumption. That channel disappears for an overhearer. A conversation transcript may faithfully preserve every sentence while omitting the opportunity the participants had to test an interpretation when it mattered.

Dingemanse et al. (2015) compared repair across conversations in twelve languages. A listener could make an open request for repetition, identify the part they had not understood or offer a candidate interpretation. The more targeted forms placed a larger share of the verbal work on the person initiating repair, measured by the length of the transcribed turns. A precise repair is not necessarily long. Its advantage is that it preserves what has already been resolved and puts one disputed element back into play. A candidate only helps, however, if it can be confirmed or rejected without presenting an uncertain guess as a settled fact.

The consequences are visible in a different cooperative task. In CoDraw, one participant describes an unseen clip-art arrangement and the other reconstructs it. Kim et al. (2019) reported mean scores of 4.17 out of five for live pairs and 3.83 when human drawers received replayed directions for the same scenes. The replay preserves the teller’s words but cannot respond to what the current drawer has just put on the canvas. That is a difference in the setting of the instruction, not evidence that a live speaker is invariably more articulate.

A handoff transfers decisions, not only descriptions

In Lee’s (2007) study of a museum exhibition on dogs, an educator’s image inventory sorted material by exhibit. Graphic designers needed images organized by individual labels because each exhibit could contain more than one. The collaborators built a new table and developed conventions that made a second distinction explicit: some images were required in combination, while others were alternatives the designers could choose between. Merely reorganizing the original list would not have conveyed this discretion. The new artifact exposed the decisions that had already been made and indicated which belonged to the next person.

In a dispute over the same exhibition’s narrative, curators treated their written account as determining the organization, while educators treated it as material they could recombine. The latter narrative ultimately organized the work. A shared document did not confer a shared agreement about its authority. This is why a useful handoff might need to label a statement as a requirement, an option or an open issue. It also needs a way to say which of those labels is itself disputed.

Source and status can come apart even within one person’s memory. Johnson et al. (1993) describe source attribution as drawing on features such as contextual details and signs of the mental work involved in producing a memory. Those features can point in different directions. In Brown and Murphy’s (1989) category experiments, participants sometimes produced examples introduced by other people when asked not to repeat prior responses. The example could still be accessible while its origin was not. A design history can make the same distinction explicit by retaining the originating suggestion alongside the resulting change and the reason it was accepted. This helps the next reader assess whether a recurring idea has independent support or has simply passed through several documents.

Retrieval is not a reason to speak or act

LongMemEval tests an assistant’s ability to answer 500 questions drawn from extended conversation histories (Wu et al., 2025). Its cases include updating a previously given fact, combining information from different exchanges and recognizing that the available history does not answer a question. These are not interchangeable. Retrieval that finds an older and a newer plan is only the start of determining which plan is current. Even correct identification of a current plan does not show whether the eventual action will follow it.

There are also situations where facts are available but should not be surfaced. Sumida et al. (2026) compared direct memory questions with corresponding natural exchanges drawn from a long-term deployment. Their comparison holds the recovered history and model fixed while changing what the current prompt asks. A direct question names a target; an ordinary turn may only imply that some earlier fact is relevant. Conversely, Hu et al. (2026) designed constructed cases in which invoking a remembered detail would be off-topic or an expression of a standing preference would fit the wrong request. An evaluation that rewards every recovered detail will miss this failure mode. The remembered fact has to earn a place in the exchange.

Writing an answer that looks like a clarification is another insufficient proxy. In an evaluation using existing dialogue contexts, Shaikh et al. (2024) found that prompting the tested model for better grounding nearly quadrupled its rate of follow-up questions with little change in matching the comparison speaker’s turns. Chance-adjusted agreement on clarification turns declined. Quantity and placement were different outcomes. A request can contain enough information to proceed, only enough to propose a reversible draft, or too little to act without asking. Those are decisions a clarification test would need to capture.

Preference selection is a related problem but not the same one. Yoon et al. (2026) kept profiles available while changing the purpose and audience of drafting tasks. Annotators marked whether a preference fit a particular recipient and task, and an automated judge scored whether it appeared. A perspective suitable for a creative piece was not automatically useful in a formal record for a tenant screening organization. The benchmark allows a memory system to fail even when it accurately recalls the preference, because recall and judgment about where to apply it are different actions.

There is an analogous distinction inside a model. The tuned lens developed by Belrose et al. (2025) maps a transformer’s hidden activations at different layers to estimates of the model’s final token distribution. It describes how well an intermediate state can predict that distribution; it does not show on its own whether a particular state was required. In work on locating and editing factual associations, Hase et al. (2023) found that their intervention-based localization scores were not reliable guides to which layers produced successful edits. Reading out information, identifying causal sensitivity and making a durable change are distinct tests. By the same logic, showing that an assistant can state a past decision is not evidence that the decision will govern its next action.

Test which state survives after a change

Imagine a team developing exhibition labels. The record begins with two candidate images. Later the team chooses one for a printed panel and keeps both available to compare for the website. A successor receives the record and is asked to prepare each output. It should be possible to test whether they can identify the committed print image, preserve the online choice, explain what changed and ask about a genuinely missing credit. A summary that simply recommends one image might sound clear while discarding the decision that was delegated.

This connects to the chapter on dividing work: information has not been passed successfully merely because it left one person’s hands. The next person must be able to interpret it in the setting where it will guide a decision. It also connects to tools that make room to speak. A repair is only available if a person has the time and the means to make it before the conversation, the publication or the machine has moved on.
