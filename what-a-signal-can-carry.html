Three ways to hear a heartbeat

An acoustic experiment and a musical instrument ask different questions of the same kind of signal.

An audio editor puts a slow heartbeat under a close-up of someone looking toward an empty doorway. In a second cut, only the heartbeat changes. The shot contains no new action, but a viewer might now read the glance as alarm rather than expectancy. That shift could be the point of an edit. It would make a poor test of what the actor was actually feeling.

Choose the job before choosing the sensor

A cue for a listener. R. Michael Winters, Bruce Walker, and Grace Leslie paired photographs of eyes with different heartbeat sounds. Listeners sometimes changed their emotion judgments after hearing the heartbeat. The timing of the sound became material for interpreting the picture; it was experimentally varied, not a measurement of the pictured person’s current feelings.

A description of a conversation. In K-EmoCon, Cheul Young Park and colleagues collected recordings of paired debates and kept emotional annotations from participants, partners, and outside observers separate. The same stretch of conversation could be rated from three positions. A model’s score is interpretable only once the study specifies which position supplies its target. See also what can be read from a model.

A control for a performer. Marco Donnarumma and colleagues turned electrical muscle activity and mechanical vibration into sound. In a small trial, four of five novices learned to isolate the sound linked to mechanical vibration with at least one mapping after instruction; none isolated the electrical signal in that practice phase. Once the body becomes part of the instrument, the artistic question is whether a person could produce one audible change while leaving another alone. A more accurate sensor would not settle it.

The editor wants to influence how a scene is read. The dataset distinguishes the perspectives from which a scene is judged. The instrument gives a performer a new part to play. A waveform can serve all three purposes, but the evidence of success has to come from the listener’s interpretation, the named judgment, or the performer’s deliberate control, respectively.
