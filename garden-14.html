What the water queue leaves out

In a study of four Rohingya refugee camps in Cox’s Bazar, many of the drinking-water sources observed were within about five minutes’ walk. Residents at some locations still described lengthy queues: shared taps had short supply windows, so many people needed to arrive at the same time (Alam et al., 2020). A later survey across nineteen camps asked a different question. Nearly all surveyed households reported an improved source, but only an estimated 44% reported that water from their usual sources had been continuously available in the previous week (Butt et al., 2025, Table 1). Distance made one part of the journey visible. The queue made another visible. Neither answered a question that often matters before someone leaves home: will there be water when they get there?

A public-service improvement ordinarily comes with a count: minutes saved, clients seen, taps fixed. The measure becomes more useful once its denominator is clear. Fifteen minutes spent in line by the people who collected water means something different from fifteen minutes per household that tried to collect it. The second group includes people who waited and left empty-handed. Both counts still leave out residents who found a different source or decided not to make the trip. Those distinctions become observable when a study follows the households the system is meant to serve.

What arrives at the tap, and what arrives at home

A scheduled flow of water is not evenly useful to every user. In Hubli–Dharwad, India, some households had overhead tanks and their own connections. Others shared taps and kept water in portable containers. Kumpel et al. (2017, §§2.3, 3.2) had meter or billing data for only 212 of 1,898 households they could classify by access type. The team measured containers in a subset and estimated water used directly from the tap while it flowed. The storage-based estimate and the metered estimate were statistically similar for groups with overhead tanks. A container inventory on its own underestimated usage for households without overhead tanks that moved water-intensive tasks such as washing clothes into the supply window. The inventory could tell a researcher something about a household’s capacity to bridge an interruption and still miss something about how the household got its work done.

Different parts of the journey can yield different results for quality as well. Kumpel and Nelson (2013) took samples over a year from service reservoirs, household taps, and drinking water offered by households in continuously and intermittently supplied areas. Reservoir water quality was comparable across the networks. At the taps, 0.7% of samples from the continuous-supply area and 31.7% from the intermittent area tested positive for E. coli. Contamination in households’ stored drinking water narrowed that contrast. Testing water at the tap located a substantial benefit of the upgrade; sampling what people drank showed how much of the difference remained by that point.

The same reorganization had another consequence. In some places the utility removed public standpipes and closed public sources. Residents could spend less time waiting under continuous supply, yet residents formerly reliant on free public sources could lose their backup. Ray et al. (2018, pp. 44–45) report that some households with private borewells kept using them for nondrinking tasks. There was no equivalent private option for everyone who had used the public ones. Counting how often people still visited alternative sources would register a decline; distinguishing voluntary and forced switches changes what that decline means.

When does a repair become an open tap?

When a tube well failed in Camp 2 in Cox’s Bazar, residents described telling NGO workers and going to neighboring wells. Repairs often took an hour or two; a delay could mean a day or two. People in Camp 4 Extension described switching between tube wells and solar-powered taps as availability changed with season and weather (Akhter et al., 2020, §3.1.3). A fault therefore changes two operating conditions at once. One collection point stops supplying water, and another receives some of the arrivals it would not otherwise have seen. A queue at the second point may become longer even if the people operating it have changed nothing.

A seven-month study of rural handpumps in Rwanda gave the term “repaired” an observable definition (Nagel et al., 2015, Table 2). After an observed failure, the researchers timed the first repair attempt and then the point at which the pump had been returned to function for at least seven days. With periodic maintenance visits, the estimated medians were about 40 days to a first attempt and 57 days to a repair meeting the study’s criterion. With sensor-informed dispatch of a dedicated crew, the estimated median to a successful repair was about 21 days, compared with about 152 for on-request service. The dispatch arrangement provided information, staff, tools and transport together. For residents, it was a difference in the service process, not merely a different way of detecting a fault.

Those repair timelines stop short of the trip somebody has to make. A resident deciding whether to leave wants to know when the local point is open, whether that status has been checked, and which alternatives are currently usable. A status update read before the trip can prevent wasted travel. One received after the resident has stood in line may do nothing but explain why the trip failed.

Measure the route people can actually use

A study can begin with the collection point closing and follow the consequences rather than ending the record at the closure notice. When was the fault first noticed? How was it reported? Did the first attempt restore flow? When did residents actually learn that a point could serve them? Track the destination when someone switches sources, and include the additional arrivals in the record for that destination. Then check the households that did not appear in either line. Some may already have a full tank; others may have lost access and used less than they needed.

Waiting time is still worth measuring. It becomes a stronger result when accompanied by the number of households served, what they brought home, the cost of the extra trips, and which alternatives they had if the usual point failed. A shorter queue is a property of the people visibly standing in line. Better service is a claim about the larger group the water system exists to reach.
