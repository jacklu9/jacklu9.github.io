Now make the proposed change to the phosphorus piece: let and add NA = 9 × 1015 cm−3 acceptors. The net concentration is only ∆N = 1015 cm−3. This is a useful stopping point: we can calculate a population using an equilibrium relation, but we have not yet explained why that relation works, or how many states the electrons have available. The band curves must now become a counting tool.

1.1.3 The density of states and the Fermi–Dirac distribution function

The same band curve can contain different populations

Draw the conduction and valence bands for the undoped silicon reference and for the boron piece. In our dilute-doping model, their host curves are nearly unchanged. Yet the reference has n = p = 1010 cm−3, whereas the boron piece has approximately p = 1016 and n = 104 cm−3 under the assumptions just used. Looking at the shapes of the two host curves has not told us those numbers.

To discover what is missing, try counting a narrow energy interval before attempting the whole band. Suppose an illustrative interval of width 0.010 eV offers 2×1017 states per cm3, and suppose five per cent of those states are occupied. It contains 1016 electrons per cm3. The arithmetic required two pieces of information: available places and occupied fraction. These bin values are a separate counting exercise; the populations of our silicon pieces will require their actual state counts and equilibrium filling.

We will describe the occupied fraction at electron energy E by a probability f(E). For the illustrative bin it is 0.05. To describe availability without committing to one particular bin width, divide the available-state concentration by the width. This gives 2 × 1019 eV−1cm−3. Call this quantity g(E), the density of states, or DOS: states per real-space volume per energy. Decide what counts as one available state A drawn point on a band curve is not automatically one complete state. The dispersion E(k) assigns an energy to a wavevector k, but a one-electron quantum state also needs its band and spin labels. Opposite wavevectors may have the same energy and still label different states. In the spin-degenerate model, each wavevector has two independent spin states. The Pauli principle permits at most one electron in each complete state, so two electrons at one k occupy the two different spin states.

Each box is one complete one-electron state: band, wavevector and spin specified. A filled dot means one electron; an empty box still counts toward the DOS. Pauli exclusion allows at most one electron per box. A probability such as f = 0.05 describes an ensemble average, not 0.05 of

an electron inside one box.

The drawing separates availability from occupancy. g(E) counts all complete states, including empty ones, and carries units; f(E) is a probability between zero and one. We next count the available boxes in each energy interval, then fill them and add their contributions. No filling probability can create carriers where no states exist.

Find the states hidden behind the smooth curve

Imagine increasing the physical size of one of our samples without changing its material, temperature or doping concentrations. It should contain more available states and more carriers, although the drawn bulk band curve can remain the same. The missing size dependence is in the spacing of its allowed wavevectors.

To make that spacing explicit, model a large piece as a cube of side length L and real-space volume V = L3. The three components kx, ky, kz of its wavevector measure spatial phase variation and have units of inverse length, usually m−1. They do not locate an electron inside the cube. We will count their allowed combinations and only afterwards sort them by energy.

For bulk counting, join opposite faces mathematically by imposing periodic boundary conditions: translating the wavefunction by L in any coordinate returns the same value. This is a counting convention that suppresses surface complications. For its phase factor exp(ikxx), the requirement

Periodic boundaries avoid surface complications when counting bulk states. Hard-wall standingwave boundaries lead to a different-looking grid and a different region of allowed labels; a consistent bulk count has the same leading DOS. We use one boundary convention throughout: spacing 2π/L, all signs of each wavevector component, and the full sphere. Mixing the spacing from one convention with the counting region from another introduces erroneous factors.

Other valleys or bands must be counted separately; their multiplicity cannot be hidden in the spin factor.

Which grid points fall into an energy interval?

We can count points in a region now, but our original shaded strip was an energy interval. To connect the two, look near a single isotropic parabolic band minimum and select all states below a chosen energy. Points the same distance from the minimum have the same energy, so the selected points fill a sphere in reciprocal space.

For our silicon calculation, retain the spherical geometry without importing the metal’s filling. We can count available points below any trial energy whether they are occupied or empty. Calling a boundary a Fermi surface adds a statement about filling in the metal example. In a semiconductor the equilibrium Fermi level can lie in the gap, where no extended band state exists; no occupied conduction-band sphere follows from that placement. We will give the finite-temperature filling meaning of EF after finishing the state count.

Return to the enlarged sample that motivated the grid. The sphere for a fixed energy is unchanged, but the grid gets denser as L grows. Doubling the real-space volume therefore doubles its state count. Dividing that count by volume will give the same DOS per volume, as a bulk material property should.
