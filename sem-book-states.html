1.1.3
The density of states and the Fermi–Dirac distribution function
The same band curve can contain different populations
Draw the conduction and valence bands for the undoped silicon reference and for the boron piece.
In our dilute-doping model, their host curves are nearly unchanged. Yet the reference has n =
p = 1010 cm−3, whereas the boron piece has approximately p = 1016 and n = 104 cm−3 under
the assumptions just used. Looking at the shapes of the two host curves has not told us those
numbers.
To discover what is missing, try counting a narrow energy interval before attempting the whole
band. Suppose an illustrative interval of width 0.010 eV offers 2×1017 states per cm3, and suppose
five per cent of those states are occupied. It contains 1016 electrons per cm3. The arithmetic
required two pieces of information: available places and occupied fraction. These bin values are
a separate counting exercise; the populations of our silicon pieces will require their actual state
counts and equilibrium filling.
We will describe the occupied fraction at electron energy E by a probability f(E).
For the
illustrative bin it is 0.05. To describe availability without committing to one particular bin width,
divide the available-state concentration by the width. This gives 2 × 1019 eV−1cm−3. Call this
quantity g(E), the density of states, or DOS: states per real-space volume per energy. The
narrow shaded strip below shows why its area, rather than its height alone, supplies a number of
available states per volume.

Decide what counts as one available state
A drawn point on a band curve is not auto-
matically one complete state. The dispersion E(k) assigns an energy to a wavevector k, but a
one-electron quantum state also needs its band and spin labels. Opposite wavevectors may have
the same energy and still label different states. In the spin-degenerate model, each wavevector has
two independent spin states. The Pauli principle permits at most one electron in each complete
state, so two electrons at one k occupy the two different spin states.

Find the states hidden behind the smooth curve
Imagine increasing the physical size of one of our samples without changing its material, tempera-
ture or doping concentrations. It should contain more available states and more carriers, although
the drawn bulk band curve can remain the same. The missing size dependence is in the spacing
of its allowed wavevectors.
To make that spacing explicit, model a large piece as a cube of side length L and real-space volume
V = L3. The three components kx, ky, kz of its wavevector measure spatial phase variation and
have units of inverse length, usually m−1. They do not locate an electron inside the cube. We will
count their allowed combinations and only afterwards sort them by energy.

Periodic boundaries avoid surface complications when counting bulk states. Hard-wall standing-

bulk count has the same leading DOS. We use one boundary convention throughout: spacing

convention with the counting region from another introduces erroneous factors.

For our silicon calculation, retain the spherical geometry without importing the metal’s filling. We
can count available points below any trial energy whether they are occupied or empty. Calling a

meaning of EF after finishing the state count.
Return to the enlarged sample that motivated the grid. The sphere for a fixed energy is unchanged,
but the grid gets denser as L grows. Doubling the real-space volume therefore doubles its state
count. Dividing that count by volume will give the same DOS per volume, as a bulk material
property should.
