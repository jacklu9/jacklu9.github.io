Back at the bench
The plates are still separated. We have not ﬁlled the gap with an unseen wire. What has changed
is what we can say about the space between them.
The meter and the probe answer diﬀerent questions. The meter records free charge crossing a
section of wire per unit time. The probe measures a ﬁeld at its own position. During charging,
charge accumulates on the plates and the ﬁeld in the gap changes. Once an ideal capacitor reaches
a steady voltage, its conduction current can vanish while its electric ﬁeld remains. A nonzero
electric ﬁeld does not require a local ﬂow of free charge.
That distinction also explains why a charge-free part of the gap can contain a ﬁeld. Gauss’s law
says that its net outward displacement ﬂux is zero; it does not say that every ﬁeld arrow there is
zero. A ﬁeld can enter the region through one side and leave through another. The closed surface
measures their balance. Its shape alone does not determine the ﬁeld at each point: we still need
the sources, material properties, and geometry.
The apparent break in the magnetic account is resolved by the changing ﬁeld. One surface span-
ning a loop cuts the wire; another passes through the gap. During charging, conduction current
contributes on the ﬁrst, while changing electric displacement contributes on the second. Ampère–
Maxwell’s law gives the same circulation for that one loop. Charge has not jumped across the
insulator. The ﬁeld-change term belongs in the magnetic law alongside the transport of charge.
When the drive repeats sinusoidally, phasors let us keep the amplitude and phase at every point
without rewriting the common oscillation each time. Restoring the time factor and taking the real
part gives the real ﬁeld again. A rotating arrow on a complex plane describes that calculation; it
does not by itself describe a rotating physical ﬁeld. Likewise, the direction in which a phase pattern
advances and the direction in which a local electric ﬁeld points are separate pieces of information.
The two-layer example brings the questions together. With no initial interface charge, permit-
tivities determine the initial ﬁeld division. If the initial ﬁeld division gives unequal conduction
currents in the two layers, charge accumulates at their interface. Gauss’s jump condition then re-
quires the ﬁelds to adjust. In the ﬁnal steady state the conduction currents match, even though the
ﬁeld strengths usually do not. Throughout the transient, the sum of conduction and displacement
current is continuous. Each earlier deﬁnition now has a job in one connected explanation.
We can now read a ﬁeld picture more carefully: distinguish a value at a point from variation
around it, a ﬂux balance from a circulation, and a spatial direction from a time trace. The next
question is what forms those ﬁelds can take as they propagate through space. That will require
solving Maxwell’s equations with suitable material and boundary data. The wave chapters can
begin from here, with the geometry and the meaning of the symbols already in place.

a region, or a limiting value. It does not automatically mean a derivative.

The assumptions behind a simple material equation
Linear: doubling the input ﬁeld doubles the material response. Isotropic: no spatial direc-
tion has a diﬀerent response. Homogeneous: material properties do not vary with position.
Time-independent: those properties do not change with time. Instantaneous: the re-
sponse has no memory in the time-domain model. These are separate statements. They must
not be inferred merely because a formula is written compactly.

Questions that test the picture
These checks concern the meaning of the equations, rather than speed of calculation. Try to picture
the stated region, loop, or measurement before reading each explanation.
A uniform ﬁeld crosses a closed box. Does zero net ﬂux mean nothing crosses?
No.
The ﬁeld enters one side and leaves the other. Each face can have a nonzero contribution, but
their signed sum is zero. Divergence measures the imbalance, not the total amount crossing in
either direction.
Field lines bend. Must the curl be nonzero?
No. Curl compares circulation around an
inﬁnitesimal loop with its area. The ﬁeld strength on neighbouring paths matters as well as the
direction. Circular ﬁeld lines with magnitude proportional to 1/r have zero curl away from their
singular axis, whereas rigid rotation has nonzero curl.
A Gaussian sphere encloses a complicated source. Is the ﬁeld constant on it?
The
choice of surface does not change the physical ﬁeld. Constant magnitude on that sphere requires
symmetry of the entire source and boundary conﬁguration. Gauss’s law ﬁxes the total ﬂux even
when the local values are unknown.
A capacitor gap carries no conduction current.
Why can it still aﬀect magnetic
circulation?
Charging changes D in the gap. Ampère–Maxwell’s law contains both J and ∂tD.
They ensure that two surfaces sharing one loop give the same circulation even when one cuts a
wire and another passes between the plates.
A complex phasor rotates after the time factor is restored. Is the physical electric
ﬁeld rotating?
That rotation occurs in the auxiliary complex plane. A real ﬁeld with only
one spatial component oscillates along one physical line. To determine physical vector rotation,
reconstruct all spatial components and compare their time dependence.
Two materials have continuous tangential electric ﬁelds.
Are the full ﬁelds equal?
Only their two tangential components must agree under the ordinary interface assumptions. Their
normal components depend on permittivity and free surface charge.
A vector contains more
information than one of its projections.
Conduction currents diﬀer on opposite sides of an interface. Is charge conservation
violated?
The diﬀerence can accumulate as surface charge or be redistributed by a sheet current.
The correct balance includes ∂tρs and ∇s · Js.
In the two-layer transient, conduction-current
imbalance is exactly balanced by the displacement-current diﬀerence.
What completes a ﬁeld problem?
Maxwell’s equations constrain ﬁelds, but a particular
physical solution also needs its sources, material relations, geometry, and appropriate initial and
boundary data. A formula is useful only when these assumptions, its units, and the meaning of
its components remain visible.
