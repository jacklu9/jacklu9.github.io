The choice a rewrite should leave open

A poem can please its writer and still misdescribe the event that prompted it. Nicholes (2025) asked 88 university writers to compose a poem about a meaningful, difficult experience and to prompt a text generator to write about the same event. Writers judged their own poems closer to their experiences and felt more ownership over them. They gave the generated poems higher ratings for imagery, language, and form; half preferred the generated version. To call one poem better would leave out the disagreement between the writer as a witness to an event and the writer as a reader of poetry. Both judgments belong to the same person.

Writing tools often collapse that disagreement into a choice between keeping and replacing a passage. A sentence that reads more easily may be a welcome invention, an acceptable change in register, or a substitution the writer would reject if the difference were made apparent. A useful test of this kind of help asks not only which version survives but which decision the writer had a chance to make.

The first line sets a direction

In the poetry experiment by McGuire et al. (2024), some participants received a complete eight-line poem to edit. Others chose thematic words, wrote the first line, and alternated lines with the same generation system. A third group wrote without assistance. Ten poets rated the outputs. The alternating-line group averaged 14.70 on the 40-point creativity scale, above the 12.53 of the complete-poem editing group; the comparison with the unaided group, which averaged 15.74, was inconclusive. The redesign gave the writer several opportunities to set a direction: the theme, the opening, and what to add between the model’s contributions. The experiment changed those opportunities together, so it does not establish that alternating lines by itself produced the difference.

A more familiar design problem shows why a first suggestion can be so persistent. Jansson and Smith (1991) gave engineering students a brief for a spill-proof cup and told them not to use a straw. Some also saw a faulty example containing a straw. In the example condition, straws appeared in 17 percent of submitted designs; in the control condition the figure was 1 percent. The example supplied a possible form for the whole cup, with the forbidden part already in it. A poet editing a complete draft may have a similar difficulty finding which piece to disturb. A suggestion can provide something valuable to push against, but having a choice between two complete versions is not the same as being able to reconsider one assumption they share.

There is no corresponding argument that first drafts must be left entirely to the writer. A person often wants to hear what an unfinished thought could sound like. The distinction is what happens when the suggestion has a consequence the writer would rather undo. Can they fall back on the sentence they had already chosen? Can they ask for two alternatives that preserve an image while changing its rhythm? Is the first example still visible after a new one arrives? These are concrete affordances, not a requirement that every assisted writer work more slowly.

When everybody hears the same suggestion

The question looks different when the unit being examined is a collection of finished texts. Padmakumar and He (2024) collected short argumentative essays from people writing alone or with suggestions from one of two language models. The essays written with InstructGPT were more similar to one another than the independent essays on the study’s measures, including a comparison of the argumentative points extracted from the text. The same statistically significant difference did not appear with the base GPT-3 model. Similarity among the contributions supplied by the tool accounted for an important part of what happened. That is evidence about what became common in a set of essays, not evidence that the writers themselves all adopted the same point of view.

Agarwal et al. (2025) found a related pattern for culturally grounded English writing. The experiment produced 472 short essays from 118 writers in India and the United States. Without autocomplete, a classifier distinguished the two groups’ essays with 90.6 percent average accuracy. With suggestions it did so at 83.5 percent. One unassisted account of biryani, for example, named accompanying pickles and chutneys; an assisted account emphasized taste and richness in broader terms. These were different writers, not a pair of revisions tracking exactly which words an interface removed. A classifier can detect whether the collections have become more alike. It cannot tell whether a particular author meant to keep the name of a side dish for a particular reader.

Both things can be worth caring about. A writing tool for a publication can offer each contributor an effective opening yet leave editors with less variety across the whole issue. Conversely, when a writer changes how they explain a dish to someone who has never had it, the result may look less typical of the group the classifier was trained to recognize and communicate the writer’s purpose more clearly. Diversity across writers and freedom to vary one’s own writing are different properties.

Help without imitation

The problem is especially clear when a tool recognizes a register and starts to perform it on the writer’s behalf. In Basoah et al.’s (2025) study, 13 Black American adults with experience using writing tools drafted a short text as if for a close friend and examined the suggestions their writing tools made. Participants described autocorrection changing expressions they had intended to use, including expressions in African American Vernacular English. Yet some valued the same kinds of tools in professional writing. When a model was asked to continue the story in their tone and vernacular, some judged the attempt unconvincing or stereotyped. The two responses are consistent. A person can want a tool to stop treating a phrase as a mistake without wanting the tool to write the next sentence in that register.

This is a reason to test an editing tool with two genuine audiences. A writer might tell the same story once to a friend who knows the people in it and again to readers who need more context. Before revising, the writer could identify an image or relationship both readers should understand. Afterwards, a reader from each audience could say what they understood, and the writer could point out changes they wanted rather than ones they merely accepted. That comparison gives the writer room to judge that the most familiar phrasing is wrong for one reader, or that a strange-looking phrase is exactly the right one for another.

The difficult judgment is not always whether a generated text sounds human, personal, or polished. The generated poem can sound better and be further from the event. The suggested line can change how the writer understands what they want to say. The writer is entitled to keep either result. An editing tool deserves credit when it lets the writer discover the difference while there is still time to choose what goes on the page.
