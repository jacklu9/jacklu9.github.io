Semiconductor Physics · Chapter 1

Reading a Semiconductor Band Diagram

Find the lowest electronic excitation, then ask what a photon can reach without borrowing momentum from the lattice.

A semiconductor can have an electronic gap of 1.20 eV and a lowest vertical separation of 1.35 eV. Both numbers can be read from the same bands. They answer different questions. The smaller number compares the most favorable initial and final electronic states. The larger one asks for the smallest separation when both states have the same crystal wavevector. An optical transition must account for momentum as well as energy, so knowing which comparison was made matters.

Read a state, not a route

A dispersion diagram puts electron energy on the vertical axis and crystal wavevector kk along the horizontal axis. Picking a curve and a point on it picks an allowed electronic state (leaving spin labels implicit). Reading to the left or right does not follow an electron through the sample. A graph of band-edge energy versus physical position in a junction uses a different horizontal variable. On that graph an electron can move along the device; on this graph a change in horizontal position represents a different wavevector.

In a three-dimensional crystal, k\mathbf{k} has three components. A printed band plot usually connects selected paths between special points. Γ\Gamma denotes the zone center, k=0\mathbf{k}=0; labels such as X and L denote other symmetry points for particular crystals. A turn in the plotted path changes the direction in reciprocal space. The plotted line is a selection of states, not proof that no extremum lies off it. A claim about the absolute minimum of a real material requires a search of the relevant zone.

The bands show which states are available; occupations tell whether those states contain electrons. An ideal intrinsic semiconductor at zero temperature has filled valence states and empty conduction states. Promoting an electron can leave a vacancy in the valence band. That missing electron can be tracked as a positively charged carrier, a hole. The excitation creates a pair, not a net charge. For occupation at higher temperatures, continue to counting states and filling a band.

Take the two energy differences

Let Ec(k)E_c(k) be the lowest conduction energy at a given kk and Ev(k)E_v(k) the highest valence energy there. Minimize the first and maximize the second independently to get the fundamental gap. To find the lowest vertical gap, subtract first at each kk, then minimize the result.

Eg=min⁡kEc(k)−max⁡kEv(k),Evert=min⁡k[Ec(k)−Ev(k)].E_g=\min_k E_c(k)-\max_k E_v(k),\qquad E_{\mathrm{vert}}=\min_k\big[E_c(k)-E_v(k)\big].

For every kk, Ec(k)E_c(k) is at least its global minimum and Ev(k)E_v(k) is at most its global maximum. Subtracting gives Ec(k)−Ev(k)≥EgE_c(k)-E_v(k)\ge E_g. Equality is possible when some kk serves as both extrema: the fundamental gap is direct. If no kk achieves both extrema, it is indirect. Equality is a statement about energy and wavevector, not a guarantee about transition strength. A photon-only transition can still be weak or forbidden by optical selection rules.

Move the conduction minimum away from the valence maximum. Look first at the difference between the heights of the two extrema, then at the shortest line you could draw vertically between the curves. There is no electron trajectory in either line; they are two comparisons among states.

Separate the energy axis from its zero

A point marked 0 eV is a reference, not a claim about the occupation of that point. If the reference lies between two states, one plotted height can be negative and the other positive. A valence state at −0.33-0.33 eV and a conduction state at +0.92+0.92 eV at the same kk are separated by 0.92−(−0.33)=1.250.92-(-0.33)=1.25 eV. Subtracting the two unsigned distances would miss the part of the interval below the origin. The Fermi level, which helps determine occupations, is another quantity. It may be selected as zero by convention, but choosing zero cannot establish where the Fermi level lies relative to the bands.

Worked example: the shortest vertical line is between the two extrema

Use a one-dimensional model near the relevant extrema with dimensionless coordinate x=ka/πx=ka/\pi, where aa is the lattice period. Take the two plotted branches to be the ones that set the gaps, with energies given in eV. These are illustrative parabolas, not a fit to a named material or a periodic dispersion over the whole zone.

Ec(x)=1.20+0.80(x−0.75)2,Ev(x)=−0.40x2.E_c(x)=1.20+0.80(x-0.75)^2,\qquad E_v(x)=-0.40x^2.

The conduction minimum is 1.20 eV at x=0.75x=0.75, and the valence maximum is 0 at x=0x=0. Thus Eg=1.20 eVE_g=1.20\,\mathrm{eV}. At x=0x=0, the vertical separation is 1.65 eV. At x=0.75x=0.75, it is 1.425 eV. Neither is the answer to the vertical-minimum question: complete the square on the difference.

Ec(x)−Ev(x)=1.35+1.20(x−0.50)2(eV).E_c(x)-E_v(x)=1.35+1.20(x-0.50)^2\quad\text{(eV)}.

The minimum vertical gap is 1.35 eV at x=0.50x=0.50, strictly between the extrema. Adding 3 eV to every energy leaves both gaps unchanged. At the minimum vertical gap, hc/Ehc/E is about 919 nm, using hc≈1240 eV nmhc\approx1240\,\mathrm{eV\,nm}. That is the corresponding vacuum wavelength, not an absorption coefficient or a prediction of the brightness of an emitter.

Why a photon usually goes almost straight up on this plot

A photon delivers energy EγE_\gamma and wavevector qγq_\gamma. In vacuum qγ=Eγ/(ℏc)q_\gamma=E_\gamma/(\hbar c), so a 1.35 eV photon has qγ≈0.0068 nm−1q_\gamma\approx0.0068\,\mathrm{nm}^{-1}. With a=0.50a=0.50 nm the separation between the model’s extrema is 0.75π/a≈4.7 nm−10.75\pi/a\approx4.7\,\mathrm{nm}^{-1}. A refractive index of a few changes the photon wavevector by a factor of a few, not the hundreds needed to bridge that separation. For light at those energies, the photon-only approximation is therefore nearly vertical in the electronic diagram.

In an indirect transition, a phonon (a quantum of lattice vibration) can supply or take away the additional crystal momentum, with momentum conserved modulo a reciprocal-lattice vector. The phonon also exchanges energy. If it is absorbed, the photon supplies the electronic energy difference minus the phonon energy; if it is emitted, the photon supplies the difference plus the phonon energy. The phonon must have the right wavevector too. The absorption onset therefore does not follow from converting EgE_g to a wavelength alone. In a clean crystal near the gap, requiring this additional exchange generally makes indirect absorption weaker than a comparable allowed direct transition.

An optical spectrum brings together availability of states, their occupation, the energy and momentum balance, and the transition strength. A band plot provides the first two energy comparisons. The following lesson explains what wavevector labels and why the lattice treats some labels as equivalent.

Check your understanding

The conduction and valence extrema occur at the same nonzero kk. Is the fundamental gap direct?

Yes. Direct means the same crystal wavevector, not necessarily Γ\Gamma. Optical strength remains a separate question.

In the worked example, make the valence band flat at 0 eV. What changes?

The fundamental gap stays 1.20 eV. Now the conduction minimum at x=0.75x=0.75 also has an eligible valence maximum, so the minimum vertical separation drops to 1.20 eV.

A transition spans 1.20 eV and absorbs a phonon of 0.04 eV that supplies the required momentum. What photon energy obeys energy conservation?

1.20−0.04=1.16 eV1.20-0.04=1.16\,\mathrm{eV}. For emission of a phonon of the same energy the photon would instead need 1.24 eV, with the momentum balance checked separately.
